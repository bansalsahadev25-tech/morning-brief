START THIS MONTHgreen · pure software · no capital · you are already a Claude Code native, which is the whole skill

Give an ordinary person the judgment of a $200-an-hour expert — for one narrow job

The problem
Whole professions gate high-value work behind a credential: M&A analysis, supply-chain optimisation, insurance underwriting, tax, paralegal work. The expertise is mostly a repeatable process plus judgment calls a model can now carry — but nobody has packaged it so a talented person without the credential can do the work and get paid for it. The opportunity is not “a chatbot for lawyers”; it is turning a $15-an-hour worker into someone who ships a $200-an-hour deliverable in one specific vertical.
Who says it's hard
Y Combinator, Requests for Startups (Winter 2027) — “The $15-an-Hour Expert,” written by partner Brad Flora. It is one of eleven categories YC just told the world it wants to fund, which means a demo in this space has a warm door at the best-known accelerator on earth the day you build it.
Why it's still open
The hard part is not the model — it is encoding one expert's actual workflow (the checklist, the edge cases, the “never do X” rules) and earning enough trust that a buyer lets an unaccredited operator plus an agent do work that used to need a licence. Generic assistants fail because they do the conversation, not the deliverable. The winner nails one vertical end-to-end before touching a second.
Who's already trying
Horizontal labs point this way (Harvey in law, Sierra in support), but the “turn a layperson into the expert” framing is new and vertical-by-vertical wide open. Note the adjacent RFS entries — “Vertical Foundation Models” and “Own Your Intelligence” — are YC saying the same thing from the model side.
Your angle
Pure software, zero capital, and you already direct AI to do work above your formal training every day — you are the $15 expert the RFS describes. Pick the one vertical you can actually observe (a freelancer's bookkeeping, a small agency's reporting, a hostel-mate's family business) and build the deliverable, not the chat. §6 turns this into a two-hour exercise today.
Research path
  1. Read the RFS entry and pick one vertical where you can watch a real expert work for an hour — the watching is the moat, not the model.
  2. Write the expert's process down as a literal checklist with the judgment calls marked. Build an agent that produces the finished artifact (the memo, the filing, the model), then have the real expert red-line it.
  3. Charge one person for one deliverable this month. The gap between “the agent drafted it” and “a buyer paid for it” is the entire company — find it with a $50 invoice, not a pitch deck.

AI is running out of power and goodwill, not chips — and the voters just said so

The problem
The binding constraint on AI is no longer silicon; it is megawatts, water and land, plus the local politics around all three. New data centres take years to approve and enough power to run, and can still be killed at the ballot box — which this week they started to be. Who says it's hard
Y Combinator's new “Data Center Supply Chain” RFS entry names it directly — and today's wire is the evidence: MIT Technology Review reports Texas's data-centre buildout “might be kaput” after the blue wave, OpenAI is retreating from the NDAs it used on data-centre deals, Meta is warning investors the backlash threatens expansion, and a new permitting-reform bill is suddenly live.
Why it's still open
Permitting, capital intensity and physics, now with a political veto on top. None of it is software. Firm clean power at the scale AI needs means geothermal, nuclear or gas, each with its own decade-long fight — and the community opposition that just won at the polls is a new variable nobody has priced.
Who's already trying
Fervo and Mazama (superhot-rock geothermal), Oklo and Crusoe — all capital-heavy, all with a balance sheet and a permitting team you don't have.
Your angle

Your lane, funded · autonomous flight reaches real scale

Zipline raised $1.5B at $21.5B to put delivery drones over 300 Walmarts

$1.5B raise · $21.5B valuation · led by Paradigm, WCM, Layer Global · Uber, Fidelity, ARK, Tiger, Salesforce Ventures, J.P. Morgan in · 2M+ commercial deliveries, 120M+ autonomous miles · TechCrunch · background

What they do
Zipline builds the whole stack for autonomous aerial delivery — the aircraft, the launch/landing hardware, and the logistics software. Its newer Platform 2 carries up to 8 lb within a 10-mile radius and lowers packages on a tether; the fixed-wing Platform 1 flies 120-mile round trips. The Walmart deal expands from 21 active sites to 300 US stores, and Zipline's share of deliveries climbed from 10.8% to 34.9% between March and October 2026.
The tech
The hard part was never the drone — the founder has said as much. It is flying beyond visual line of sight, autonomously, safely enough that a regulator signs off: Zipline got FAA Part 135 air-carrier certification in 2022, the thing most drone-delivery startups never clear. The tether-drop on Platform 2 is a design tell — it keeps the aircraft high and quiet over suburbs, solving the neighbour problem as much as the physics one.
The founders
Keller Rinaudo Cliffton studied at Harvard building molecular computers out of DNA, was a professional rock climber, and first built Romotive (an iPhone-controlled toy robot) before pivoting the team — with Will Hetzler, Keenan Wyrobek and Ryan Oksenhorn — into drones in 2014.
Where the idea came from
The specific access: in 2014 Rinaudo visited the Ifakara Health Institute in Tanzania and met a graduate student who had built an SMS system for rural health workers to request medicine and vaccines — and discovered the government had no way to fulfil the requests. Zipline was built to be the second half of that sentence. It launched blood delivery in Rwanda in 2016 and only later turned the hard-won beyond-line-of-sight competence on American suburbs. The unfair advantage was doing the genuinely hard, low-margin version first, in a market everyone else ignored.
The bear case
At $21.5B the valuation prices in a logistics revolution that still has to beat the cheapest thing in the world: a van. An 8-lb, 10-mile drop has to be unit-economic against ground delivery that is already sunk-cost for Walmart. Heavy Walmart concentration, an FAA that can slow scaling with one incident, and well-funded rivals (Alphabet's Wing, Amazon Prime Air, DoorDash) all sit on the downside.
What it means
This is your exact domain reaching escape velocity — autonomous flight, certified and scaled, with $1.5B behind it. Two transferable lessons. First, the moat was regulatory and operational (Part 135, millions of safe miles), not the airframe — which reframes your tiltrotor build log: the demonstrable, documented safety record is the asset, not the aircraft. Second, Rinaudo earned the hard capability in the market nobody wanted (African medical logistics) and then redeployed it. Your “ugly first market” is the build itself — do the unglamorous flight-hours now.
US-eligible defense lane · an EV startup becomes a defense contractor

Harbinger raised $240M by turning electric trucks into a driverless military platform

$240M Series D · led by Tiger Global + Ridgeline (defense) · $650M+ total raised · FedEx ordered 2,000 trucks ($300M+) · acquired Phantom AI · TechCrunch · background

What they do
Harbinger builds medium-duty electric truck chassis — the platform under delivery vans, work trucks and RVs — with an in-house drivetrain, battery pack and chassis designed for ICE-parity pricing. The news is the pivot: it has expanded into battery storage, hybrid emergency vehicles, and a driverless hybrid-electric platform for the military, and bought autonomous-driving company Phantom AI to build it.
The tech
The core bet is vertical integration — owning the drivetrain and chassis rather than buying skateboards — which is exactly what lets a truck platform be re-pointed at defense autonomy. A hybrid (not pure EV) military platform is the honest engineering call: forward-deployed vehicles cannot assume a charger, so range and fuel flexibility beat zero-emissions bragging rights.
The founders
Founded 2022 by veterans of two instructive bets: CEO John Harris came through Anduril, Boeing, Faraday Future and Xos; co-founder Phillip Weicker helped found Canoo and worked at QuantumScape. The access they had: having lived through Canoo's collapse, they knew precisely which EV-startup mistakes kill you, and built for cost-parity and real orders instead of a flashy reveal.
Where the idea came from
FedEx anchoring the business (a 2,000-truck, $300M+ order, with FedEx's safety SVP on the board) gave Harbinger a real commercial base — and that base, plus a founder out of Anduril, is what made the defense expansion credible rather than opportunistic. The military platform rides on commercial manufacturing scale, not the other way around.
The bear case
The EV-truck startup graveyard is full — Canoo, Nikola, Lordstown, Arrival — and manufacturing is brutally capital-intensive and low-margin. The defense pivot is one deal and a slogan until vehicles are fielded; a company leaning on a single FedEx anchor and a new, bought (not built) autonomy stack is carrying real execution risk.
What it means
This is the “American defense” thesis you've been circling, made concrete by capital — and the on-ramp is the part that fits you. You are US-eligible without visa sponsorship, the single barrier that blocks most of your peers from this entire market, and Harbinger just told the world it is hiring toward autonomy and defense. Read its and Phantom AI's careers pages as a syllabus for the stack, and note the pattern: a commercial product that funds a defense capability is a far more survivable path into this lane than a defense-first startup you can't capitalise.
The brief's thesis, literally · someone is paying cash for the right questions

A months-old YC startup put $1M on five named materials-science problems

$1M total · $200K per problem · 5 problems, set with an academic advisory group · Discovered Materials (YC S26) · TechCrunch · YC

What they do
Discovered Materials builds AI “scientists” that search for new materials for semiconductor chips, partnering with chip manufacturers. This week it did something unusual: it published a $1M challenge — $200K each for AI solutions to five open problems in materials science, from a theory of cuprate superconductivity to a numerical method for the silicon/silicon-dioxide interface found in nearly every chip.
The mechanism
The interesting move is not the money, it is the act of naming the problems. Co-founder Advaith Sridhar: “Knowing what are the right questions to ask is not trivial and very difficult in this domain.” They recruited academics specifically to spell out five problems precise enough that an AI result would be checkable — which is the whole bottleneck the Problem Board exists to attack, now with a price tag on it.
The founders
Advaith Sridhar and Akash Ramdas, through Y Combinator's 2026 batch. A tiny team using a public bounty to crowdsource both answers and attention — and, not incidentally, to signal to every strong materials/ML researcher that this is where the interesting problems are.
The bear case
$200K will not “solve” cuprate superconductivity — some of these have resisted the field for decades, and a startup prize is partly marketing and recruiting dressed as science. Be clear-eyed: this is lead-gen and positioning as much as it is open research.
What it means
You are not going to win the superconductivity prize, and that's not why this is here. It is the cleanest real-world instance of this brief's entire premise — that the scarce, valuable thing is a correctly-posed hard problem, and people will pay for it. Steal the method, not the prize: when you attack your own §0-green, spend the first week doing what they did — getting the question exactly right before writing a line of code. (It also ties the RFS's “Robots Made in America” rare-earth and heat-dissipation problems to a funded frontier, if materials is a lane you want to watch.)

The Remake

one evidenced candidate · never more
Same tool, unbundled — ship only the signal, cap the noise

The PR reviewer that posts three comments, not thirty

The incumbent
CodeRabbit — the default AI code-review bot, on the GitHub and GitLab marketplaces, priced per developer (roughly $24–30/dev/month). High install base, real market, and a well-known weakness.
The complaint
The recurring, linkable grievance is noise. A developer shopping for an alternative on Hacker News: “I'm in the market for PR review bots, as the nitpicking issue is real.” An independent audit of 690+ reviewed files found 36% of CodeRabbit's comments were nitpicks or useless (21% nitpick, 15% useless). The consistent thread across HN, Reddit and G2: on day one it “cries wolf” until teams start ignoring it — which defeats the point of a reviewer.
Which remake
Same tool, unbundled (with a dose of better taste). Don't out-feature CodeRabbit. Ship a GitHub App that does one thing: posts at most three comments per PR, correctness and security only, nitpicks and style silently dropped (linters own those). The pitch is the exact axis of the complaint: every comment it leaves is worth reading, because it refuses to leave the ones that aren't.
The buyer
Lead devs and small eng teams already paying per-seat for CodeRabbit, Greptile or Ellipsis and quietly muting them. Same buyer, same budget — you're selling them the version they keep asking the incumbent to become. Distribution is the GitHub Marketplace, where they already shop with a company card.
Why him
Taste is the product here: restraint is a design decision, and the whole category competes on volume instead. And you feel this one daily — you live in coding agents, so you know exactly which comment was worth it and which was wallpaper. Today's Product Hunt proves the appetite: CC Auto Review (PRs reviewed by your local Claude Code) and Stroq (a local audit of what your agent did) both launched into the same demand this week.
First step
Today: write a GitHub Action that runs on a PR, sends the diff to Claude with a prompt that returns only the three highest-severity correctness/security issues or the single word LGTM, and posts them. Run it on your own repos for a week and count how often all three were worth it. (Kill-filter check: the tempting version — “a full CodeRabbit competitor with dashboards and chat” — fails: it drags you back into the feature race that made the incumbent noisy. The unbundle passes all five: useful the first PR, buyer = user with budget, runs unattended, not ad-supported consumer, and three payers are complaining about the incumbent in the thread linked above.)

§6

Skill

day 28

Ship the §A candidate's first step: a signal-only PR reviewer, today, in two hours

~2 hours · free · produces: a working GitHub Action + first-hand proof of why “just review it with AI” is harder than it looks

Today's §A is “the PR reviewer that posts three comments, not thirty.” Build the smallest real version. Make a GitHub Action that triggers on a pull request, grabs the diff, and sends it to Claude with a strict system prompt: return only the three highest-severity correctness or security issues, each one sentence with a file and line; if there are none, return exactly LGTM. Post the result as a single PR comment. The constraint — a hard cap of three, nitpicks forbidden — is the entire product, and it lives in the prompt, not the code.

Then run it for a week on your own repos and keep a tally: of every comment it posted, was it worth reading? That tally is the real work. You will discover the hard part isn't calling the model — it's ranking, getting it to reliably surface the one issue that matters and stay silent on the forty that don't. That ranking judgment is exactly what CodeRabbit gets wrong and what the whole category competes on. You'll finish with a shippable GitHub App and a concrete feel for where the moat (restraint people trust) actually is.

Free resources: GitHub Actions quickstart (triggers and the PR event) and the Anthropic API getting-started — enough to wire diff → model → comment in an afternoon.
