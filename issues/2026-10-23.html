START THIS MONTHgreen · solo · no capital · no credentials

Nobody can prove what an AI agent actually touched

The problem
Agents run with far more authority than their task needs — the same shell, filesystem and credentials whether they are summarising a doc or changing your infrastructure. When one goes somewhere it shouldn't, there is no audit trail to answer the only question that matters afterwards: what did it touch, under whose authority, and when. Sandboxing (keeping the agent in a box) shipped. Provenance (a receipt for everything it did) did not.
Who says it's hard
The wire said it three ways in one morning. 1Password shipped a way for agents to log in on your behalf — handing them your authority by design. NVIDIA open-sourced OpenShell, “a safe, private runtime for fleets of autonomous AI agents” (338 pts on HN) — which boxes them but still does not log what each one did. And Anthropic donated the Agent Skills standard to a new foundation, with 70+ platforms signed on: the ecosystem is standardising how agents act faster than how anyone audits them.
Why it's still open
Brand new, and lopsided. The money went to the box, not the receipt — NVIDIA and Cisco sandbox, neither keeps a per-action provenance log a human can read later. The surveyed research is mostly 2024–26 preprints; it is a field with no incumbent and no standard yet.
Who's already trying
NVIDIA OpenShell and Cisco on sandboxing; a thin layer of arXiv work on the audit question (today's “Reliable AI-Assisted Decisions via Selective Auditing” and “Can an Agent Debug a Web Corpus?” are both this-week signals). Nobody owns “the black-box flight-recorder for your agent.”
Your angle
Zero capital, zero credentials, and you run agents daily on a machine holding your keys — you can feel this today. §6 turns it into a concrete thing you can run on your own setup tonight; the log you produce is the prototype.
Research path
  1. Instrument your own Claude Code setup: log every tool call — command, file, credential, timestamp — for one week. The log is the product's first screen.
  2. Read “Reliable AI-Assisted Decisions via Selective Auditing” for how researchers decide which agent actions are worth a human's attention — the hard part isn't logging, it's ranking.
  3. Clone OpenShell and ask the one question it doesn't answer: after a run, can you reconstruct what each agent touched and why? That gap is the whole opening.
REAL IN 6–12 MONTHSamber — needs real users, not a hostel room

There will not be enough people to care for the old

The problem
By 2030 one in five Americans is over 65, with millions of unfilled caregiving jobs and tens of millions of family members already doing it unpaid. Almost no technology is built for older people — even Alexa and Google Home frustrate most seniors — and the buyer (the adult child) is not the user (the parent), a split that breaks normal product intuition.
Who says it's hard
Y Combinator's RFS names it outright (“AI for the Aging Population”). And note where this morning's medical AI actually aimed: Microsoft's Mustafa Suleyman chasing “medical superintelligence” on hard diagnostic cases, Moderna/Merck's mRNA cancer vaccine cutting recurrence 42% — frontier health AI chases the diagnosis, not the daily care of the person who already has the diagnosis. That's the gap.
Why it's still open
Consumer tech optimises for people who adapt fast; the people who need this adapt slowest. And you have to satisfy two humans at once — the parent who uses it and the child who pays — which is why the usual “delight the user” playbook misfires.
Who's already trying
Papa, Honor, ElliQ — mostly US, mostly expensive, mostly built around a companion device rather than the coordination a family actually drowns in.
Your angle
India's aging curve is steeper and its caregiving market is almost entirely informal — you can observe it directly where US founders cannot. Amber, not green: this needs real users and real trust, not a weekend. But it is a problem you can start interviewing your way into this month, long before you build.
Research path
  1. Interview five families in your own extended network about who cares for the oldest member, and write down the exact moment it breaks — a missed medicine, a fall, a discharge nobody understood.
  2. Read ElliQ's published retention data: the honest numbers on whether seniors keep using these things past month one.
  3. Look at what an Indian hospital discharge actually hands a family. It is usually one confusing sheet of paper — the cheapest possible wedge is making that one artifact legible.

New on the feed today: nothing to add to the board. DARPA, IFP and Marginal Revolution all moved, but produced award topics and essays, not a new funded problem with a door attached. When YC's RFS changes or a grade here gets solved, that becomes a headline of its own.

Defense × biotech · the frontier money went physical

Warhound raised $200M to clone and gene-edit war dogs — with the CIA's fund in the round

$200M raised · $1.2B valuation · launched from stealth · led by In-Q-Tel, Sequoia, 8VC · Dallas / SF · TechCrunch · Axios

What they do
Breed better military and law-enforcement working dogs by taking cell lines from top Belgian Malinois and German Shepherds, editing genes to keep the useful traits and strip the health defects (hip dysplasia, poor eyesight, cancer risk), then cloning the result through partner Viagen. Anduril bolts on hardware — sensor vests, protective goggles — “on top of the dog, not in the dog.”
The tech
Two proven stacks pointed at a new target: Colossal's germline gene-editing and cloning pipeline (the one it uses on dire wolves and mammoths) plus Anduril's sensor-and-autonomy kit. The hard part isn't the clone, it's selection — knowing which genes make a dog that passes training, when today over 80% of US military dogs are imported from Europe and fewer than half of candidates make it through. That's a data-and-genetics problem before it's a dog problem.
The founders
Ben Lamm (Colossal Biosciences CEO, who co-founded it with Harvard geneticist George Church) and Palmer Luckey (Anduril co-founder, a partner here with no equity). CEO is David Spirk, the former first Chief Data Officer of the US Department of Defense. The unfair advantage is almost comic: one founder already owns the world's leading cloning lab, the other owns the defense-distribution channel.
Where it came from
Lamm and Luckey met at a conference where Luckey floated the idea; the research that turned it into a company was the supply-chain fact — America's working-dog pipeline is foreign, shrinking, and genetically unreliable. First cloned, gene-edited dogs are expected born early 2027, in service within two years.
The bear case
The ethics and optics of gene-editing and cloning animals for war invite regulation and protest, and “better working dogs” is a small, slow procurement market that moves at government speed. A $1.2B valuation on zero dogs delivered is a bet on the founders' logos, not revenue. No numbers on cost per dog, yield, or a signed military contract were disclosed.
What it means
Read the shape, not the dogs. This is a company built entirely out of two founders' existing unfair access — a cloning lab and a defense channel — aimed at a boring, specific, unglamorous supply-chain failure. That is the most transferable pattern in the brief, and it sits one domain from your own lane: In-Q-Tel (the CIA's venture fund) writing checks into young, hardware-light defense ideas is the signal, and you are US-eligible, which is the barrier that blocks most of your peers from the entire market.
Money · the top of the market · a dealmaking tell

OpenAI is raising $30B at $1.25T — a number it cut by $150B in three weeks

Pre-IPO round · up to $30B · $1.25T valuation · led by UAE investors & Coatue · bridge to a 2027 IPO · TechCrunch · CNBC

What happened
OpenAI is finalising up to $30B at a $1.25T pre-money valuation — which would make it the most valuable private company ever — led by investors from the UAE and Coatue, and framed explicitly as a bridge to an IPO now expected in 2027, not this year. It wants signatures within a week.
The tell
$1.25T is $150B below the $1.4T OpenAI pitched BlackRock and MGX only weeks earlier (Techmeme). A company cutting its own number mid-raise, in a market this hot, is the single most honest data point you'll read this week. The UAE money may also come bundled with a deal for more compute capacity — capital and chips moving as one.
The pattern underneath
It rhymes with two other things on today's wire. Sifted: “Nvidia funds European startups. They spend it on Nvidia” — capital that returns to its source. And a one-year-old lab, Flapping Airplanes, raised $1B at $6B with no product. Money is concentrating into a handful of AI names on reputation, not results.
The bear case
Crunchbase calls the AI IPO wave “the dot-com bubble's evil twin”: Anthropic's own delay, a NYC pension fund asking it to wait, Altman calling an OpenAI IPO “ill-advised.” Anthropic lost a reported $42B in 2025 on $16.8B of quarterly revenue. Trillion-dollar private marks with negative economics is exactly the setup that ended badly in 2000.
What it means
Two things you can act on. First: valuation is not validation — when the richest buyer in the market cuts its own price, treat every “AI” mark you read as a story, not a fact. Second, and more useful: if the top is frothy, the durable value is one layer down, in the companies doing actual engineering against a real constraint (see Helion, below). Build toward the constraint, not the narrative.
Energy · the subsurface/power wedge · design-process, literally

Helion is fabricating its own power chips — because a 5% loss halves a fusion plant

$225M ($175M CHIPS Act + $50M private) · total raised $3.2B+ · 2nd best-funded fusion startup · Everett, WA · TechCrunch

What they do
Helion is a fusion company that just became, in part, a semiconductor company. It won a $175M CHIPS Act award (plus $50M private) to build a US production line for high-power semiconductors — the switches that route electricity — because today's devices lose 5–10% of the power flowing through them, and for Helion specifically that loss is enormous.
The tech — and why the chips matter so much
Most reactors boil water to spin a turbine. Helion uses direct energy conversion: it slams plasmas together at 100M°C and captures the electricity straight off the reaction with magnets and capacitors. There is no turbine to average out losses — so the efficiency of the power electronics is the plant's net output. CEO David Kirtley: “If we can cut that loss in half, that's actually twice the power output of the fusion system.” A 5% part became a company-defining variable.
The founders
David Kirtley (CEO, plasma physicist) has run Helion since 2013; it is the second best-funded fusion startup in the world ($3.2B+ raised), with a contract to supply Microsoft electricity. R&D runs with GlobalFoundries in Vermont (~$80M) and a new line in Washington State (~$100M).
Where it came from
The insight is the whole lesson: because their reactor topology makes semiconductor loss a first-order term, they couldn't wait for the chip industry to get around to it — they had to go build the part themselves. That is the design-process instinct you read for, at the scale of a power plant.
The bear case
Kirtley openly admits this R&D won't reach Orion, the first plant due to power Microsoft in 2028 — federal contracts take months, so the chips land in later plants. Fusion timelines famously slip; a plant that hasn't produced net commercial power yet announcing a chip program is a bet stacked on a bet.
What it means
This is the energy/subsurface wedge earning its place with real money and a live company, as promised. Two openings for you: the new WA line makes a class of power semiconductor used in “fusion, manufacturing, wastewater, medicine, and defense” — power electronics is an under-glamorous, under-staffed field adjacent to your interests. And the mental model — find the 5% part that your system's physics makes a 100% problem — is exactly how to think about your own tiltrotor's weak link.

The Remake

one evidenced candidate · never more
Same tool, unbundled — a flight-recorder for Stripe sellers

The one thing Stripe sellers beg for — “why is my money held?” — unbundled into its own app

The incumbent
Stripe's own dashboard. It processes the payment flawlessly and then goes opaque at the exact moment a seller panics: a payout is delayed, a balance is held, an account is under review, and the dashboard says almost nothing about why. Stripe has a Stripe App Marketplace — the distribution surface where its millions of sellers already shop with a card out.
The complaint
A real, dated thread from this week's r/startups, listing the pains that “keep coming up over and over”: “customer support is non existent when things go wrong … stripe has held funds/payouts with no explanation … has banned my account with no explanation.” A seller enumerating, in their own words, the moment the tool goes dark. The whole thread is people shopping to leave over it.
Which remake
Same tool, unbundled. Do not build a Stripe alternative — see the kill-filter note. Build the one missing screen: an app that connects over Stripe's own API (OAuth, read-only) and surfaces, in plain English, exactly what the dashboard hides — payout schedule and why the next one is delayed, held balance, open disputes, Radar reviews, and the account requirements that trigger holds — with an alert the moment any of them moves. A flight-recorder for your money.
The buyer
Indie sellers, course/PDF sellers, small SaaS — anyone with revenue flowing through Stripe who has felt a payout freeze. They pay $0 today and eat the anxiety, or churn to a worse processor. $9–29/mo for “never be surprised by a held payout again” is an easy yes, and the buyer is the user.
Why him
Your taste edge is the entire product: Stripe already has the data and shows it badly; your job is to turn “balance: pending” into one legible sentence a stressed founder understands at a glance. That's the same “make the opaque thing legible” instinct behind every item you upvote — and it ships where buyers already are, so distribution, the hard part, is half-solved by the marketplace.
First step
Today: spin up a Stripe test account, hit the payouts, balance, disputes and Radar reviews endpoints, and render a single page that says in plain English what's happening to the money and why. If it explains one hold more clearly than Stripe does, you have the wedge. (Kill-filter check: the obvious idea — “a cheaper Stripe” — fails: payments means PCI, regulation, and trust/network effects on day one. The unbundled monitor passes all five: useful day one, buyer = user with budget, runs unattended, not ad-supported consumer, and you can name three payers from that thread alone.)

Skill

day 23

Put a flight-recorder on your own AI agent — log every tool call for a week

~1–2 hours to set up · free · produces: a provenance log that is literally the first screen of the §0-green product

Today's §0-green is “nobody can prove what an agent touched,” and the wire made it urgent: 1Password now hands agents your logins, NVIDIA shipped a runtime for fleets of them, and Anthropic turned Agent Skills into a standard. Everyone is racing to let agents act. Almost nobody is building the receipt. So build yourself the smallest possible one. Wrap your Claude Code setup (or any agent you run) so that every tool call it makes — the command, the file it read or wrote, the credential it used, the timestamp — gets appended to a plain log. A shell wrapper, a hook, or a tiny proxy is enough; you are not building a product yet, you are building a diary.

Run it for a week of normal work, then read the log back cold. Two things will jump out: how much authority the agent exercised that its task never needed, and how impossible it is to answer “what did it do on Tuesday?” from raw lines. That second problem — ranking which actions a human should actually look at — is the real product, and it's exactly what this week's “Reliable AI-Assisted Decisions via Selective Auditing” is about. You'll finish the week standing inside the problem instead of reading about it.

Free resources: NVIDIA's OpenShell (read how it sandboxes, note what it doesn't record), the “Can an Agent Debug a Web Corpus?” paper for the agentic-auditing framing, and the Agent Skills standard now that 70+ platforms share it — a provenance format that works across all of them is a standard-shaped opportunity.
