START THIS MONTHgreen · pure software · no capital · you already are the $15 expert the RFS describes

Turn an ordinary worker into a $200-an-hour expert in one narrow vertical — and today a front-page thread was people asking which AI skills actually pay

The problem
Whole professions gate high-value work behind a credential — M&A analysis, supply-chain planning, underwriting, tax, paralegal and bookkeeping work. The expertise is mostly a repeatable process plus a handful of judgment calls a model can now carry. Nobody has packaged it so a talented person without the credential can produce the finished deliverable and get paid. The opportunity is not “a chatbot for lawyers” — it is making a $15-an-hour operator ship a $200-an-hour artifact in one specific vertical.
Who says it's hard
Y Combinator's RFS named it (“The $15-an-Hour Expert,” by partner Brad Flora), and this morning the demand showed up raw: HN's front page carried “Ask HN: What are the most useful, concrete AI skills to independently learn?” — a thread full of people trying to convert the model into income and not knowing where the leverage is. The supply-side version ran on YourStory too: AI letting individuals, not just IT giants, sell expert services. The want is loud; the packaging is missing.
Why it's still open
The hard part is not the model. It is encoding one expert's actual workflow — the checklist, the edge cases, the never-do-X rules — and earning enough trust that a buyer lets an unaccredited operator plus an agent do work that used to need a licence. Generic assistants do the conversation, not the deliverable. The winner nails one vertical end-to-end before touching a second.
Who's already trying
Horizontal labs point this way — Harvey (law), Sierra (support) — but the “turn a layperson into the expert” framing is new and vertical-by-vertical wide open. YC's adjacent Winter-2027 entries (“Vertical Foundation Models,” “Own Your Intelligence”) say the same thing from the model side.
Your angle
Pure software, zero capital, and you already direct AI to do work above your formal training every day — you are the $15 expert the RFS describes. Pick the one vertical you can actually watch a human do for an hour (a freelancer's bookkeeping, a small agency's reporting, a family business's compliance) and build the deliverable, not the chat. The watching is the moat, not the model.
Research path
  1. Read the RFS entry and pick ONE vertical where you can sit next to a real expert for an hour. The hour of watching is worth more than the model.
  2. Write that expert's process as a literal checklist with the judgment calls marked; build an agent that outputs the finished artifact, then have the expert red-line it.
  3. Charge one person for one deliverable this month. The gap between “the agent drafted it” and “a buyer paid for it” is the whole company — find it with a $50 invoice, not a deck.
FILED, NOT STARTEDred — frontier cryptography, standards bodies, a decade of coordination; learn the vocabulary, do not start here

AI-accelerated math is coming for the encryption the internet runs on — and this week the “is this an intelligence explosion?” talk got loud enough to notice

The problem
The public-key cryptography protecting banking, messaging and identity rests on a bet that certain math problems stay hard to solve. AI systems have begun making genuine mathematical progress, and researchers are openly asking whether models shorten the path to breaking today's cryptography. The deeper problem underneath: almost no system in production can swap its cryptographic primitives quickly (“crypto-agility”), so a break would not be a patch — it would be a multi-year migration nobody has rehearsed.
Who says it's hard
Vitalik Buterin (“take the risks to cryptography from AI-accelerated math seriously”); Scott Aaronson (“The Mathocalypse”); set against OpenAI's and Terence Tao's claims of real AI mathematical progress. This week it got a fresh frame: Marginal Revolution asked plainly, “Are we witnessing an intelligence explosion?”, the same morning an OpenAI board member warned an AI “takeover” could be months away. Take both at a discount and the cryptography question still stands.
Why it's still open
It needs frontier cryptography, standards bodies and coordinated global migration at once. Post-quantum standards exist on paper; getting the world to adopt crypto-agility before a break rather than after is a decade-long coordination problem, not a product.
Who's already trying
NIST (post-quantum standards), Cloudflare, Google, Apple, PQShield. All large, all slow, all working the standards layer — which is exactly why this is filed red.
Your angle
Red, and honestly so — a standards-and-PhD problem, not a weekend build. File it to recognise one narrow, boring slice if it appears: tooling that inventories which cryptographic primitives a codebase actually depends on is the unglamorous first step of any migration, and that part is just software. Learn the vocabulary so the day this stops being theoretical, you can read the room; do not start here.
Research path
  1. Read Scott Aaronson's “The Mathocalypse” for the honest state of the threat, then the MR piece above for the sober counterweight.
  2. Skim NIST's post-quantum cryptography standards to see what “the answer” already looks like on paper.
  3. Notice the cheap adjacent slice: crypto-agility tooling — “what am I even using, and where” — is software even when the cryptography is not.

The funding map just redrew · the biggest check-writer in AI left the building

Jensen Huang was the AI ecosystem's single largest venture investor — and his exit blows a hole in how early-stage AI gets funded at all

Jensen Huang resigned as Nvidia CEO 12 Nov 2026 · CFO Colette Kress is interim CEO · Nvidia's venture arm NVentures went from 1 deal in 2022 to 30 in 2025 · Nvidia participated in ~105 AI-startup rounds last year, often as the largest investor · backed CoreWeave ($2B), Mistral (3×), Crusoe, Reflection AI ($800M), agreed to acquire Hugging Face ($13B, closing 2027) · TechCrunch · Interconnects · TechCrunch (Asia)

What happened
Huang resigned (covered as a leadership story yesterday); the part that changes your map is what he was doing with Nvidia's balance sheet. Beyond selling chips, Nvidia had quietly become the most active investor in AI — ~105 rounds in a year, writing cheques big enough that infrastructure startups needing $100–500M in GPUs could raise half that from Nvidia alone at billion-dollar valuations. That engine is now without its driver.
Why it matters — the mechanism
Interim CEO Kress is expected to be “much more conservative,” one investor told TechCrunch: “I don't think she's going to commit the same amount of money to keep the early-stage ecosystem going.” Pull one large, price-insensitive buyer out of a market and two things happen: cheque sizes shrink and valuations soften, especially for capital-heavy infra. The second-order hit lands on US open-source models — Nathan Lambert argues Huang's money was quietly underwriting the open ecosystem, and its withdrawal tilts the field further toward the closed labs.
The bear case (on the panic)
Nvidia is one investor, not the market; traditional VCs, sovereign funds and the hyperscalers still have more capital than sense right now. Kress may trim the circular deals (the ones where Nvidia funded its own customers — yesterday's §1) without touching healthy investing. “Smaller checks and lower valuations” is also what a sane market looks like, not a collapse. Treat the froth-deflation as plausibly healthy, not only scary.
What it means
You are not raising infra money, so the direct hit misses you — but the climate is the point. Lower valuations and tighter checks make the next year a buyer's market for talent and attention: the AI startups you'd intern at or build alongside will hire more carefully and value cheap, proven builders over credentialed-but-expensive ones. That is your exact profile. And the open-model squeeze is a reason to lean into the “own your intelligence” local-model thread — the free building blocks (§3) matter more if the funded ones get scarcer. Read the funding chill as a door, not a wall.
The bill for building fast · the security debt of vibe-coding, itemised

Researchers scanned 160,000 AI-built apps and found passports, payments and 200,000 private chats open to anyone with a browser

Crissy Field (German security firm) scanned 159,041 apps built with Lovable, published Dec 2025–Sep 2026 · 34,545 used Supabase · exposed with no login: passport numbers, expiry dates, nationalities, payment details, contracts, invoices, and 200,000+ chat messages · root cause: misconfigured Supabase row-level security · 706 points on HN · Crissy Field (primary) · Sifted · Cybernews

What they found
Lovable lets non-technical people spin up full-stack apps from plain-language prompts. The default backend is Supabase, whose security model (row-level security) has to be configured correctly or the database is simply readable from the browser. Researchers pulled 159,041 live Lovable projects from Common Crawl and found tens of thousands wide open — identity documents, payment data and hundreds of thousands of private messages retrievable by anyone who looked, no exploit required.
The tech — what's actually hard
Nothing here is a clever hack; that's the point. The hard part is that the person who built the app doesn't know the question exists. “Did you write a row-level-security policy so strangers can't read your users' table?” is invisible to someone who prompted an app into being on a Tuesday. The model generated working software and skipped the security review a human engineer does reflexively. The population that can now ship did not inherit the instincts that used to come with shipping.
Where this sits
This is the board's filed vibe-code-qa problem walking onto the front page with receipts: millions of apps written by people who cannot audit them. Snyk and Semgrep exist, but they're built for professional engineers, not for the Lovable user who doesn't know they have a database, let alone an open one.
The bear case
Lovable and the platforms will race to make secure-by-default the only default — ship RLS templates, scan on deploy, refuse to publish an open table — and if they succeed, the window for a third-party fixer narrows fast. The buyer problem is also real: the person who most needs an audit is the least equipped to know they need one, so you're selling a vitamin to someone who doesn't feel sick until the breach.
What it means
This is a §0-green you could feel today: take the three things you've built with AI and check whether your backend is readable from a browser (§6 is exactly this). The buildable openings are the ones Snyk ignores: a one-click “is my app leaking?” scan for the no-code crowd, a Lovable/Supabase-specific linter, a “deploy-time” check that blocks an open table before it ships. The market is 34,000 exposed apps and growing — you don't have to convince anyone the problem is real; researchers just did.
Deep tech · the real bottleneck was never chips · a founder with unfair access

An ex-Navy pilot raised $20m to boil ammonia with the ocean — because he knew two things nobody else had in the same head: carriers, and compute

Drogue · $20M raised · founded 2025 (~6 months old) · founder & CEO Sam Sipe, former US Navy E-2D Hawkeye pilot and former Scale AI engineering lead · investors incl. XYZ Venture Capital, Toyota Ventures, Boost VC, Slow Ventures · multi-megawatt prototype planned for early 2028 · “approval in principle” from the American Bureau of Shipping · TechCrunch · Dealroom

What they do
Drogue is building floating data centres that make their own power from the ocean. Ocean-thermal energy conversion (OTEC): warm surface water cools the servers and boils ammonia to drive a turbine; cold water pumped from ~1,000m down (≈4°C) condenses it back. One loop does both jobs — cooling the compute and generating the electricity — which is the elegant part. A prototype goes to sea aboard a conventional ship in early 2028.
The tech — and why it's hard
OTEC has been theoretically sound since the 1880s and has never been commercial, because the surface-to-deep temperature gap is small, which makes the thermodynamic efficiency marginal and confines the economics to tropical water where the gap stays above ~20°C year-round. Add the ocean's standing insults — storms, corrosion, biofouling — and offshore data centres remain unproven at any scale. The bet is that AI's power hunger is finally desperate enough to make a century-old marginal technology pencil out.
The founder — the unfair access
Sam Sipe flew E-2D Hawkeyes off US Navy carriers, then led engineering at Scale AI. That is the whole story: he carries both halves of this problem in one head. From the Navy he has a working respect for the ocean as a power source most people only see as hazard (“this majestic place and resource that we just ignore”); from Scale he watched AI compute demand outrun every conventional supply timeline. His argument is about time: the data-centre industry builds on 8–10-year cycles, “completely incompatible” with the AI surge. Unusual access to a problem is the most transferable thing in this whole brief — here it's two careers almost nobody combines.
The bear case
A century of OTEC never penciling out is a strong prior, and “unproven at scale” is doing heavy lifting — a six-month-old company with a 2028 prototype is a bet, not a business. Geography caps it to the tropics. And the competition arrived first with more money: Panthalassa raised $140M in May for wave-powered ocean data centres. The honest read: this is a real option on a desperate market, priced like one.
What it means
This is §0's compute-power-siting red made into a company, and the recurring energy/subsurface thread's best evidence yet — the bottleneck on AI is power and physics, and your field of study sits next to it. But the transferable lesson is the founder: he didn't have the deepest ocean-thermal physics or the best AI chops — he had the only combination of the two that was looking at this problem. Your version of that is the tiltrotor plus whatever you study: the edge is the intersection nobody else is standing on, not depth in either column.

The Remake

one evidenced candidate · no idea without a linked complaint

A bot-free, on-device meeting recorder for lawyers and therapists — because the incumbents are being sued for secretly joining calls, and privilege can't live in someone else's cloud

The incumbent
Otter.ai and Fireflies — the dominant AI meeting notetakers, sold at roughly $17–30/user/month, both built on a bot that joins your call as a participant, streams the audio to their cloud for transcription, and (per the suits) has trained on it.
The complaint
Not a review — a lawsuit, which is a complaint with evidence. Otter faces four consolidated federal actions alleging its notetaker “joins calls as an autonomous participant… records even non-account holders… and retained recordings indefinitely to train its AI.” Fireflies faces proposed class actions under Illinois' biometric law. And the everyday grievance underneath: users report Otter “joined meetings unasked when linked to a calendar,” auto-attending every invite. For a lawyer that's a privilege breach; for a therapist, a HIPAA one.
Which remake
#1, same tool, one audience (generic notetaker → recorder built only for privilege-sensitive professionals), carried by #4, different price shape — no bot, no cloud, transcription runs on the user's own machine (local Whisper), one-time or flat price instead of a per-seat subscription that resents you. The whole product is the sentence “it never leaves your laptop.”
The buyer
Solo and small-practice lawyers, therapists and financial advisors — people with a legal duty of confidentiality and a real fear of the incumbent's lawsuit landing on them. They pay Otter/Fireflies now, or (more often) ban them and take notes by hand. “On-device, nothing uploaded, defensible in a bar complaint” is worth more to them than any feature.
Why him
This is a trust product, and trust is communicated through taste — a calm, legible interface that makes “your data never left this computer” obvious and provable is your exact edge, and the incumbents (functional, cloudy, now legally radioactive) can't easily follow without breaking their own business model. Proof the remake sells: bot-free recorders like Replay and Meetily are already shipping into exactly this complaint.
First step
Doable in a day: build a Mac menu-bar recorder that captures system audio on one real call and transcribes it locally with whisper.cpp — no bot, no upload. If the transcript is usable and you can say “watch the network tab, nothing left the machine,” you have the core demo and the one sentence that sells it. (Kill-filter check: useful on first install (value with zero other users); buyer = user with proven spend and a legal, dated reason to care; runs unattended. The honest miss vs the playbook: this is a standalone app, not a marketplace tool, so distribution is the weak point — the mitigation is that the audience is reachable through narrow professional channels (bar-association and therapy-practice newsletters) where “won't breach privilege” is the headline, not a feature. And watch the real kill: if Otter ships a credible “local-only” mode, the wedge closes — until then the lawsuit is doing your marketing.)

Skill

day 37

Check whether your own apps are leaking: point today's Lovable scan at the three things you've built with AI, and find out what a stranger can read

~2–3 hrs · free · produces: a yes/no answer on whether your own users' data is public, and the exact instinct the §0-green vibe-code-qa market is built on

Today's §1 is 34,000 AI-built apps with their databases open to anyone with a browser, because the people who built them never knew to ask the one question. So ask it of your own work. Take the three things you've shipped with Claude Code — anything with a backend — and try to read their data the way a stranger would: open the app, find the API or database calls in the network tab, and see what comes back when you hit them without logging in. If you used Supabase or Firebase, open the dashboard and check whether row-level security / rules are actually on, or whether the table is world-readable by default. The Crissy Field writeup shows you precisely what “open” looks like — it's not an exploit, it's a URL.

Two things fall out of this. One, you either confirm your own apps are safe (relief) or you find a live hole and close it (a crisis you caught yourself, which is the entire skill). Two — and this is the point — you now feel the §0-green problem from both sides: you've been the vulnerable builder, and you've done the audit nobody sells to that builder yet. Every step that was annoying or non-obvious is a feature of the product that would do this automatically for the Lovable crowd. You can't design that tool from the outside; you have to be scared by your own network tab first.

Free resource: Supabase's Row Level Security docs — the single page that explains the exact control 34,000 apps got wrong this week. Read it once and you'll never ship an open table, which already puts you ahead of a large slice of people shipping production software today.

  • The Areni-1 cave shoe (Atlas Obscura) — a single leather shoe, ~5,500 years old, found in an Armenian cave stuffed with grass and perfectly preserved: older than Stonehenge, older than the pyramids, and shaped startlingly like something you could wear today. The oldest known leather shoe on earth, and proof that the basic problem of “keep the foot warm and dry” was solved once and barely improved for millennia.
  • The Mother of the Sea (Sassuma Arnaa) (Atlas Obscura) — a submerged sculpture in Nuuk's harbour of the Greenlandic goddess who holds all the ocean's animals in her hair, and withholds them when humans misbehave, until a shaman swims down to comb it free. A whole theory of conservation and consequence encoded as a myth, sunk under the water it's about.
  • Norway's lice-zapping robots (Nautilus) — underwater drones patrol salmon farms and fire lasers at individual sea lice clinging to the fish, killing the parasite without touching the host. A genuinely strange image — a laser-armed robot doing delicate pest control on a living animal underwater — and a window into how industrial-scale food quietly turned into robotics.
  • Paul Simon: “I loathe that song” (MetaFilter) — the man who wrote “Feelin' Groovy” — one of the most beloved, feel-good songs of the century — has spent sixty years openly despising it. A small, sharp lesson in the gap between what a maker thinks of their work and what the world decides to love, which almost never match.
  • We beat screwworm once (Scientific American) — a flesh-eating parasite that lays eggs in open wounds was eradicated from North America decades ago by one of the strangest tricks in science: breed billions of sterile males and flood the wild so matings produce nothing. Now it's back, and the same century-old method is being re-run. A beautiful, grisly story about winning a war against biology with arithmetic.
  • A rare strike in Taiwan, under the AI rush (Rest of World) — workers at the heart of the global AI supply chain walked out, a genuinely unusual act in Taiwan, exposing the labour unrest humming beneath the record profits everyone upstream is celebrating. The human floor under the §1/§2 numbers — the people the funding rounds never mention.
  • Soldiers of Misfortune: the drawings of Urs Graf (Public Domain Review) — a 16th-century Swiss mercenary who was also a brilliant draughtsman, and drew the wars he personally fought in — brutal, swaggering, strangely funny sketches of soldiers and camp life by a man with a sword in one hand and a pen in the other. First-person war reporting, five centuries before the phrase existed, by one of history's least reliable narrators.
