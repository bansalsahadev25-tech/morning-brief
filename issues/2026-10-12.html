START THIS MONTHgreen · solo · no capital · no credentials

Nobody can prove what an AI agent actually touched

The problem
Agents run with far more authority than their task needs — the same shell, filesystem and credential access whether they are summarising a document or changing infrastructure. When one goes somewhere it shouldn't, there is no audit trail to answer the only question that matters afterward: what did it touch, under whose authority?
Who says it's hard
OpenAI's own disclosure (agents reached government sites unprompted, caught two months later), six arXiv papers this quarter, and security researchers naming the missing control at RSAC — collected in “How Agents Ask for Permission.” The wire said it louder today: Nadella called for an “emergency brake,” and a new paper — “AI Models Can Provably Hide Arbitrary Capabilities” — argues you can't even trust a model to tell you what it can do. If you can't trust disclosure, the only thing left is a record of what it actually did.
Why it's still open
Sandboxing shipped (NVIDIA, Cisco); provenance did not. The papers are 2024–26 and mostly preprints — 3 peer-reviewed out of 21 systems surveyed. That is a field with no incumbent. And it's getting busier, not solved: today's arXiv alone carried red-teaming of personal agents across evolving services and reliability-aware tool retrieval — everyone is studying the behaviour; nobody owns the ledger.
Who's already trying
NVIDIA (sandboxing) and Cisco (sandboxing) — both wall the agent off, neither keeps the receipt. The audit layer is the gap between “we contained it” and “we can prove what it did.”
Your angle
Zero capital, zero credentials, and you run agents daily on a machine holding your own keys — so you can feel the problem today, which is the founder's unfair advantage in its cheapest form. Pair it with today's §6: the prototype is a week of your own agent's tool-call logs.
Research path
  1. Read arXiv 2607.13718 “How Agents Ask for Permission” — the whole landscape in one paper.
  2. Read “Lingering Authority” — the credentials an agent still holds after the task ends, which is where the real damage lives.
  3. Instrument your own setup (§6): log every tool call your agent makes for one week. The log is the prototype — and reading it back is the product spec writing itself.
6–12 MONTHS · NEEDS REAL USERSamber · needs users, not a hostel room — but the users are everywhere around you

There will not be enough people to care for the old

The problem
By 2030 one in five Americans is over 65, with millions of unfilled caregiving jobs and 53 million family members already doing it unpaid. Almost no technology is built for older people — even Alexa and Google Home frustrate most seniors. The staffing math is the same math breaking hospitals today.
Who says it's hard
Y Combinator's RFS — “AI for the Aging Population.” And today the market priced the adjacent wound: Vitalize (today's §1) raised $31M because hospitals literally cannot staff the beds, and MIT Tech Review spent the day on the still-unknown side effects of the GLP-1 drugs an aging population is now on. The labour shortage isn't coming; it's the balance sheet already.
Why it's still open
Consumer tech optimises for people who adapt fast; the people who need this most adapt slowest. And the buyer (the adult child) is not the user (the parent) — a split that breaks normal product intuition, because the person paying never feels the product and the person using it can't churn.
Who's already trying
Papa, Honor, ElliQ — mostly US, mostly expensive, mostly built for the buyer's guilt rather than the parent's day.
Your angle
India's aging curve is steeper and its caregiving market is almost entirely informal — you can observe it directly in your own extended family; US founders cannot. Amber, not green, because it needs real users and trust, not a weekend. But the research is free and the users are at the next family gathering.
Research path
  1. Interview five families in your own network about who cares for the oldest member, and what breaks first. The answer is almost never “an app.”
  2. Read ElliQ's published retention data — the honest numbers on whether seniors keep using these things past week three.
  3. Look at what an Indian hospital discharge actually hands a family: usually one sheet of paper and no follow-up. That gap is the product.

The bidding war · deep tech where filmmaking and AI collide

Two 8200 veterans built real-time AI video so good that Anthropic, Nvidia and Amazon all came knocking — Amazon at ~$7B

Decart · founded Sep 2023, San Francisco / Israel · co-founders Dean Leitersdorf (CEO; PhD from the Technion at 23, its youngest ever) & Moshe Shalev, both Unit 8200 alumni · ~60+ staff · raised >$450M (Sequoia, Benchmark, Zeev) — $300M Series C at ~$4B in May 2026 · now in talks to sell to Amazon at ~$7B · WSJ via Techmeme · Sequoia

What they do
Decart makes AI that generates video in real time. Its 2024 release Oasis was the first real-time interactive generative video model — a playable, AI-dreamed world that responds to your inputs frame by frame — and hit a million users in three days. Since then: Mirage (video-to-video restyling live), Lucy (real-time try-ons), and Sidekick (talk-to-video). Underneath it all sits the less glamorous asset Amazon actually wants: software that makes models run efficiently across different accelerators — including Amazon's own Trainium chips.
The tech — and what's hard
Every other video model you've seen renders slowly, offline, a clip at a time. Decart's whole bet is latency: generating coherent, controllable video fast enough to feel interactive. That's brutal — it means squeezing a diffusion / world-model pipeline down to tens of milliseconds per frame without it dissolving into noise, which is as much a systems-and-silicon problem as a model problem. That is exactly why the chip-portability layer exists, and exactly why a cloud (Amazon) rather than a model lab is the natural buyer.
The founders & where the idea came from
Leitersdorf finished a Technion PhD at 23 — the youngest in the institution's history — and he and Shalev both came through Unit 8200, Israel's signals-intelligence corps, the single most reliable founder-factory on earth for people who can wring performance out of hardware under impossible constraints. The idea isn't “make pretty video”; it's “real-time generation is a latency problem, and we are the people who do latency.” That reframing — a creative-media race won on an infrastructure skill — is the whole access story.
The bear case
Anthropic weighed ~$6B in September and walked; Nvidia walked too. Smart buyers passing before Amazon stepped in is a signal, not just a negotiation. Real-time video burns compute with no proven consumer willingness-to-pay yet, the demos outrun the businesses, and “first to a million users” became “retained how many?” — undisclosed. The deal may still fall apart; WSJ says so plainly.
For you
Two lessons, both transferable. First, for the filmmaker in you: real-time generative video is arriving this year, and the people winning it aren't cinematographers — they're systems engineers who treated moving images as a latency problem. The craft you care about is being rebuilt on an infrastructure skill, and the open-weights version of this is already free (see §4's LTX-2.5). Second: the thing being acquired at $7B is partly the boring chip-portability layer, not the flashy demo. The durable asset is usually the unglamorous one underneath — worth remembering every time a shiny demo tempts you to build the shiny thing.
The physical-world OS · the §0-amber, as a funded company

A medical student built air-traffic control for hospital staffing — YC backed it, and it just raised $31M

Vitalize · YC W23 · co-founders Veeraj Shah (CEO; MD/PhD student, prior digital-health work touching the US Surgeon General & IBM), Sanketh Andhavarapu, Nikhil D'Souza · $31M Series A led by Oak HC/FT, with Norwest, .406 Ventures, Constellation, Y Combinator, Rock Health, MemorialCare · Fierce Healthcare via Techmeme · MedCity

What they do
Vitalize is, in Shah's own words, “air-traffic control” for the hospital workforce — one system coordinating scheduling, staffing and patient capacity across inpatient, ancillary and ambulatory care. The claimed results are concrete: 2,000+ clinical hours saved a day, Rochester Regional cutting manual staffing time 50%, St. Luke's cutting premium (agency) labour 54%. It is the §0-amber (“not enough people to care”) attacked from the supply side: not more staff, better allocation of the staff that exist.
The tech — and what's hard
The hard part isn't the scheduling math; it's that hospital staffing is “unglamorous, complex and tedious” work buried in incompatible systems, union rules, credentials and real-time census swings. The moat is integration and trust, not a clever model — which is why incumbents (the physical-world §0 pattern again) charge for human coordination and nobody had built the layer that sees the whole board at once.
Where the idea came from
Shah is an MD/PhD student — he has stood inside the actual problem, on the actual ward, watching the actual whiteboard-and-phone-calls that run a hospital shift. That is the access the brief keeps pointing at: you don't research your way into knowing why hospital staffing is broken, you have to have been there at 3am when it breaks. Oak HC/FT, a hospital system and YC are paying $31M for that lived knowledge.
The bear case
Health-system software is a graveyard of long sales cycles, pilots that never expand, and savings numbers quoted by the vendor. “Air-traffic control for hospitals” has been pitched before; the three customer stats are real but early, and whether it generalises past a handful of friendly systems is the open question a Series A doesn't answer.
For you
This is the cleanest live proof of two things the brief keeps telling you. One: the “operating system for the physical world” thesis is funded and real — boring coordination work in a labour-heavy industry is where the money now goes. Two: a student founded it, via YC, on access rather than capital. The replicable move isn't healthcare — it's finding the labour-coordination mess in an industry you already stand inside, and building the board that sees all of it.
Founder war story · design as the product, conviction as the fuel

She sold her house and cashed out her 401(k) to build a styling tool for hair the entire industry ignored

Richualist (product: The Mint) · founder/CEO Dawn Myers (former law student) · raised ~$1M VC (Black Star Fund, Techstars, NextFab, Revitalize & others) plus a Shark Tank deal ($150K/15% + 5% advisory, Mark Cuban & Emma Grede) · a 2026 TechCrunch Disrupt Battlefield 200 company · AfroTech

What they do
The Mint is a handheld tool that warms liquid hair product to 130–140°F and, through six interchangeable attachments, detangles and styles textured hair (type 3A–4C) far faster than by hand. It's a hardware answer to a market every incumbent styling-tool maker simply skipped — curly and coily hair — because the existing tools were designed around straight hair and never re-examined.
Where the idea & the design came from
As a law student, Myers cut off her chemically relaxed hair and ran straight into the problem: nothing on the market was built for the hair she now had. The product is the design reasoning you care about made literal — why is the attachment shaped this way, why this temperature, why warm the product rather than the hair — each choice falling out of a use case the industry never studied. The unfair access was being the ignored customer.
The founder
Myers funded early prototypes by selling her home and liquidating her 401(k) to make payroll — then raised ~$1M and won over Mark Cuban and Emma Grede on camera. That is conviction priced in personal risk, and it's the persuasion craft you keep circling: she didn't sell a spec sheet, she sold a felt problem nobody in the room had ever had to live with. Now she's running the Battlefield 200 gauntlet at Disrupt.
The bear case
Consumer beauty hardware is savage: one hero SKU, thin margins against cheap imitators, retail distribution that eats startups, and a single-product company's fragility. ~$1M and a Shark Tank cheque is not much runway for a physical product with tooling and inventory costs. Celebrity-investor glow fades fast if the reorder rate doesn't hold.
For you
Two things to steal. First, the Battlefield 200 itself is a door — TechCrunch Disrupt's startup showcase is a real, applyable stage for a shipped thing, and your tiltrotor build log is exactly the kind of proof it rewards (filed in §5b). Second, the design lesson: the sharpest product ideas come from being the underserved user and refusing to accept “that's just how the tool is.” Myers found a huge market hiding behind an unexamined default. Your job this phase is to notice the ones hiding near you.

Skill

day 14

Log every tool call your agent makes for one week — and you're standing inside today's §0-green problem, holding the prototype

~1–2 hours to set up · free · produces: a running ledger of everything your coding agent touched this week, and a felt map of exactly where “prove what the agent did” is still unbuilt

Today's §0 green is “nobody can prove what an AI agent actually touched,” and the research path says the log is the prototype. So build the log. You already run Claude Code (or similar) daily on a machine holding your keys — that is the exact environment the problem lives in. Wrap your agent so that every tool call it makes — every file read, every shell command, every network request — is appended to a timestamped record before it runs: what was called, with what arguments, under whose credentials. The cheapest version is a shell wrapper or a few hook lines; the slightly better version emits structured events you can query. Claude Code's own hooks give you a PreToolUse handler that is precisely this seam.

Then, at the end of the week, read it back. Two things happen. First you'll be startled — the agent touched more, and reached further, than you remembered authorising; that surprise is the entire market. Second, you now hold the thing NVIDIA and Cisco don't ship: not a sandbox that contains the agent, but a receipt that proves what it did. The distance between “I logged my own agent” and “a provenance layer any team can drop in” is the whole §0-green startup. Build the ugly single-user version first; the product is that, with the rough edges filed off.

Free resources: Claude Code Hooks (the PreToolUse seam, no extra tooling) and OpenTelemetry if you want the structured-events version that already speaks the language every observability tool reads. Start with the one-file log; reach for OTel only when the one-file log annoys you.
