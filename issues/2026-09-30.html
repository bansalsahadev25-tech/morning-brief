Deploying personal software is still hard, and agents made it urgent

The problem
Agents made it trivial to BUILD software that will only ever have one or a handful of users — a tool for your team, a tracker for your own workflow. Deploying and sharing it is still as hard as it was in 2015. AWS and Azure were designed for software that scales to millions, and that complexity is now the bottleneck.
Who says it's hard
Y Combinator, Requests for Startups — 'A Cloud for Small Software' — source
Why it's still open
The hard parts are not the hosting. They are auth and permissions for arbitrary user-written code, per-company environment customisation, and letting non-technical people share executable code without it becoming a security incident. Everyone underestimates the third one.
Who's already trying
Vercel, Replit, Val Town, Cloudflare Workers, Deno Deploy
Your angle
You already live in this problem — you build with Claude Code and then have nowhere good to put the result. That is the founder's unfair advantage in its cheapest form: you are the user.
Research path
  1. Build the ugliest possible version for yourself first: one command that takes a Claude Code project and gives you a URL with auth.
  2. Read Val Town's public writeups on sandboxing user code — they have hit every wall you will hit.
  3. Then ask 10 people in your hostel who build things where their projects currently live. The answer is 'localhost' and that is the market.

The software running physical work has not changed in 20 years

The problem
80% of the global workforce does not sit at a desk. Construction, maintenance, fleet operations still run on software that dispatches people, tracks them, manages assets and bills the customer. Those industries spend 10-100x more on labour than on software.
Who says it's hard
Y Combinator RFS — 'New Operating Systems for the Physical World' — source
Why it's still open
Incumbents charge for human coordination. Nobody has built the layer that schedules agents, robots and humans together — and whoever does captures end-to-end records of how the work actually happens, which no frontier lab or robotics startup will have.
Who's already trying
ServiceTitan, Samsara, Procore — all built for the old model
Your angle
This is the category your cab booking project sits in. Dispatch, matching, fleet, real-world supply. You are already inside the problem, with users walking past you, which is the part nobody can buy.
Research path
  1. Re-read your own PLAN.md with this framing — you are not building a booking form, you are building dispatch.
  2. Talk to the middleman you already know about what HE cannot see: idle vehicles, no-shows, driver reliability.
  3. Samsara's S-1 — what fleet operators actually pay for, in their own words.

Platform war · the tools you build with just changed under you

OpenAI cut its model price by 80% and turned ChatGPT into an app store — the same week it raised $30B at $1.4 trillion

OpenAI DevDay 2026 · Tue 29 Sep · GPT-6.1 Sol $2/$10 per M tokens (cached $0.10) · run-rate ~$70B · raising ≥$30B at $1.4T · OpenAI · TechCrunch · Apps SDK · Bloomberg · Simon Willison

What shipped
Three moves in one keynote. GPT-6.1 Sol: near the intelligence of the flagship GPT-6 Astra at roughly a fifth of the price — $2/$10 per million tokens in/out, cached input at $0.10 (95% off). By Artificial Analysis' cost-per-task, Sol runs $0.72 vs $3.26 for Astra. Apps in ChatGPT: a full SDK, an app directory, and a marketplace — third-party software now runs inside the chat window, in front of a claimed 1.2B weekly users. And Dots: always-on agents with their own cloud computer and access to 4,000+ apps, running across ChatGPT, Slack and Teams.
The tech
The detail that matters for a builder: the Apps SDK is built on MCP — the Model Context Protocol that Anthropic created. OpenAI adopting its rival's open standard means one app can, in principle, target both ecosystems. On top of that sit an Agents API (OpenAI runs the multi-agent orchestration for you), a Decisions API for classification/routing, and a Codex that now executes in the cloud and reviews your GitHub PRs. The hard part they solved is not intelligence — it is making agents cheap and repeatable enough to leave running.
Why it works
This is the mirror image of yesterday's Anthropic S-1. Both companies are racing to be the default cheap-and-fast worker, because that is the only way to service compute bills measured in hundreds of billions. But OpenAI added a second lever Anthropic didn't: distribution. It is aiming at the app-store model directly — own the surface where the software runs, take a cut, and the model underneath becomes a commodity you happen to also sell.
The money
The same week, OpenAI is in talks to raise ≥$30B at ~$1.4T — a bridge in place of an IPO, since Altman said it won't go public in 2026 on safety grounds. Revenue run-rate is nearing $70B, up ~70% since the start of Q3. Its March round committed $122B at an $852B valuation. The valuation nearly doubled in six months.
The bear case
A $1.4T private valuation on a company still losing money is the same curve-bet as Anthropic's, one turn higher. And the app-store play invites the platform risk it is inflicting on others: every developer who builds on ChatGPT is one policy change from being cut off. Note also OpenAI shelved a more powerful GPT-6.1 as too insecure to release — the ceiling is getting harder to raise safely, which is exactly why the game moved to price and distribution.
For you
Two concrete things this week. First, your input cost just dropped again — the model you drive Claude Code and its rivals with is on a downward price escalator two companies are now funding for you. Build things that get cheaper to run every quarter. Second, and more actionable: there is now an open app directory in front of 1.2B people, with an SDK, and it is accepting submissions. That is a distribution channel a solo builder can reach without a marketing budget — and shipping one live, listed app is exactly the dated, off-campus proof-of-work your transcript will never give you. See today's §6.
Vertical AI · the money is in the boring back office

EliseAI hit a $4B valuation automating the paperwork of apartment buildings — a business nobody in AI wanted to build

EliseAI (fka MeetElise, 2017) · founders Minna Song (CEO), Tony Stoyanov · raised $350M at $4B, co-led a16z + Bessemer, w/ Ontario Teachers' Pension · $200M ARR · used by 1 in 6 US apartments · TechCrunch · Fortune · CNBC

What they do
EliseAI automates the back-office work of landlords — leasing enquiries, maintenance tickets, renewals — and now the same for healthcare (patient intake and paperwork for specialty physician groups). The new product, Apollo, is an AI teammate that works inside a property team's existing software and can complete a task end to end, across every role. Not a chatbot bolted on; the labor itself, done by software.
The founders
Minna Song has a CS degree from MIT and wrote software at Microsoft and MIT Lincoln Laboratory. Co-founder Tony Stoyanov came out of Cambridge CS. Neither came from real estate — which is the whole point. They were technical people who noticed a dumb, expensive problem in an industry that had no technical people in it.
Where the idea came from
This is the transferable part. Song had worked as an administrative assistant at a real estate firm and saw the failure directly: prospective tenants couldn't reach anyone, nobody called back, tours went unbooked on Sundays and after hours. Then she and Stoyanov moved to Arlington, Virginia to sit inside AvalonBay, their first design partner, and be a fly on the wall. The unfair advantage wasn't a model — it was unusual, boring access to a problem everyone else considered beneath them.
The trajectory
Founded 2017, well before the LLM wave — they were doing this the hard way when it was unfashionable, then the technology caught up to them. $200M ARR, and it is the fourth time a16z and Bessemer have put money in since 2023. The last round was ~13 months ago: $250M at $2.2B. The valuation nearly doubled in a year on real recurring revenue, not a demo.
The bear case
Vertical AI wrappers are exactly what the §1 story threatens: if agents inside ChatGPT can call any tool, what stops a landlord from assembling this themselves for the price of tokens? EliseAI's answer has to be the integrations and the data moat, not the model. And housing is cyclical — a rental downturn hits its customers directly. $4B on $200M ARR is a 20x revenue multiple that assumes the healthcare expansion works.
For you
The single most useful pattern in today's brief. The billion-dollar company was not the clever model — it was the founder who had done the boring job and knew where the pain was. You don't need a research lab; you need unusual access to a problem, and the willingness to go sit inside it like Song sat inside AvalonBay. You already have that instinct — it's why you keep a public build log. Point it at an industry you can actually get into, and the AI is now cheap enough (see §1) to be the easy part.
Physical AI · what building robots actually costs

Tesla lined up $30B in credit for robots and robotaxis — and told everyone it has no plans to spend it yet

Tesla · $30B in new credit: Citi $20B 3-yr delayed-draw term loan, Wells Fargo $8B revolver + $2B 364-day · for Cybercab, Optimus, Semi · 2026 capex projected ≥$25B · TechCrunch · Benzinga · FourWeekMBA

What happened
Tesla secured $30B in fresh credit facilities to scale three physical products: the Cybercab robotaxi, the Optimus humanoid, and the Semi truck. The structure: a $20B three-year delayed-draw term loan from Citi, an $8B five-year revolver and a $2B 364-day revolver from Wells Fargo. Crucially, Tesla told regulators it does not plan to draw on any of it this year.
What it really is
Read past the headline number. This is not $30B raised; it is $30B of optionality — a liquidity backstop arranged now so the capital is there when the factory lines need it. One analysis called the 8-K “says almost nothing.” The signal isn't the loan; it's why a company sitting on tens of billions in cash felt it needed the line at all.
Why it's here
Because it prices the thing this brief keeps circling: physical AI is capital-intensive in a way software never is. Optimus and the Semi each required entirely new dedicated factories. Tesla already projects ≥$25B in 2026 capex. The contrast with §1 is the lesson: OpenAI raises equity at $1.4T on a software curve; Tesla arranges debt because it is pouring concrete. Different money for different physics.
The bear case
Neither Cybercab nor Optimus ships at volume yet, and both have slipped before. Arranging credit you don't draw can read as confidence — or as pre-positioning for a cash-hungry year the market hasn't priced. And debt against unproven product lines is the kind of leverage that looks cheap until demand is late.
For you
You build a tiltrotor — you already know hardware eats money that software doesn't. Here is the biggest player in physical AI confirming the shape of it at scale: the constraint on a hardware company is not the idea, it's the capital to build the line that makes the thing. Design your seven rungs so that each one is provable cheaply, before it needs a factory. The founders who survive hardware are the ones who front-load proof and back-load capex — the opposite of how software is built.

Skill

day 4

Ship one app into the ChatGPT directory

~3 hours · free · produces: a live, listed, dated piece of public proof-of-work

Today's §1 opened a distribution channel in front of 1.2B people and started accepting submissions. Yesterday's skill got a model running on your own hardware; today's puts something you made in front of an audience you could never buy your way to. This is the credential your transcript can't give you.

The Apps SDK is built on MCP — the same Model Context Protocol Anthropic uses, so what you learn here transfers to Claude too. Pick the smallest useful thing: an app that does one job well (a unit converter for RC parts, a thrust-to-weight calculator for your drone, anything you actually want). Wire it up with the SDK, render a simple panel, test it locally, then submit it for the directory. The point is not the app — it's completing the full loop from idea to listed and public once.

Free resource: the Apps SDK docs (start with the quick-start, ignore monetization until it works). Once one app is live, the “boring vertical” lesson from §1's EliseAI stops being a story and becomes something you could actually ship — a real tool, in a real directory, that a real stranger can open.
