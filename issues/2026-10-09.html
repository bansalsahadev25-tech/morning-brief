START THIS MONTHgreen · solo · no capital · no credentials

API providers announce their breaking changes instead of just applying them

The problem
When an API changes, every customer hand-edits their own codebase to catch up — the same migration, done thousands of times, badly, in parallel. The provider knows exactly what changed and how to fix it, but ships a changelog and a deprecation warning instead of the fix itself. The missing layer is the one that lets a provider push a code change into its customers' repos, not just a notice.
Who says it's hard
Y Combinator's Requests for Startups, from a founder who worked with 50+ API vendors and watched every one of them announce changes they could have propagated.
Why it's still open
Two years ago nobody would grant an outside tool write access to their repository. Claude Code, Devin and Greptile normalised exactly that — agents proposing commits against a stranger's codebase is now ordinary. The permission barrier dropped recently enough that the obvious product on top of it hasn't been built.
Who's already trying
Greptile and Ellipsis (code review), Renovate (dependency bumps, not API semantics). Nobody connects a provider's breaking change to an automatic, reviewable PR in each customer's repo. And the write-access plumbing keeps spreading — Docker's agent tooling and the week's small agent models (§4) are all bricks in the same wall.
Your angle
Pure software, zero capital, and you already understand agentic code editing from the inside — you are a Claude Code user who's felt a dependency break your build. Narrow enough to prototype in a weekend against a single noisy API. That's exactly this issue's §6 skill.
Research path
  1. Pick one API with frequent breaking changes (Stripe, Twilio) and read two years of its changelog — feel how much of it is mechanical.
  2. Write the migration codemod for a single breaking change by hand (today's §6). You'll understand the real shape of the work in an hour.
  3. Read Renovate's architecture docs on how an automated PR against someone else's repo actually earns enough trust to get merged — that trust, not the diff, is the hard part.
NEEDS A LAB, STANDARDS AND A DECADEred · filed so you recognise it, not so you start it

AI-accelerated math is coming for the encryption the internet runs on

The problem
The public-key cryptography protecting banking, messaging and identity rests on a bet that certain math problems stay hard. This week made the bet look less safe: OpenAI released 722 manuscripts of machine-generated proofs across geometry, algebra and computer science. The deeper problem underneath is that almost no system in production can swap its cryptographic primitives quickly (“crypto-agility”), so a break wouldn't be a patch — it would be a multi-year migration nobody has rehearsed.
Who says it's hard
Vitalik Buterin (take the risk to cryptography from AI-accelerated math seriously) and Scott Aaronson (“The Mathocalypse”), set against OpenAI's drop and Terence Tao's call for a new way to teach math around the machines.
Why it's still open
It needs frontier cryptography, standards bodies and coordinated global migration at once. Post-quantum standards exist on paper; getting the world to adopt crypto-agility before a break rather than after is a decade-long coordination problem, not a product.
Who's already trying
NIST (post-quantum standards), Cloudflare, Google, Apple, PQShield. All large, all slow, all working on the “answer” while adoption lags years behind.
Your angle
Red, and honestly so — a standards-and-PhD problem, not a weekend build. Filed so you recognise it, not so you start it. The one cheap, boring slice that's just software: tooling that inventories which cryptographic primitives a codebase actually depends on, the unglamorous first step of any migration. Learn the vocabulary now; don't found here.
Research path
  1. Read Aaronson's “The Mathocalypse” for the honest state of the threat, hype stripped out.
  2. Skim NIST's post-quantum standards to see what “solved” already looks like on paper.
  3. Notice the cheap adjacent slice: crypto-agility tooling — “what am I even using, and where” — is software even when the cryptography isn't.

Founder war story · a 19-year-old who already did it once

Cal AI's teenage founder sold a $30M-a-year app, then raised $10M to collapse your phone into one wrist-worn assistant

Persona · founded 2026 by Zach Yadegari (19) and co-founders · $10M led by Vine Ventures (w/ Z Fellows' Cory Levy, Collective Global) · valuation undisclosed · $179 wearable band ships December; free iMessage beta live; “a few thousand” beta users, five-figure preorders · TechCrunch · Runtime

What they do
Persona is a personal AI assistant with a hardware shell: a $179 band you tap (or trigger with a wrist-flick) to speak to an agent that books rides, orders food, handles email and plans travel. Deliberately not ambient — it isn't always listening, which is the pointed design choice against the always-on Humane/Bee generation. The thesis, in Yadegari's words: “all of the utility apps you use day-to-day will collapse into a single super app powered by an AI assistant.”
The founder
This is the part worth your attention. Yadegari built his first business in ninth grade and sold a gaming startup for $100,000 at 16. Then he co-founded Cal AI, a calorie-tracking app that hit >$30M in annual revenue in under two years and got acquired by MyFitnessPal in March 2026. He did the acqui-hire, stayed through June, then left to start Persona. He is 19 and on his third company.
Where the idea came from
Same move as Cal AI: build for a user he is. Cal AI worked because he was a young person who wanted to track food without friction; Persona is a young person who wants his phone's busywork to disappear. The unfair advantage isn't a technology — it's that he ships consumer apps fast for a demographic he belongs to and reads instinctively, then lets the app-store ranking tell him if he's right. He's done that twice.
The bear case
Hardware is a graveyard — Humane and the first wave of AI pins died, and a few thousand beta users plus five-figure preorders is a rounding error. Meta (Muse), Amazon (which bought Bee) and Instinct have distribution he can't match. The “super app” thesis has been attempted and failed in the West for a decade, and a button-triggered band is a harder sell than “it's just on your phone.” Software, not the band, is probably the real product.
For you
Forget the wristband; steal the pattern. A 19-year-old with no network and no capital built a $30M business by shipping fast for himself and reading the app-store scoreboard honestly — the single most replicable founder story in this brief, and close to your own position. Note the exact mechanic: pick a user you already are, ship something ugly, let the ranking tell you the truth. His gaming-startup exit at 16 is the version of “proof of work” that no transcript can give you — and it's the thing that made VCs return his call at 19.
Buildable · AI small enough to live on your drone

A speech-recognition model in 16.9 megabytes — smaller than a photo, running on a microcontroller with no internet

Whistle, by Cactus Compute (YC S25) · San Francisco, 8 people · founders Henry Ndubuaku (turned down an Nvidia research role) & Roman Shemet (ex-quant) · open weights on Hugging Face, code on GitHub · Cactus blog · HN (625 pts) · GitHub

What they do
Whistle is a speech-to-text model that fits in a single 16.9MB file and runs on a CPU with no dependencies — phones, wearables, cars, smart-home gadgets, and microcontrollers. Seven languages with auto-detection, word-level timestamps, all on-device. No server, no API bill, no network round-trip.
The tech — and what's hard
The hard part is doing it small without going deaf. Whistle's encoder is eight “Simple Attention” blocks with a Monarch–Hadamard MLP; the whole thing hits 11.1ms to first token on an Apple M4 Pro CPU and decodes 1,319 tokens/second. The headline claim: it beats OpenAI's Whisper Base (145.3MB — 8.6× larger) on word-error rate for LibriSpeech, SPGISpeech and Earnings-22, and loses to it only on a couple of noisier conversational sets. Roughly a tenth the size, better on clean speech.
The founders
Cactus is eight people out of YC's Summer 2025 batch. Ndubuaku walked away from an Nvidia research position to build an on-device inference engine; Shemet is a former quant and economist. They met through YC co-founder matching in London. The whole company is a bet that the interesting frontier isn't bigger models in data centres — it's squeezing useful ones onto the chips already in your pocket.
The bear case
Tiny models have a ceiling — strong on clean, read speech, weaker on cross-talk and accents, which is most of the real world. Apple and Google ship their own on-device speech for free inside the OS, and open weights are hard to turn into a business. “Better than a two-year-old Whisper Base” is a real but modest bar.
For you
This is directly load-bearing for the tiltrotor. On-board speech or audio inference with no radio link, in 17MB, on the kind of microcontroller a drone already carries, is a capability you can actually wire in this month — and it's open, so you can. Beyond the drone, study the design move you keep saying you love: every architectural choice here exists to hit a size budget. That's the “why is this part shaped this way” instinct applied to a neural net. And Cactus is the shape of company you could be in two years — eight people, one sharp constraint, shipping.
The robotics moat · four founders with no robotics background

Sequoia put $60M into a company that pays strangers to film themselves making coffee — the training data robots don't have

Mecka AI · founded 2024 by Josh Gao, Jason Chong, Mogen Cheng & Duy Nguyen (none from robotics) · $60M Series B led by Sequoia, w/ Nvidia and Microsoft's M12 · $500M valuation · 2 years old · TechCrunch · TechCrunch (Sept)

What they do
Mecka (from “mecha”) pays ordinary people to record themselves doing everyday physical tasks — making coffee, fixing a car — wearing body sensors and holding a phone. That motion data is the fuel for training humanoid and other robots. The explicit pitch: be Scale AI for robotics — the human-labelled-data layer, but for bodies in space instead of text.
Why it works (if it works)
Language models had the internet to train on; robots have almost nothing — there is no web-scale corpus of “how a human hand actually opens a jar.” Whoever manufactures that corpus owns an input every robotics lab needs and none can scrape. Sequoia, Nvidia and Microsoft all writing cheques says the data bottleneck is believed to be real and worth owning upstream, the way Scale sat upstream of the whole LLM wave.
The founders
The tell: none of the four come from robotics. Gao and Cheng (both Canadian) built a restaurant-fintech startup; Chong sold a crypto exchange to Coinbase and worked there; Nguyen runs operations. They didn't win on robotics expertise — they won by recognising that the valuable, unglamorous job in any AI boom is building the data pipeline, and moving on it first. That's a pattern, not a fluke (it's literally the Scale AI story).
The bear case
Data-labelling is a margin-thin, commoditising business — Scale's own moat is under pressure. Competitors are already crowding in: XDOF is reportedly raising at a $1.2B valuation, Micro1 at $500M, and Scale itself is expanding into robotics. It's unproven that human motion capture transfers cleanly to a robot with different joints and limits. And big robotics labs may simply collect their own.
For you
Two things to pocket. First, a door: this is a real way for ordinary people to earn by recording data — worth understanding the economics of, because the “humans generate the training data” market is going to be large and is hiring. Second, the mental model, which is the valuable half: in every AI wave, the people who owned the data pipeline made more durable money than the people who built the models — and they usually weren't domain experts, just first. Ask what the equivalent un-scraped dataset is in a corner you can actually see.

Skill

day 11

Write a codemod — automate one breaking API change by hand, and you've built the §0-green prototype

~1–2 hours · free · produces: a script that rewrites old API calls to new across a whole codebase, and a felt understanding of the problem nobody's productised

Today's §0 green is “API providers announce breaking changes instead of applying them.” The cheapest way to understand it is to do one migration the hard way. Pick an API you know has changed — Stripe and Twilio both have long, breaking changelogs — and choose a single change: a renamed field, a moved parameter, a deprecated method. Now write a codemod: a small program that parses source code into a syntax tree, finds every place the old pattern appears, and rewrites it to the new one automatically. ast-grep is the gentlest way in — you write a pattern that looks almost like the code itself, and it does the find-and-rewrite across every file. (The heavier, JavaScript-native tool is jscodeshift.)

Two things happen. First, you feel the shape of the problem: most breaking changes are mechanical, the provider already knows the exact transform, and yet every customer rediscovers it by hand. Second, you're holding the primitive. A codemod that reliably upgrades a caller from v1 to v2 is literally the first feature of the product the YC RFS is asking for — the thing Renovate does for dependency numbers but nobody does for API semantics. Write it once for yourself; the gap between “my script” and “a provider ships this to all its customers” is the whole startup.

Free resource: ast-grep — its playground lets you test a rewrite pattern in the browser before you touch your repo, no install. Then read Renovate's docs on how an automated PR against someone's codebase actually earns a merge — because trust, not the diff, is the part that's hard.
