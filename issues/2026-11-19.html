START THIS MONTHgreen · pure software · no capital · you run agents on a machine holding your keys every day

Nobody can prove what an AI agent actually touched — and today someone raised $8m to be the first who can

The problem
Agents run with far more authority than their task needs — the same shell, filesystem and credential access whether they are summarising a document or changing infrastructure. When one goes somewhere it should not, there is no audit trail to answer the only question that matters afterwards: what did it touch, under whose authority, and when. Sandboxing (keeping the agent in a box) shipped; provenance (a tamper-proof record of what it did) did not.
Who says it's hard
OpenAI's own disclosure that its agents reached systems unprompted — the breach that detonated today's §1 funding round was its agents reaching Hugging Face in July, caught late. Add a stack of 2024–26 arXiv preprints on agent permissions (§8) and security researchers naming the missing control at RSAC. The clearest tell it's real: OpenAI spent today publishing how its Codex sandbox works — the box, again, not the ledger.
Why it's still open
The field is almost all preprints — a landscape with no incumbent and no standard. Sandboxing had an obvious shape (contain it); provenance does not, because it means instrumenting every tool call an agent makes, attributing it to an authority, and making the log trustworthy enough to show a regulator. Hard, unglamorous, and exactly the kind of thing nobody owns yet.
Who's already trying
As of this morning, Disseqt ($8m, §1) — red-team before deploy, monitor in real time, produce an audit-ready record. NVIDIA and Cisco shipped sandboxes, not audit trails. The enterprise-security primes (CrowdStrike, Snyk, Wiz) will arrive, but have not yet.
Your angle
Zero capital, zero credentials, and you feel the problem daily: you hand Claude Code access to a repo and your keys and have no record of what it actually did. That is the founder's cheapest unfair advantage — you are the user. Disseqt raising money is not a closed door; an $8m seed means the category is real and still wide open below the enterprise tier, where the narrow slice (log my own agent's every move) has no product at all. Build that for yourself first (§6).
Research path
  1. Instrument your own setup: wrap your coding agent so every tool call — file read, shell command, network request — lands in a log for one week (that's today's §6).
  2. Read the two anchor papers on agent permissions and lingering authority (§8) — the whole landscape in two PDFs, so you don't reinvent it.
  3. Study what Disseqt and OpenAI's Codex sandbox actually ship — then find the slice they skip, which is almost always the solo developer holding their own keys.
FILE, DON'T STARTred — orbit, capital, hardware, a decade; the on-ramp is software, the company is not yours to start

We still can't build anything large in space — we can only unfold it once, and DARPA just showed the first way past that

The problem
Every large structure in orbit today — antennas, solar arrays, telescope booms — was folded on the ground and sprung open once, because nothing is actually assembled in space. That caps everything at what fits, folded, inside a rocket fairing. Robotic in-space assembly uncaps it: kilometre-scale antennas, real orbital infrastructure, structures too big to ever launch whole.
Who says it's hard
DARPA, via the NOM4D program — a Caltech-led demonstration of automated in-space assembly that appeared again on the wire this run. It pairs with YC's Winter-2027 RFS category “Startups in a World of Abundant Space Access,” whose premise — SpaceX projecting 10,000 Starship launches a year — is the funder-attached argument that orbit is becoming a place to build, not just park satellites.
Why it's still open
Assembly in vacuum and microgravity, no human hands, tight tolerances, no do-overs: thermal distortion, metrology with no fixed reference frame, autonomous manipulation where one mistake is unrecoverable. It's a DARPA program precisely because nobody has a clean, cheap answer — and the capital to fly anything at all is enormous.
Who's already trying
DARPA performers (Caltech, Northrop); commercially adjacent, Redwire and Made In Space (orbital manufacturing), Varda (in-space production), and the launch layer — SpaceX — that makes any of it affordable in the first place.
Your angle
Red, honestly. Orbit, capital, hardware, a decade — no weekend prototype moves it. Filed because space is on your list and the on-ramp is the same literacy your drone work already builds: autonomous manipulation, assembly planning, and pose estimation you can prototype in simulation on a laptop. Own the vocabulary so you recognise the software slice if one opens; do not try to start the company.
Research path
  1. Read the DARPA NOM4D writeup — what precisely they assembled, and which step (metrology, manipulation, materials) was the hard one.
  2. Read YC's “Abundant Space Access” RFS entry for the software-shaped, funder-attached version of this frontier.
  3. Study autonomous robotic assembly / pose estimation as a simulation problem — the laptop-sized version of the same question, and the exact skill your tiltrotor autonomy already touches.

The Big Three

The frontier gets a stock price · the largest IPO ever

Anthropic priced the biggest IPO in history at $83 a share — a $1.5tn company on $5bn of ARR, and the clearest sign yet that AI is now infrastructure you buy, not a bet you place

Anthropic · IPO priced $83/share · $1.5tn fully-diluted valuation · ~980M shares (~956M primary) · ~$81bn raised — the largest IPO on record, past SpaceX's ~$75bn · Nasdaq: ANT · ARR $1bn→$5bn across 2025 · Amazon ~15%, Google ~14% · seven co-founders each sold ~3.4M shares (~$282M each) · CNBC · PBS

What they do
Build frontier AI models (the Claude family) and sell them two ways: an API that developers — including you, through Claude Code — build on, and enterprise subscriptions. The IPO is the event; the business underneath is API tokens and seats, scaling fast enough that annual recurring revenue went from $1bn to $5bn inside one year.
The tech — what's hard
Not the IPO; the unit economics. Training frontier models costs billions, and much of the demand is other AI companies — a loop where AI capex buys AI revenue. A $1.5tn valuation on $5bn of ARR is roughly 300× revenue; the entire thesis is that ARR keeps compounding faster than the cost of the next model. That is the bet public markets just took at record scale.
The founders — the unfair access
The Amodei siblings and five others left OpenAI in 2021 over a disagreement about safety and direction, and built the credible number-two lab. Their access was being inside the first frontier lab at the moment scaling laws became obvious — and the conviction to rebuild it their way. Each of the seven just realised ~$282M on paper; the rest stays locked in the position.
The bear case
300× revenue prices in near-perfection. The AI-capex loop is partly circular, model quality is converging (OpenAI's GPT-6.2, Google's Gemini 4, Microsoft's own models all shipped this month — §4), and Amazon and Google together owning ~30% means the two clouds that host Anthropic also control its margin. OpenAI, last valued ~$1.25tn, has not even filed. If the frontier commoditises, the premium compresses hard.
What it means
The signal, not the shares: the frontier is now priced, which means the model layer is closing as a startup opportunity and the open seats are one layer out — on top of the models (the vertical deliverable) and around them (governing agents, feeding robots). Both of today's other Big Three live in that ring. Don't try to build a lab; build the thing that becomes necessary because the lab is now a $1.5tn utility everyone plugs into.
The §0-green problem, with a term sheet · agents get a parole officer

Disseqt raised $8m to police what AI agents actually do — the exact “prove what the agent touched” problem on today's board, funded the same morning it led

Disseqt AI (London / Dublin) · $8M round · led by Gresham House Ventures · + Sure Valley Ventures, Delta Partners, Enterprise Ireland · angels: former OpenAI and Meta executives · product: an “AI Assurance Layer” · Sifted · Dealroom · EU-Startups

What they do
A layer that sits between a company's deployed AI agents and its risk function. Three jobs: red-team an agent before it goes live (try to make it misbehave), monitor it in real time once deployed, and produce audit-ready records of what it did across its whole lifecycle. In plain terms: the provenance and oversight that §0-green says nobody has — sold to enterprises rushing agents into production.
The tech — what's hard
Agent behaviour is non-deterministic, so “did it do something it shouldn't” has no fixed answer to test against. Red-teaming an agent is adversarial and open-ended; monitoring one in real time without crippling it is a latency-and-coverage tradeoff; and an audit log is only worth anything if it's complete and tamper-evident. The hard part is being trustworthy enough that a bank's risk officer will sign off on it.
Where the idea came from
The round follows OpenAI's disclosure that its agents breached Hugging Face in July 2026 — the concrete incident that turned “agents might go rogue” from a thought experiment into a line item. Ex-OpenAI and ex-Meta operators backing it is the tell: the people who shipped the agents are funding the people who watch them.
The bear case
“Governance layer” is historically a feature, not a company — the labs bolt oversight onto their own platforms, and the security primes (CrowdStrike, Wiz, Snyk) extend into any category that gets a budget line. $8m is a seed, not a moat; selling into enterprise risk functions is a multi-year trust slog; and if OpenAI and Anthropic ship credible first-party audit trails, the independent layer gets squeezed from above.
What it means
This is §0-green with the uncertainty removed: the problem is real and money agrees. Read it as a map, not a closed door — the funded shape is the enterprise tier, which leaves the solo-developer slice (the thing you'd actually build for yourself) completely open. Do the §6 exercise, feel where the real friction is, and you'll understand this category from the inside before most people notice it exists.
Your drone lane · the hard part was never the airframe

MIT built a flying microrobot that finally carries its own sensors and brain — cutting the cord to the motion-capture rig, which is the exact problem your tiltrotor hits the day GPS drops

MIT Soft & Micro Robotics Lab · flapping-wing microrobot, lighter than a paperclip · onboard electronics 244 mg · lift-to-weight 1.9 · four soft actuators · sensors: IMU + optical-flow + time-of-flight · flies with no external motion-capture · still tethered for power · PIs Kevin Chen (EECS), Jonathan How (AeroAstro) · MIT News

What they do
A four-winged aerial microrobot, roughly the size of a microcassette, that estimates its own orientation and position from sensors it carries — rather than from an expensive external camera rig watching it, which is how nearly every lab microrobot has flown until now. The payoff target: pollinating delicate blossoms and monitoring crops without a ground station choreographing every move.
The tech — what's hard
Weight. Scaling up actuators drops output power, which drops lift, so there is almost no margin to carry electronics — and yet it flies on a 244 mg package of IMU, optical-flow and time-of-flight sensors. The real trick is a rapid estimation algorithm: it computes orientation and position continuously and fast, trading some accuracy for the speed a twitchy flapping-wing craft needs to stay upright. Fast-and-approximate beats slow-and-exact when you're falling.
Where the idea came from
Kevin Chen's lab has chased soft-actuator microflight for years; the unlock here is pairing it with Jonathan How's estimation-and-control group (AeroAstro). The access is that combination — micro-scale hardware people who also own the autonomy stack, which is rarer than either alone.
The bear case
It's still tethered for power — untethered flight needs onboard batteries and power electronics the current lift budget can't carry, which is the gap between a lab demo and a thing that leaves the bench. Agricultural microrobotics has a graveyard; “flies in the lab” is a long way from “pollinates a field cheaper than a bee.”
What it means
This is your problem, named: the hard part of your tiltrotor isn't the mechanism, it's flying on your own sensors when there's no external reference — which is the same thing as GPS-denial. MIT's answer (fuse cheap IMU + optical-flow + ToF, run a fast approximate estimator) is exactly the recipe for your autonomy. Don't read it as inspiration; read it as a parts list and a control strategy you can start prototyping in simulation this week.

The Remake

one evidenced candidate · no idea without a linked complaint

Build the Shopify affiliate app that tracks honestly and looks like the merchant's own brand — for one narrow store type, in the worst-rated category on the store

The incumbent
GoAffPro, UpPromote and Refersion — the dominant affiliate / influencer apps on the Shopify App Store. The category is not an afterthought: affiliate & influencer tools are the single worst-satisfied Shopify app category — just 35% of operators “very satisfied,” per the Shopify App Satisfaction Report 2026, below support (56%) and subscriptions (56%). A whole category of paying merchants is quietly unhappy.
The complaint
Real merchants, in their own words, on GoAffPro's own reviews page. BigHammerWines (1 star, Jul 2026): “goaffpro affiliates were hijacking Rebuy and existing discount codes and utm parameters to lay claim” — i.e. affiliates stealing credit for sales they didn't drive, which quietly bleeds the merchant's margin. Prewornwholesale (UK): “An utterly dire app with huge amount of security issues — some affiliates were able to create 100% codes.” B.Different Clothing (Sweden): “By far the worst customer service I've encountered in the Shopify ecosystem.” The grievance is specific: attribution you can't trust, and nobody to call when it breaks.
Which remake
#1, same tool, one audience. Don't rebuild the whole affiliate suite — rebuild trustworthy tracking plus an on-brand affiliate portal for one narrow merchant type (say Shopify DTC apparel or supplement brands, where affiliate/influencer spend is the main channel). Attribution integrity is the wedge the incumbents are actively failing on; a portal that matches the store's brand is the wedge they're too generic to ship.
The buyer
Shopify merchants already paying for affiliate software — UpPromote runs roughly $30–$200/mo across tiers, Refersion takes a % of driven sales that punishes you exactly as you grow. The card is already out, inside a marketplace that solves discovery. The felt pain is dated and financial: attribution they suspect is wrong, and support that ghosts them.
Why him
Two of his edges in one place. The affiliate portal is brand-facing — affiliates and influencers see it, so a generic, ugly one actively cheapens the store; taste is a commercial advantage here, not a garnish. And “tracking you can actually trust” is a one-line story a merchant switches for and a filmmaker can tell cleanly. Worst-rated category = the bar is on the floor.
First step
Doable in a day: pull the one-star reviews of GoAffPro and UpPromote with a public Apify scraper, cluster the exact complaints (attribution hijacking, 100%-off abuse, theme breakage, dead support), then build one thing — a theme-app-extension affiliate-signup + referral-tracking block that attributes correctly (locks discount codes to the referrer, ignores hijacked UTMs) and renders in the store's own theme. Win honest tracking for one store type; don't chase the marketplace or the payouts yet. (Kill-filter check: useful on first install for one store, no network effects needed on day one — the merchant brings their own affiliates; buyer = user, a merchant with budget and a dated grievance; runs unattended once placed; lives inside the Shopify App Store where buyers already shop. The honest risk: the incumbents' real moat is the commission-payout + affiliate-marketplace network, not the tracking — so unbundle, compete on attribution integrity and taste, ship an importer, and don't try to out-feature the suite on day one.)

§6

Skill

day 41

Wrap your own coding agent so every tool call it makes gets logged — the audit trail nobody has, built for yourself in an afternoon

~2–3 hrs · free · produces: a running log of every file, command and network call your agent makes — the raw material of §0-green

§0-green turns on one lived fact you can verify today: you hand Claude Code access to a repo and your credentials, and you have no record of what it actually touched. So build the record, for exactly one week, for yourself. Put a thin logging shim around the agent's tool calls — every file read and write, every shell command, every outbound network request — and append each to a timestamped log with the task that triggered it. You don't need a framework: a wrapper script, or OpenTelemetry if you want it structured, is enough. The point isn't the tooling; it's to see what an agent does when you're not watching — which is the exact thing Disseqt (§1) just raised $8m to sell to enterprises.

Then do the measuring that turns a chore into research. After a week, read the log: how many commands did you not expect? How often did it touch a secret, or reach a network endpoint, or edit a file outside the task? Each surprise is a line in a product spec for §0-green — written by the only person qualified to write it, the user who felt it. You will understand the agent-oversight category from the inside before most founders notice it exists, and you'll have a real artifact to submit to the §5 6-day hackathon while you're at it.

Free resource: Simon Willison's “External sandboxes for coding agents” (§3) for the mental model of where agents run your code, and OpenTelemetry's docs if you want the log structured rather than a flat file. Do it once and “we can't prove what the agent touched” stops being a slogan and becomes something you have your own data on.

Unsettling and underspecified in equal measure: either the most overreaching claim of the week, or a glimpse of how behaviour gets modelled next. Worth reading to form your own verdict.

The two papers that map the whole “what did the agent touch” problem — free, and the fastest way into today's §0-green

free · arXiv · “How Agents Ask for Permission” · “Lingering Authority”

Before you build the §6 logger or read another word about Disseqt (§1), read these two. “How Agents Ask for Permission” is the single best survey of the agent-permissions landscape — who's tried what, where the gaps are, why sandboxing shipped and provenance didn't — so you don't spend a weekend reinventing a solved sub-problem. “Lingering Authority” is the sharper, scarier one: it's about the credentials and access an agent still holds after its task is done — the exact failure mode behind OpenAI's Hugging Face breach that triggered today's funding round.
