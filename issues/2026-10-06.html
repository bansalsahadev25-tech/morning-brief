START THIS MONTHsolo · no capital · no credentials

Millions of apps are now written by people who cannot audit them

The problem
AI now writes shippable code for people without the background to review it — and the bill is arriving. This week alone: a cross-tenant data-exposure flaw in Cloudflare Containers, a Danish government database leaking 8 million citizens' records, and a structural trust flaw in MCP that lets one agent feed another a malicious prompt. One honest Lobsters post shipped a mail-security tool and called it “100% vibecoded.” The gap between what people can now build and what they can verify is the fastest-growing attack surface on the internet.
Who says it's hard
Practitioners in r/SaaS asking, repeatedly, how to test vibe-coded apps — source — reinforced this quarter by a run of live data exposures from AI-built products. It is new enough that the affected population did not exist three years ago.
Why it's still open
The incumbents (Snyk, Semgrep) built for professional engineers who already understand the finding. The new population can't read the output, so a scanner that dumps 200 warnings is useless to them. The unsolved part is not detection — it is translation and triage for someone who shipped a Stripe integration they don't fully understand.
Who's already trying
Snyk and Semgrep (pro-grade, wrong audience); a few “AI reviews your AI code” startups, mostly aimed at dev teams. Nobody owns the non-technical builder who just wants to know “is this safe to put on the internet, yes or no.”
Your angle
You are the user, and so is everyone in your hostel who ships with Claude Code. Green for the cheapest possible reason: no capital, immediate feedback, and a market you can interview in person this week. Start by breaking your own apps — that is literally today's §6 skill.
Research path
  1. Take three things you built with Claude Code and genuinely try to break them — exposed keys, open endpoints, missing auth. Write down what you find.
  2. Read Semgrep's rule syntax — the state of the art you'd be simplifying, not out-engineering.
  3. Ask in r/SaaS what they'd pay for. They answer in specifics, and the answer is usually “tell me what's dangerous in plain English.”
NEEDS REAL USERS, OR 6–12 MONTHSamber · you're already inside this one

The software running physical work hasn't changed in 20 years

The problem
80% of the global workforce does not sit at a desk. Construction, maintenance, fleet and dispatch still run on software built to coordinate humans — schedule them, track them, bill the customer. Those industries spend 10–100× more on labour than on software. The opening now: nobody has built the layer that schedules agents, robots and humans together. IEEE Spectrum this week profiled a researcher trying to eliminate stoplights entirely by coordinating vehicles directly — the same idea, one domain over: the coordination is the product.
Who says it's hard
Y Combinator's Requests for Startups — “New Operating Systems for the Physical World” — source.
Why it's still open
Incumbents (ServiceTitan, Samsara, Procore) charge for human coordination and are structurally built for the old model. Whoever builds the agent-plus-human dispatch layer captures the end-to-end record of how the work actually happens — data no frontier lab or robotics startup will ever have, because it lives in the field, not the datacentre.
Who's already trying
ServiceTitan, Samsara, Procore — all built before agents existed and priced for enterprise. The physical-world VLA-model wave (see §3's Construction Physics piece) is making the “robot” half real faster than anyone has built the scheduling layer above it.
Your angle
This is the exact category your cab-booking project sits in. You are not building a booking form — you are building dispatch, with real-world supply walking past you every day. That proximity is the founder's unfair advantage you can't buy. Amber, not green, because it needs real users and trust, not a weekend — but the field research is free.
Research path
  1. Re-read your own PLAN.md with this framing: you are building a dispatch OS, not a form. The reframe changes what you build next.
  2. Talk to the middleman you already know about what he can't see: idle vehicles, no-shows, driver reliability. That blindness is the product.
  3. Read Samsara's S-1 — what fleet operators actually pay for, in their own words. It is a map of where the money already is.

New problems on the feed today: DARPA re-surfaced the Vertical Lift Challenger and the CIDAR passive-ranging challenge — both already filed under the defense entry, neither new. YC's RFS is unchanged since the Fall 2026 publish, and nothing from Marginal Revolution or the Institute for Progress rose to a fundable new problem. No addition to the board today.

Founder war story · your exact age, with a16z's cheque

A 19-year-old just raised $11M from a16z to sell a computer built for your AI, not for you

Ghost · San Francisco · founder Zain Javaid, 19 (ex-quant trader) · co-founders Nicholas Chua (19), Yifei Chen (24), Gautam Sharda (23) · product Core, a $3,499 headless PC · $11M seed led by a16z (Abstract, Audacious, SV Angel, Nova) · ships late Oct 2026 · TechCrunch · Techmeme

What they do
Ghost sells Core: a $3,499 headless box — no screen — with an Nvidia RTX Pro 4000 SFF Blackwell GPU, built to run AI agents locally and continuously. It ships with three open models preinstalled (Qwen3.8-27B, Qwen-3.8-Next, Gemma-4-31B), its own software layer, browser and filing system, and you talk to it from your phone. The pitch, in Javaid's words: “the next computer isn't for you, it's for your AI agent.” Your models, memory and data stay on the device, encrypted so that not even Ghost can read them.
Where the idea came from
Javaid was a quantitative trader — math and data applied to markets — who pivoted after ChatGPT's 2022 release. His observation is the whole company: “running models on existing hardware is a terrible experience.” He is building for the gap between “agents can now do real work” and “there is nowhere good, private and always-on to run them.” Yesterday's brief covered Strata stacking tricks to run a 125B model on a gaming GPU; Ghost is the company shaped like that same realisation — local inference just crossed from hack to product.
Why it works (if it works)
Two bets. One, that privacy becomes the buying reason: a box no cloud provider — and no ad model — can read is a real answer to the exact anxiety OpenAI's ad launch (below) creates. Two, that “always-on agent in the house” is a new device category, the way the router or the NAS was. a16z leading at seed means the thesis, not the revenue, is what got funded — this is a bet on a category existing.
The bear case
A $3,499 single-GPU box is a hard sell when the same buyer can rent far more compute by the hour, and Apple/Nvidia/any OEM can bundle “local AI” into hardware you already own. Consumer hardware is brutal: inventory, support, returns, a late-October first batch with everything unproven. “Your data stays local” is a value most people say they want and few pay $3,499 for. This could be a beautiful product that is simply too early.
For you
Read the founder, not just the box. Javaid is 19, non-technical-origin (a trader), and raised an a16z seed on a thesis plus a prototype — the single most useful data point in this brief for someone your age. What got funded was a sharp observation about a gap he personally felt, not a credential. That is the entire proof-of-work bet you're already making, validated at $11M. Study how a 19-year-old with no hardware background got a16z in the room — the on-ramp is more transferable than the product.
Deep tech · the most expensive bet on an architecture in the market

Etched is being offered a $40–50B valuation for a chip that can run only transformers

Etched · founded 2022 by Gavin Uberti, Chris Zhu, Robert Wachen (Harvard dropouts; Uberti & Zhu are Thiel Fellows) · product Sohu, a transformer-only ASIC · offers at $40–50B, up from $21B in Aug (Jane Street, $700M) · TechCrunch · Techmeme · background

What they do
Etched builds Sohu, an ASIC — a chip hard-wired to do exactly one thing — and that one thing is run the transformer, the architecture under nearly every modern LLM. A GPU is a generalist; Sohu throws away everything that isn't a transformer and claims an order-of-magnitude speed/efficiency lead over Nvidia's best as a result. The investment thesis just repriced: offers at $40–50B, double August's $21B, in a couple of months. McKinsey's note this week on inference reshaping chip economics is the macro that money is chasing.
The founders
Uberti and Zhu met in Math 55, Harvard's famously brutal intro-math course, bonded over a project on AI at scale, dropped out, and wrote a paper arguing that transformers were important enough to deserve silicon built only for them. That paper is the company. It is the Nvidia-in-your-canon pattern exactly: bet the whole company on a market structure that doesn't fully exist yet, and be right about it before anyone else commits.
Why it works (if it works)
Specialisation beats generality once the workload stops changing. If the transformer is the steam engine of this era — the fixed thing everyone builds on — then a chip that does only transformers wins on performance-per-dollar the way an ASIC always beats a CPU at a frozen task. The whole valuation is a wager that the architecture has stopped moving.
The bear case
It is the cleanest bear case in tech: if AI moves off transformers, Etched is toast. An ASIC can't be reprogrammed around a new architecture — that's the entire point of it. Meanwhile the research frontier (state-space models, diffusion LMs, whatever's next) is explicitly hunting for the transformer's successor, and Nvidia's generality is precisely insurance against that. A $40B+ valuation on a single-architecture bet is either visionary or a widow-maker, with very little in between.
For you
This is a masterclass in the shape of a deep-tech bet, and it's worth holding next to Ghost above. Both are all-in wagers on “this is the thing now” — one on local inference, one on the transformer. The transferable lesson: the biggest outcomes come from committing hard to a specific belief about the future and being early, and the size of the prize is proportional to how wrong you'd be if the belief is wrong. Etched is the pure version. Watch whether the architecture holds — it's the single most important variable under half of today's funding news.
Dealmaking · how the dominant AI product decides to make money

OpenAI turned on ads inside ChatGPT — and the first ones sit next to your images

OpenAI · visual display ads alongside ChatGPT image generation · limited US advertisers, rolling out later Oct 2026 · eventual reach 1.2B weekly users · paid tiers see no ads · plus a Razorpay deal to onboard Indian advertisers · TechCrunch · OpenAI

What happened
OpenAI added visual display ads that appear next to the images ChatGPT generates for you — product inspiration, how a thing is used, the experience around a service. It is labelled, kept separate from the generated image, and (OpenAI says) does not influence the model's answer; Plus/Pro/Enterprise subscribers see none of it. It arrives with a full ad-tech stack — click attribution, a dozen measurement partners — and a Razorpay partnership to onboard Indian brands. This is the free tier's business model arriving.
Why it matters
For three years the open question under every consumer-AI company was “how does the free tier pay for itself?” This is the answer, from the market leader: advertising against 1.2 billion weekly users. The “answer engine” is becoming an ad channel, which means the incentives of every free AI assistant are about to bend the way search's did. That reframes a lot — including exactly why someone would pay $3,499 for a box (Ghost) whose entire pitch is that no ad model can see your data.
The bear case
Ads may corrode the one thing that makes ChatGPT useful — trust that the answer isn't bought — and the “separate, doesn't influence the answer” firewall is exactly what search promised before it eroded. Power users are on paid tiers already, so the ad-funded cohort may be the least commercially valuable one. Regulators are circling AI output (the EU watermarking rules shipped the same week). The format may also simply underperform — nobody yet knows what an ad is worth next to a generated image.
For you
Watch the business-model move the way you'd watch a deal in Entourage — for the structure. Two takeaways. Strategically: when the dominant free product monetises by advertising, it opens a durable wedge for anyone selling the opposite — privacy, no-ads, you-own-it (that's Ghost's whole thesis, handed to them). And practically: ChatGPT is becoming a distribution channel with a Razorpay on-ramp in India — a place your future product's customers will be reachable, and worth understanding before everyone else does.

Skill

day 8

Break your own app — run a real security pass on something you shipped

~2 hours · free · produces: a list of real findings, and a felt sense of today's §0 green problem

Today's §0 green says millions of apps now ship from people who can't audit them, and the only way to know if that's a real company is to be that person for an afternoon. So take one thing you built with Claude Code — the more “it just works” it feels, the better — and genuinely try to break it. Check the obvious killers first: is an API key committed to the repo or shipped to the client? Is any endpoint missing an auth check? Can you read another user's data by changing an ID in the URL? Point Semgrep at the codebase and read what it flags — then, crucially, notice how much of its output you don't understand.

That gap is the whole §0 company. The scanner finds the issue; it does not tell a non-expert what to do, or which of 200 warnings actually matters. Whoever closes that gap — turning raw findings into “here are the three things that could leak your users' data, and here's the fix” — owns a market that did not exist three years ago. You'll have run the first user interview on yourself, and you'll know by dinner whether the problem pulls you.

Free resource: Semgrep — open-source static analysis with a big community ruleset. Start with its playground and the default rules for your language. Read its rule-writing docs after, to feel exactly how much expertise today's tools assume — expertise your users don't have.
