Every accelerator says it funds people “solving hard problems,” and none tells you which. This does — sourced, graded honestly, with somewhere to start reading. Today both read straight off the day's thesis: one green you live inside every time you finish building something with Claude Code — the thing you made has nowhere good to go; and one red to file, not start — telling a real human from a machine, a question that only gets heavier the week capital buys AI into the banker's and lawyer's seats.

START THIS MONTHgreen · pure software · no capital · you are the user every single day

You can now build software in an afternoon and still have nowhere to put it — and agents just turned that into the bottleneck

The problem
Agents made it trivial to build software that will only ever have one or a handful of users — a tool for your team, a tracker for your own workflow. Deploying and sharing it is still as hard as it was in 2015. AWS and Azure were built for software that scales to millions, and that complexity is now the bottleneck: you can produce the thing in an afternoon and it dies on localhost.
Who says it's hard
Y Combinator's RFS names it directly — “A Cloud for Small Software.” And the wire is the premise: Harvard researchers found that once agents write the code, the bottleneck moves to reviewing and shipping it, and Simon Willison spent the day mapping how to actually run agent-built code safely. More software, built faster, by more people — and still no clean path from “it runs” to “a stranger can use it.”
Why it's still open
The hard parts are not the hosting. They are auth and permissions for arbitrary user-written code, per-person customisation, and letting non-technical people share something executable without it becoming a security incident. Everyone underestimates the third one — which is exactly why the incumbents built for scale, not for sharing, and left the gap open.
Who's already trying
Vercel, Replit, Val Town, Cloudflare Workers, Deno Deploy — all closer than AWS, none of them yet the one-command answer for “take this thing I built and give me a URL with a login.”
Your angle
You already live in this problem: you build with Claude Code and then have nowhere good to put the result. That is the founder's unfair advantage in its cheapest form — you are the user, today, for free. Build the ugliest possible version for yourself first and the market shows up in your own friction.
Research path
  1. Build the ugliest version for yourself: one command that takes a Claude Code project and hands you a URL with auth. Ship it for one project (see §6).
  2. Read Val Town's public writeups on sandboxing user-written code — they have hit every wall you will hit, and documented it.
  3. Ask ten people in your hostel who build things where their projects currently live. The answer is “localhost,” and that answer is the market.
FILE, DON'T STARTred — cryptographic standards, regulators and distribution at once; not a building problem for you

We no longer have a reliable way to tell who is real — the week machines took the banker's, lawyer's and closer's chair

The problem
Every trust signal we have — a face, a voice, a phone call — was built for a world where faking a human was expensive. That world is gone. Whoever builds the replacement — a reliable, portable way to prove “a real, authorised human is on the other end” — becomes the layer every bank, app and video call checks.
Who says it's hard
The live evidence is the standards fight and the fraud wave: C2PA is the industry's attempt at cryptographic content provenance, built because the old signals stopped working. And today's own page is the argument for the problem — if an AI closes your sales calls (yesterday's Belfort voice startup), argues your M&A (Astra for Law) and models your deal (Model ML), then “is the thing acting actually a real, authorised person?” becomes the load-bearing question under the whole economy.
Why it's still open
It needs cryptographic standards, regulators and distribution deals simultaneously — the hardest kind of problem, because no single actor can ship the answer alone. A provenance standard nobody adopts is a PDF; adoption needs the platforms, the banks and the law all moving together.
Who's already trying
Worldcoin, Persona, Clear, C2PA, and Apple/Google device attestation — each owns a slice (biometrics, KYC, provenance, hardware trust), none owns the whole chain from “this is a human” to “and everyone trusts that claim.”
Your angle
Red for you, honestly. This is a standards-and-distribution problem, not a building problem — no weekend prototype moves it. Filed so you recognise it when it matters, not as a thing to start. The useful move is literacy: understand C2PA well enough to have an opinion, and watch which high-trust seat AI takes next.
Research path
  1. Read about C2PA content provenance until you can argue both sides of whether it will work.
  2. Track the pattern on the wire: each time an AI takes a named human role (banker, lawyer, closer), note which trust signal it quietly bypasses.
  3. File it and revisit in two years. The narrow, boring slice — if one appears — will be tooling, not the standard.

The Big Three

The $15 expert, funded at $1.5B · the junior banker's job, done by agents

Model ML raised $250m to do the junior banker's grunt work — at a claimed 2% of a frontier model's cost, by two brothers who last sold a camera-rental app

Model ML (London) · $250M Series B · $1.5B valuation · led by General Atlantic + QED Investors · after a $75M Series A · ~18 months from stealth · founders brothers Chaz & Arnie Englander (prev. Fat Llama, sold to Hygglo for $41.5M, 2022) · competitors Rogo ($160M at $2B), Hebbia · TechCrunch · General Atlantic

What they do
AI agents that plug into the tools a banking analyst actually lives in — Excel, PowerPoint, the data room — understand the full context of a live deal, and keep the financial model and pitch deck updated themselves when something changes. Building models, prepping decks, writing due-diligence reports: the repetitive craft juniors bill 90-hour weeks on.
The tech — what's hard
Not the chat; it's reliability. A banker will not ship a model an agent half-updated, so the hard part is an agent that tracks numbers across dozens of documents and gets them right every time. Their cost claim is the real bet: ~2% of frontier-model cost by running open-source models tuned for the task instead of calling GPT or Claude on every step. If true, that's a structural margin edge; if it's a quality floor, the labs' raw model quality wins the bake-off.
The founders — the unfair access
Chaz and Arnie Englander built Fat Llama, a peer-to-peer rental marketplace, and sold it to Hygglo in 2022 for $41.5M — a modest, real exit. They are not bankers. The access here isn't domain; it's a second-time founding team that already knows how to sell software into cautious buyers, pointed at a market (finance) that pays enormous sums for junior labour and hates every minute of it.
The bear case
Rogo ($160M at a $2B valuation in April) and Hebbia are ahead and better-capitalised; Anthropic and OpenAI ship financial tools and, as of today, vertical products that could swallow this whole. “2% of the cost” invites a quality comparison they may lose, and selling into banks is a years-long trust slog, not a weekend. No customers or revenue disclosed.
What it means
This is §0's fifteen-dollar-expert at $1.5B — proof the “produce the finished deliverable, not a chatbot” thesis raises real money. The portable lesson isn't the raise you can't replicate; it's the shape. Find one credentialed, process-heavy deliverable — a model, a deck, a filing — and ship the finished artifact end-to-end for a buyer who currently overpays for it. Pick a vertical you can watch and build the output, not the conversation.
The warehouse worker, rebuilt as a robot · a viral sim grows up into a $3.5B company

Genesis AI raised $500m to teach robots factory work from videos of human hands — led by the PhD whose simulator went viral, then got caught overclaiming

Genesis AI (Paris + San Carlos, CA) · $500M Series A · $3.5B valuation · $605M total since Mar 2025 · co-led Premji Invest, Khosla, Eclipse · + Nvidia, AMD Ventures, T. Rowe, Bpifrance, Samsung Next, Zoom; angels Eric Schmidt, Eric Yuan, Jerry Yang, Zach Kirkhorn · ~100 staff · founders Zhou Xian (CEO, CMU robotics PhD), Théophile Gervet · Crunchbase · Sifted

What they do
A full-stack robotics company — a foundation model (GENE), a simulator (Genesis World) and a robotic hand (Eno) — betting that robots can learn physical tasks by watching video of human hands, rehearsing millions of times in simulation, then doing the job on a real factory, warehouse or lab floor. The pitch is “rent the robot and the brain as one service,” not sell hardware; an LG CNS partnership is the first channel.
The tech — what's hard
The data problem. There is no internet-scale corpus of robots doing physical work the way there is text, so Genesis manufactures experience in simulation — which only pays off if the sim's physics are faithful enough that a learned skill transfers to a real arm. That “sim-to-real” gap is the whole ballgame, and the whole risk.
The founders — the unfair access
Zhou Xian started the Genesis research project at Carnegie Mellon in 2022; Théophile Gervet joined after graduating in late 2024. Their access is the simulator itself: Genesis went viral in December 2024 as an open-source “generative physics engine,” which is both the credibility and the cautionary tale.
The bear case
That viral moment is the warning. The launch claimed Genesis ran 430,000× faster than real-time, and Zhou Xian later publicly walked it back — the benchmark used static scenes, disabled self-collision and idled most frames; the HN thread took it apart. So the bear case writes itself: robotics foundation models are capital-furnaces (this is $605M before durable revenue), sim-to-real has killed well-funded robot companies before, and the founder carries one public credibility ding on exactly the claim the company rests on. Nvidia, Physical Intelligence, Skild, Figure and 1X are all chasing the same floor.
What it means
For you the signal is where the smart money thinks the next platform is — embodied AI — and the on-ramp is unusually open because the core tool is free. Genesis World is open source; you can run robot simulation on a laptop (it's today's §8). That is the exact sim-to-real literacy your tiltrotor autonomy wants, and you do not need $500m to learn the vocabulary the whole category now runs on.
The platform eats the vertical · OpenAI ships the “$15 expert” itself

OpenAI's GPT-6.2 shipped with “Astra for Law” and “ChatGPT for Sales” — the platform moving into the exact vertical seats founders were told to go build

OpenAI · GPT-6.2 Astra (frontier) + Sol (cheaper/faster) · Astra +6.2pts over GPT-6 Astra using 22% fewer tokens · on Agents' Last Exam, +5.5 ahead of Claude Fable 5.2 at 5% of the cost · for law: 62% fewer tokens, top on 8/10 research tasks across tax, M&A, litigation · ranks #2 on Artificial Analysis, behind Fable 5.2 · lower API prices · OpenAI · The Decoder · Simon Willison

What it is
OpenAI's latest pair — Astra, the frontier reasoning model, and Sol, a cheaper, faster sibling that in Cognition's FrontierCode comes within 0.5 points of Astra at ~1/5 the cost. On raw benchmarks it's incremental: it lands #2, behind Anthropic's Fable 5.2. The story isn't the model; it's what shipped alongside it — Astra for Law and ChatGPT for Sales, named vertical products.
The tech — what's hard
The verticalisation. OpenAI is tuning and packaging the model for specific professional work — legal research across tax, M&A and litigation (62% fewer tokens, stronger than alternatives on 8 of 10 tasks) — rather than leaving that to an app layer on top. The hard thing it's absorbing is exactly the “encode one expert's workflow and earn their trust” problem that was supposed to be the startup's moat.
Where the idea came from
For a year the advice — YC's $15-Expert RFS, every “vertical AI” pitch — was: the labs own the model, you own the vertical (the workflow, the trust, the deliverable). Astra for Law and ChatGPT for Sales are OpenAI testing whether it can own the vertical too, from the model down.
The bear case
Labs are historically bad at verticals: they lack the distribution, the compliance surface, the hand-holding sales motion and the appetite to support a law firm's edge cases. Harvey (law) and Sierra (support) exist precisely because the model alone was never the product. The vertical founder's bet is that OpenAI's attention is too thin to go deep in any single one of them — true until it isn't.
What it means
The sharpest competitive read of the week. §1-Model ML and §0's fifteen-dollar-expert aren't dead — but they just got a condition attached: the defensible vertical is the one the platform can't be bothered to support well, where the moat is distribution, trust and messy edge cases, not model quality. If your idea's only advantage is “GPT, but pointed at lawyers,” OpenAI shipped that today. If it's the finished deliverable, the compliance, the specific buyer relationship — you're still safe. Watch which verticals OpenAI names next; those are the ones to avoid.

The Remake

one evidenced candidate · no idea without a linked complaint

Ship the Shopify reviews widget that's fast, theme-native and flat-priced — so merchants stop rage-quitting Loox and Yotpo over $375 surprise bills

The incumbent
Loox, Yotpo and Judge.me — the dominant product-reviews and photo-UGC apps on the Shopify App Store, heavily installed and mostly priced per order or per metered tier, so the bill climbs with your traffic. Well-rated on average, which is the point: the opening isn't a bad product, it's a structural grievance (price shape + theme fit) inside good ones.
The complaint
Real paying merchants, in their own words. A 2026 analysis of 175 negative reviews across five flagship Shopify apps (Klaviyo, Yotpo, Loox, Judge.me, Recharge) found the churn triggers are price and clunk: the median disputed bill was $375/mo, the worst single complaint $7,000, and 37% of the one-star reviewers had used the app for over a year — not trial tourists, customers who hit a pricing wall. On REVIEWS.io's own reviews page, a merchant writes the app is “very clunky with Shopify, and not all of the widgets work properly with every theme.”
Which remake
#4, different price shape — with taste. A clean, fast reviews widget that renders natively in any theme and charges a flat monthly fee instead of metering per order. The incumbents can't flip to flat pricing without torching their own revenue model, which is the whole opening: the thing they can't copy is the thing merchants are complaining about.
The buyer
Shopify merchants already paying $15–$500+/mo for Loox, Yotpo or Judge.me — a card already out, inside a marketplace that solves discovery for you. The felt pain is dated and specific: bills that balloon exactly as the store succeeds, and widgets that fight the theme and slow the page.
Why him
Taste and price-shape in a marketplace that hands you distribution — two of his edges in one place. A reviews widget that is quiet, fast and matches any theme is a design-judgment product the incumbents won't ship, and “flat price, no surprise bill” is a one-line story a merchant switches for and a filmmaker can tell cleanly.
First step
Doable in a day: pull the one-star reviews of Loox and Judge.me with the public Apify scraper, cluster the exact complaints (pricing tiers, theme breakage, page-speed), then build one thing — a star-rating + photo block as a Shopify theme app extension that drops into any theme and loads in under 50 ms. Win one widget, flat-priced; don't chase feature parity. (Kill-filter check: useful on first install, one store, zero network effects needed on day one; buyer = user, a merchant with budget and a dated grievance; runs unattended once placed; lives inside the Shopify App Store where buyers already shop. The honest risk: reviews is a crowded category and the incumbents' moat is the accumulated review corpus — migration friction, not day-one utility. So compete on price shape and theme-native speed, and ship an importer so switching is painless. Don't try to out-feature Yotpo; out-simple it.)

§6

Skill

day 40

Deploy one of your Claude Code projects to a real URL with a login — in an afternoon, on Val Town or Cloudflare Workers

~2–3 hrs · free tiers, no credit card · produces: a thing you built, living at a URL behind auth — not on localhost

§0-green turns on one lived fact: you build software with Claude Code and it dies on localhost. So fix that for exactly one project. Take the smallest useful thing you've made and deploy it somewhere that gives you a URL and a login without an AWS console — Val Town for a single-file app, or Cloudflare Workers for something with a backend. The point isn't the hosting; it's to feel where the real friction is — auth, secrets, letting a stranger actually use it — because that friction is the market YC's “Cloud for Small Software” is pointing at. You cannot judge that problem from the outside.

Then do the measuring that turns the chore into research: add a login (even a single shared password), put one secret in the right place instead of hard-coding it, and send the URL to one person in your hostel. Count the steps that annoyed you — the ten papercuts between “it runs” and “someone else can use it safely.” That list is a product spec for §0-green, written by the only person qualified to write it: the user.

Free resource: Val Town's docs and Cloudflare Workers' free tier — both take you from zero to a live, authenticated URL without a credit card, which is more than AWS can say. Do it once and “deploying small software is hard” stops being a slogan and becomes a thing you have opinions about.
