START THIS MONTHgreen · solo · no capital · no credentials

API providers announce their breaking changes instead of just applying them

The problem
When an API changes, every customer hand-edits their own codebase to keep up. The provider ships a changelog and a migration guide; thousands of downstream teams each do the same edit, badly, on their own time. The work is identical across all of them, and nobody does it for them.
Who says it's hard
Y Combinator's RFS, in a request written by a founder who worked with 50+ API vendors. And the premise got louder today, not quieter: Marginal Revolution's “The economics of agents” is Tyler Cowen — the Emergent Ventures grader — thinking out loud about what happens when software maintenance becomes something an agent does on your behalf. That is this problem's whole thesis, posted by the man who writes the cheques.
Why it's still open
Two years ago nobody would grant an external tool write access to their repo. Claude Code, Devin and Greptile normalised exactly that — and today's HN top thread, Codex CLI installing a persistent background daemon by default, is the same trust-boundary question playing out in public. The capability is here; the specific layer that connects an API provider to its customers' code and applies the change is not.
Who's already trying
Greptile, Ellipsis, Renovate — but Renovate only bumps dependency versions; none of them understand API semantics (“this field moved, this call now needs auth”). That gap is the product.
Your angle
Pure software, zero capital, and you already understand agentic code editing from the inside because you live in it daily. Narrow enough to prototype in a weekend — which is exactly what today's §6 skill is.
Research path
  1. Pick one API with frequent breaking changes (Stripe, Twilio) and read two years of changelogs — feel the repetition.
  2. Write the migration codemod for a single breaking change by hand (today's §6). You cannot automate work you have not done once yourself.
  3. Read Renovate's architecture docs — how an automated PR against a stranger's repo actually earns enough trust to get merged. That trust mechanism is the hard 80%.
NEEDS CAPITAL, A LAB AND A DECADEred as a company — but this is the bottleneck your own degree sits closest to

AI is running out of electricity and land, not chips

The problem
New data centres take years to approve and enough megawatts to run, and can still be killed by local opposition. The chips exist; the power and the land do not. Every frontier model shipped this year — including today's §1 Muse Wonder, trained at Meta's new Ohio plant — is now gated on a substation, not a GPU order.
Who says it's hard
YC's RFS (“Bringing Compute to the Oceans”), and today the wire priced it three ways at once: Anthropic in talks for a 500 MW, ~$6.3B data center in Osaka; JPMorgan and Goldman selling a $5B loan for AI startup Volta at ~11% and taking the rare step of capping their own exposure; and MIT researchers publishing on how to mitigate the environmental cost of data centres. When the lenders hedge, the risk is real.
Why it's still open
Permitting, capital intensity, and physics. None of it is software. The honest proof is in today's §1: Fervo's first utility-scale geothermal unit costs $7,000/kW against a gas plant's $2,157/kW — the clean answer exists and is still three times too expensive. And the human friction is real: Longreads ran “The People of Utah vs. Kevin O'Leary,” a whole town fighting a data centre (§W).
Who's already trying
Crusoe, Fervo (§1, live as of Sept 30), Mazama, Oklo — all capital-heavy, all years in. Google just committed to ~400 MW of Fervo's future output; this is a market where the buyers are hyperscalers and the sellers raise nine figures before first power.
Your angle
Red as a company — you will not out-capital Fervo. But this is the one red on the board where your degree's subject matter is the actual bottleneck: subsurface instrumentation, drilling data and sensing are underbuilt and sit one step from enhanced geothermal. Don't start a power company. Learn the vocabulary now, because the energy wedge is the rare place where your transcript is not dead weight. (This thread retires in early November unless it has earned its place — today, with a plant actually on the grid, it did.)
Research path
  1. Read the Fervo Cape Station writeup (§1) for the real hard parts in an engineer's words: horizontal drilling, fiber-optic sensing, cost-per-kW.
  2. Chip War (in your canon) for why compute siting is geopolitics, not just engineering.
  3. Follow one number for a month: the $/kW gap between Fervo and gas. When it closes, the whole thesis changes — and you will have been watching the right dial.

The defense door · hardware + AI + a contract you could not get blocked from

Four Stanford kids who launched weather balloons from a parking lot just won a US Air Force contract worth up to $49M

WindBorne Systems · founded 2019, Palo Alto · co-founders John Dean (CEO), Andrey Sushko (CTO), Kai Marshland, Paige Brown — all Stanford, all ex-SpaceX · ~700 balloons aloft · raised a $37M Series B (by Aug 2026) · new USAF contract up to $49M, $1.7M ordered so far · TechCrunch · windbornesystems.com

What they do
WindBorne flies ~700 autonomous weather balloons that stay aloft up to 60× longer than standard radiosondes, collecting atmospheric data from places nothing else measures — then feeds it into its own AI forecast model, WeatherMesh 6, which it says beats the European Centre's physics-based forecasts. Two revenue streams: selling the raw data, and selling the forecasts built on it. The Air Force deal is structured like cloud metering — the USAF pays only as it needs data, ordering specific regions at specific times.
The tech — and what's hard
A balloon is trivial; a balloon that steers without an engine is not. WindBorne navigates by changing altitude to catch different wind layers — and in August it became the first to fly an unpowered balloon into the eye of a typhoon, using its own deep-learning model to decide, in real time, which wind to ride. The hard part is the loop: the data trains the forecast, the forecast flies the balloon, the balloon gathers the data. Whoever closes that loop owns a dataset — the actual state of the atmosphere where no sensor has ever been — that Google DeepMind's weather models can only estimate.
The founders & where the idea came from
All four came out of Stanford's Student Space Initiative, building high-altitude balloons as a club project, and all four had done time at SpaceX. The access was the realisation hiding in plain sight: the same balloon rig that carries a student experiment to the stratosphere can carry a weather sensor across the planet at a fraction of what any aerospace prime would charge. They started launching in a Stanford parking lot. That is the whole origin — cheap hardware, aimed at a market that had assumed the hardware had to be expensive.
The bear case
The contract is up to $49M; only $1.7M is actually ordered, and pay-as-you-go means it may never fill. Frontier AI weather models from Google and government labs are getting better fast and don't need a physical fleet — WindBorne's moat is the proprietary data, and if model quality outruns the value of fresh observations, the balloons become a cost centre. One government customer is a concentration risk, not a business yet.
For you
This is the §0 “American defense” thread as a door you specifically can walk through: a hardware startup selling to the DoD, founded by undergrads, and you are US-eligible without the sponsorship math that blocks your peers. Two transferable lessons. One: the unfair advantage was a student club — proof of work beats a transcript, which is the whole shape of your situation. Two: they won by making expensive hardware cheap and selling the data, not the device. Your tiltrotor gathers data too; the question is what loop it can close that nobody else is standing inside.
The energy wedge · the §0-red, as a plant that is actually on the grid

An oil-field drilling engineer taught geothermal to frack — and just put the first utility-scale plant of its kind on the grid

Fervo Energy · founded 2017, Houston · co-founders Tim Latimer (CEO; ex-BHP drilling engineer), Jack Norbeck (CTO) — met doing graduate degrees at Stanford · Cape Station, Beaver County, Utah · 33 MW live Sept 30, 500 MW by 2028 · Google offtake ~400 MW · IEEE Spectrum · background

What they do
Fervo makes clean power that runs 24/7 by drilling into hot, dry rock — no natural steam reservoir required. Its Cape Station plant synchronised its first 33 MW unit to the grid on Sept 24 and began commercial sales on Sept 30 — the IEEE calls it the first time a utility-scale, next-gen geothermal facility has sold power to the grid anywhere. Two more 33 MW blocks are targeted for Jan 2027, on a path to 500 MW by 2028. Google has committed to ~400 MW of that output; Shell takes the first unit's.
The tech — and what's hard
Enhanced geothermal (EGS) borrows the exact playbook that unlocked shale: horizontal drilling and fiber-optic sensing, used to crack hot rock and circulate water through it to make steam. The hard part is cost: Fervo's first phase runs $7,000/kW, dropping to a projected $5,500/kW in phase two — still well above a gas plant's $2,157/kW (2025). The whole bet is a drilling-cost learning curve: do enough wells and the price falls the way solar's did. Unproven until it isn't.
Where the idea came from
Tim Latimer was a drilling engineer at BHP in the Eagle Ford shale around 2012, fighting the high rock temperatures that made wells hard to drill. Researching the problem, he stumbled on geothermal — “I had never heard of geothermal at that point in my life” — and realised the oil-field skills he already had were the missing piece. He left for a Stanford MBA/MS, met Norbeck there, and founded Fervo. The access was a transferable skill pointed at an adjacent industry nobody in it had the drilling chops to attack.
The bear case
Three-times-gas is a real number, not a rounding error, and the learning-curve bet is exactly the bet that has killed capital-heavy energy startups before. Drilling is dangerous and slow; one bad well is nine figures. Offtake from Google de-risks demand but not execution. And induced seismicity — fracking's ugliest side effect — is a permitting landmine every EGS project has to defuse town by town.
For you
This is the single most important item on today's page for you specifically, and the reason the §0-red energy thread earns another week. The lesson is not “go start a geothermal company” — it's Latimer's move: a skill from a dying/adjacent field, aimed at a problem the incumbents can't drill. Your degree is subsurface-adjacent; the exact thing that feels like a box to tick is the thing that let one engineer build a nine-figure company. Watch the $/kW gap, learn the vocabulary, and notice that the energy buildout is now the bottleneck under every AI story on this page.
The frontier race · now a four-horse field, and a reboot that worked

Meta shipped its first frontier model — proof that Alexandr Wang's expensive teardown of Meta AI actually produced something

Meta Superintelligence Labs · Muse Wonder & Muse Wonder Full, released Oct 12 · led by Alexandr Wang (Scale AI founder, restructured Meta AI from Aug 2025) · trained at Meta's Prometheus data center, Ohio · priced $4 / $20 per M input/output tokens · Meta blog · TechCrunch

What they do
Muse Wonder is Meta's first model it's willing to call frontier-class, built for agentic tasks — long-running, tool-using work rather than one-shot answers. On Artificial Analysis's index, Muse Wonder Full outperforms Fable 5.1, matches Sonnet 5.5, and lands just shy of Opus 5.5. Two headline features: Active Memory (carries context about you across conversations and retrieves what's relevant) and Active Focus (keeps working a hard goal — trying alternatives, pushing through blockers). It's priced at $4/$20 per million tokens, right in the Opus 5.5 / GPT-6 Sol band.
The tech — and what's hard
The interesting tell is the split: Muse Wonder ships on the Meta Model API; Muse Wonder Full does not — it's reserved for Muse Code subscribers and never served raw. That's a company that learned from Llama being copied and is now gating its best weights behind a product. The agentic features (persistent memory, self-directed focus) are the same capability surface that makes today's §0-green audit problem urgent: the better a model is at acting on its own over time, the more it matters what it touched.
Where the idea came from
This is a reboot story, not a research story. Zuckerberg was reportedly unhappy with Llama 4, hired Alexandr Wang in mid-2025, and let him tear down and rebuild the AI org — an expensive, publicly doubted bet. Muse Wonder is the first evidence it produced a frontier model rather than just a reorg. The access being bought here was talent concentration and compute: a new Ohio data center and a rebuilt team, not a clever idea.
The bear case
“Matches Sonnet 5.5, just shy of Opus 5.5” means Meta spent a fortune to arrive at the frontier, not ahead of it — parity, a year and billions later. Gating the best model behind a subscription cuts off the open-weights goodwill that made Llama matter. And “personal superintelligence” is a slogan, not a moat; the features (memory, focus) are table stakes every lab is shipping this quarter.
For you
Two things. First, practically: there is now a fourth frontier model at commodity pricing, and the one real edge a bootstrapped builder has is knowing how to drive these tools — which today's HN also argued twice (“AI is a tool and using it well takes skill”). The models commoditise; the skill of wielding them does not. Second, as a founder study: Wang's reboot is a management case in whether you can buy your way back to the frontier with talent and capital. The answer today is “to parity, yes.” Worth filing against every “we're behind” narrative you'll hear.

Skill

day 15

Write one API migration by hand — a codemod for a single breaking change — and you're standing inside today's §0-green

~2–3 hours · free · produces: a working codemod, and a felt map of exactly where “apply the API change for me” is still unbuilt

Today's §0-green is “API providers announce their breaking changes instead of applying them,” and the research path says you cannot automate work you haven't done once by hand. So do it once. Pick one API with a documented breaking change — Stripe's upgrade log is the cleanest, Twilio's is close — and take a small repo that uses the old version (one of your own Claude Code projects is perfect). Now write a codemod: a script that mechanically rewrites the old call pattern into the new one. Use a tool like ast-grep or jscodeshift so you're transforming the syntax tree, not doing string replacement — because the moment you try, you'll hit the thing that makes this a real problem: the change isn't always mechanical. A moved field is easy; a call that now needs auth, or a parameter whose meaning changed, is not.

That wall is the entire startup. The easy 60% is a codemod anyone can write; the hard 40% — understanding API semantics well enough to rewrite code safely, and earning enough trust to open a PR against a stranger's repo — is what Greptile and Renovate haven't cracked for API changes. Read Renovate's architecture docs after you've felt the work: they're the state of the art at “automated PR a human will actually merge,” and you'll read them differently once you know why that trust is the product. Build the ugly single-API version first.

Free resources: ast-grep (tree-aware find-and-rewrite, gentle learning curve), Stripe's API upgrade log (two years of real breaking changes to practice on), and Renovate's docs (how automated PRs earn trust). Start with one change on one repo; reach for the AST tooling the moment string replacement lies to you.

The exploration arm — off-wire feeds all returned today, so this is the real thing: a wilderness disappearance, a town that beat a billionaire, a brand-new shape, the worst way to reach space, and a medieval surgeon's scroll. None of it is curated toward what you already like; that's the point. One line on why it caught my eye.

  • He went out on an epic wilderness adventure. Only his dogs came back. Longreads
    A disappearance in the wild, reconstructed from what the dogs did and didn't do. A gripping piece of narrative nonfiction about how we try to read a story from the only witnesses who can't talk — and how little we can actually know.
  • The people of Utah vs. Kevin O'Leary Longreads
    A whole town organising to stop a billionaire's data centre. The human face of today's §0-red: the compute buildout isn't blocked by physics alone, it's blocked by people who don't want it next door — and the fight is messier, and more decisive, than any megawatt figure suggests.
  • A unique shape — a 3D “einstein” tile — has been discovered Scientific American
    Mathematicians found a single 3D shape that tiles space without ever repeating — the three-dimensional cousin of the famous flat “hat” tile. New geometry, found in 2026, with no practical purpose yet and a strange beauty. Proof the basic shapes aren't all discovered.
  • Mosquitoes are a choice Works in Progress
    An argument that we could simply eradicate the mosquito species that kill hundreds of thousands of people a year — the tech exists — and mostly choose not to. A bracing essay about ambition, caution, and how often “impossible” is really “nobody decided to.”
  • Red Sea conflict is forcing cloud giants to reroute the internet Rest of World
    The internet is physical cables on a seabed, and a conflict in the Red Sea is making Google and Meta lay new routes to avoid them. The invisible geography of the thing you use all day — and a reminder that “the cloud” has chokepoints a missile can reach.
  • The worst way to get to space Kottke
    A little-told story of one of the most absurd, dangerous schemes ever proposed for reaching orbit. Here for the engineering-history delight — the catalogue of what people seriously tried before the rocket won is its own education in why the surviving designs look the way they do.
  • Anatomy unzipped: John of Arderne's surgical scroll (ca. 1425) Public Domain Review
    A 15th-century English surgeon's illustrated scroll — a how-to for medieval operations, drawn with unsettling confidence centuries before anaesthesia. Documentation as a visual object, and a window into how knowledge got passed down when a diagram was the only interface a practitioner had.

Deals

1 free resource worth your time

Coursera's entire catalogue, audited for free — the undocumented button on the most useful courses

Free (audit mode) · certificate costs money, the learning doesn't

The only clean deals signal today came from Class Central, whose guide to auditing Coursera courses for free is the genuinely useful thing: most Coursera courses let you audit the full material at no cost — you only pay for the certificate and graded assignments. For someone building proof-of-work rather than collecting transcripts, that's the whole value anyway. Pair it with their curated lists of the best AI and product-management courses if you want a starting point — but the move is: audit the material, skip the certificate, ship the thing you learned.

Honest note: §8 still has no standing deals source of its own — it needs the GitHub Student Pack, cloud/AI credit programmes and startup-tool offers wired into collection before it earns a daily slot. Today it's a pointer to the most useful free thing the feeds surfaced, which beats a coupon.

Also

filed, not featured
  • The economics Nobel goes to Robert Hall and Michael Woodford. Two macroeconomists share the 2026 prize — Woodford for the theory of how central banks steer expectations, Hall for work on labour markets and the firm. Filed as literacy: these are the frameworks the people pricing the AI buildout (and the $5B Volta loan in §2) are implicitly using. Worth knowing whose ideas sit under “the market expects.”
  • Anthropic researchers say small AI models may be months from self-replicating on IoT devices. The claim: small models could soon copy themselves onto smart ovens and off-brand Android TVs. Half alarm, half capability note — but read it next to today's §0-green and the Codex-daemon thread (§4): “what is this software doing on my hardware, and did I authorise it?” is becoming the defining question of the agent era.
  • DARPA's Vertical Lift Challenger was won — by a helicopter. The VTOL prize the §0 board has tracked finally has a winner, and it's a conventional rotorcraft, not the exotic new configuration everyone expected. A sharp lesson for your tiltrotor: judges reward what works and lands safely, not what's novel. The unsolved parts of the challenge are still your opening; the boring winner is the warning.
  • Leading Chinese mathematicians launch a 1,000-scientist alliance for specialized AI math models. Eight organisations, aiming at 1,000+ researchers, explicitly building AI for mathematics. Read it next to §3's Quanta piece: AI-accelerated math is now a coordinated national effort, not a lab curiosity — which is the exact dynamic the §0 crypto-agility problem is watching. State-scale attention is the signal.
  • Margaret Hamilton, who led the Apollo flight software, has died at 90. She coined “software engineering” and wrote the code that kept Apollo 11 from aborting its landing. Filed as the counterweight to today's model-hype: the most consequential software ever shipped was written by a small team who understood, line by line, exactly what it would do under load. The opposite of a self-updating daemon you can't audit.
