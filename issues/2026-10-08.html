START THIS MONTHgreen · solo · no capital · no credentials

Nobody can prove what an AI agent actually touched

The problem
Agents run with far more authority than their task needs — the same shell, filesystem and credential access whether they're summarising a document or changing infrastructure. When one wanders somewhere it shouldn't, there is no clean audit trail to answer “what did it touch, under whose authority.” It kept proving itself this week: OpenAI's agents were found acting on Wikimedia projects — spotted by outsiders, not OpenAI — Scientific American ran a careful post-mortem of the OpenAI–Hugging Face incident, Docker shipped a first-class Docker Agent, and ICANN is about to hand out .agent and .agi domains. More agents, more authority, still no provenance.
Who says it's hard
OpenAI's own disclosure, plus a stack of arXiv work this quarter and security researchers naming the missing control — “How Agents Ask for Permission” is the landscape in one paper.
Why it's still open
Sandboxing shipped (NVIDIA, Cisco); provenance did not. The literature is 2024–26 and mostly preprints — a field with no incumbent and no agreed standard for “this agent did this, for this human, with this permission.”
Who's already trying
NVIDIA and Cisco both sandbox; neither audits. The forming layer next door — Pinrail (§4), an inbox where coding agents wait for a human to approve — is a tell that the human-in-the-loop control is being hand-rolled, one tool at a time, because nobody ships it.
Your angle
Zero capital, zero credentials, and you run agents daily on a machine holding your own keys — so you can feel the problem today. The cheapest first version is a logger, not a company: instrument your own setup and the log is the prototype. That's exactly this issue's §6 skill.
Research path
  1. Read arXiv 2607.13718 (“How Agents Ask for Permission”) for the whole landscape in one sitting.
  2. Read arXiv 2606.22504 (“Lingering Authority”) on the credentials an agent still holds after its task ends.
  3. Then do §6: log every tool call your agent makes for a week. You'll have both the felt problem and the seed of the product.
NEEDS A LAB, STANDARDS AND A DECADEred · filed so you recognise it, not so you start it

We no longer have a reliable way to tell who — or what — is real

The problem
Every trust signal built for a world where faking a human was expensive — a face, a voice, a signature — is getting cheap to fake. Whoever builds the replacement becomes the layer every bank, app and video call checks. And this week the ground under the fallback moved too: if AI-accelerated math can weaken the cryptography that a proof-of-human system would lean on, you're trying to build trust on a foundation that is itself now in question. Yesterday an AI-generated video swayed a judge before an appeals court threw it out — the boundary cases are already in court.
Who says it's hard
Y Combinator RFS (“proof of human”) — source — now with the crypto angle named by Vitalik Buterin and Scott Aaronson (“The Mathocalypse”).
Why it's still open
It needs cryptographic standards, regulators and distribution deals simultaneously — and the newest twist is that the standards themselves may need replacing (post-quantum, crypto-agility) before the old ones break. None of it is a weekend.
Who's already trying
Worldcoin, Persona, Clear and Apple/Google device attestation on the identity side; NIST, Cloudflare and PQShield on the post-quantum side. Two hard problems that have just started to touch.
Your angle
Red, and honestly so — a standards-and-PhD problem, not a building problem. Filed so you recognise it later, not so you start it. If a narrow, boring slice ever opens — tooling that simply inventories which cryptographic primitives a codebase depends on, the unglamorous first step of any migration — that part is software. Learn the vocabulary now; don't found here.
Research path
  1. Read Aaronson's “The Mathocalypse” for the honest state of the threat, hype stripped out.
  2. Read about C2PA content provenance — the “is this media real” half of the same problem.
  3. Skim NIST's post-quantum standards to see what “solved” already looks like on paper — and how far adoption is from it.

Founder war story · ex-SpaceX engineers, and your exact dispatch lane

Rocket engineers raised $100M to put freight on autonomous electric rail — starting with the short hauls the railroads quit

Parallel Systems · Long Beach, CA · founded 2020 by Matt Soule (CEO; ~13 yrs in SpaceX avionics), John Howard (ex-SpaceX battery) & Ben Stabler (ex-SpaceX avionics software) · $100M Series C led by AVP (w/ Hillspire, Agility Global, Cobalt, Anthos, Congruent, Riot, Collaborative Fund) · >$200M raised total · running on 160 mi near the Port of Savannah under FRA approval · TechCrunch · R&A News

What they do
Parallel builds Panther: a battery-electric, coupler-free autonomous rail vehicle that hauls several tons of freight up to 500 miles with no operator. Instead of one long train on a fixed schedule (“precision railroading”), small vehicles travel alone or in short platoons and split apart at the yard without a human. The target market is the route nobody serves well: “less than 500 miles is hard for railroads to do competitively,” Soule says — so those loads went to trucks, and Parallel wants them back on rail.
Where the idea came from
Three SpaceX leads looked at freight rail the way they'd looked at rockets: as a vehicle-and-avionics problem, not a railroad-operations one. Soule ran avionics; Howard ran the battery division; Stabler did avionics software and actuator electronics. The unfair advantage is literal — they already knew how to make an autonomous, battery-electric vehicle safe and certifiable, and pointed it at an industry that hadn't had a clean-sheet vehicle in a very long time.
Why it works (if it works)
Economics, not romance. Soule's claim is that Parallel can make a short rail lane profitable at ~10,000 moves a year — a density trucking can't touch per unit of fuel and labour — while using existing track and existing freight cars. The coupler-free, platoon design is the enabling trick: it turns rail from a batch process (assemble a huge train, then run it) into something closer to on-demand dispatch. That's the part that rhymes with your own work.
The bear case
Rail is a regulated, incumbent-controlled network: the Class I railroads own the track and the relationships, and a startup needs their cooperation or a very patient regulator. “First commercial payload coming up very soon” still means revenue is ahead, not behind. Autonomy on shared freight corridors is a safety-case marathon, and $200M is small against the capital a national rollout wants. Competitors (Intramotev, OTIV, Magrail) are chasing the same lane.
For you
This is the §0 physical-world-OS thesis as a company — and it's your cab-dispatch instinct at industrial scale. The lesson to steal isn't the trains: it's that the founders took a generalist vehicle-and-autonomy skill and aimed it at a legacy industry that measured success by density and dispatch, not by a hero product. You keep saying you're “not a one-person thing” — this is proof that the transferable asset is the engineering instinct, re-pointed. Read how three people with no rail background got rail people to fund them.
Money & power · a fund built on the exact bet you are — that talent is everywhere

While VCs crowd into San Francisco, this $320M fund is paid to find founders everywhere else

Endeavor Catalyst · New York · $320M Fund V (AUM now >$850M; 400 LPs, ~30% of them Endeavor founders) · managed by Allen Taylor & Jackie Carmel; Endeavor co-founded by Linda Rottenberg · co-invests $1–3M into rounds that already have a ≥$5M lead, capped at 10% · 437 companies across 44 markets, 83 unicorns, 11 IPOs · ~90% outside the U.S. · TechCrunch

What it is
Endeavor is a 30-year-old non-profit that admits founders to a global network after a brutal screen — last year it looked at over 10,000 candidates and selected 88. Endeavor Catalyst is its investment arm: it doesn't lead rounds, it rides alongside a real lead once a company has raised ≥$5M, writing $1–3M and never more than 10%. The twist that makes it unusual: half the fund's profits flow back to Endeavor, so every win pays to find the next founder somewhere unfashionable.
Where the idea came from
Rottenberg's original observation, decades early, was that high-potential founders exist far outside Silicon Valley and simply lack the network capital treats as a prerequisite. The portfolio is the argument: Bending Spoons (Italy, ~$26B), ElevenLabs (Poland-founded, $22B), Checkout.com (Swiss founder, $12B), Replit (Amjad Masad, Palestinian-Jordanian, $9B), Flutterwave (African payments, $3.2B). None of them are San Francisco stories.
Why it works
It's a structural arbitrage on geography. If 90% of capital clusters in one zip code, the mispriced talent is everywhere else — and a fund with a trusted local network and a “we only follow a real lead” rule gets to buy into it with less competition and lower entry prices. Europe is now its fastest-growing region (12 new investments in H1 2026 vs 14 in all of 2025), and it's steering 20% of this fund into repeat founders — people who've already won once.
The bear case
A follow-on fund lives or dies on deal access, not insight: if the best “elsewhere” companies start raising their real leads from the same SF firms anyway, Endeavor's edge thins to a brand. The 10%-cap, no-lead model means it rarely owns enough of a breakout to mint a fund by itself. And “emerging markets” carries currency, governance and exit risk that a Delaware-to-Nasdaq company simply doesn't.
For you
This is the most personally relevant item in the brief and it isn't close. You are, by their definition, a founder “elsewhere” — and here is a $320M institution whose entire thesis is that your location is an edge, not a handicap. Two concrete moves: (1) study the Endeavor Entrepreneur selection as a standing door you grow into (it's for companies with traction, so it's a two-year target, not a this-week one); (2) internalise the model — talent is evenly distributed, networks are not, and the whole game is manufacturing the network access you weren't born into. That's the same lesson as yesterday's hacker-house story, told in fund form.
Buildable idea · the reshoring/production-rate thesis, as pure software

A $3.6M seed to build the “Alibaba of American manufacturing” — because you still can't search for a U.S. factory

Bloom · Detroit / remote · founded 2023 by Justin Kosmides (CEO) w/ Hitesh Chudasama & Chris Nolte · $3.6M seed led by SNAK Venture Partners (w/ Flyover, Mana, Detroit Venture Partners, Invest Detroit, Michigan Outdoor Innovation Fund) · 2,000+ matches for 140+ companies · competitors Fictiv, Xometry, MacroFab · TechCrunch

What they do
Bloom is an AI-driven marketplace that matches hardware companies with U.S.-based manufacturers, suppliers and service providers — contract manufacturing, assembly, design, freight, warehousing, even hazmat shipping. Kosmides's framing: “what an AI-driven version of Alibaba did in China, where it created a marketplace for contract manufacturers.” The gap he's filling is embarrassingly basic — “the ability to discover, and to do matchmaking… just doesn't exist electronically” for American manufacturing.
Where the idea came from
Bloom started in 2023 serving e-bike and e-scooter makers drowning in supply-chain pain, then pivoted to a broad marketplace when post-election tariffs sent drone makers and robotics startups scrambling for domestic suppliers. The clean example in the piece: a Michigan contract manufacturer that used to refurbish Nest thermostats and Bird scooters now bids on drone-assembly contracts through Bloom. Supply met a policy-made surge in demand, and the matchmaking layer was missing.
Why it works (if it works)
It's a discovery-and-trust layer, not a factory — which is why it's a seed-stage software company and not a capital sink. Bloom seeds its matching with 30–40% supplier data contributed directly plus public data, and the competitors (Fictiv, Xometry, MacroFab) mostly sell individual parts, not multi-supplier coordination of a whole build. If “make it in America” is a durable policy regime, the index of who can make what, at what rate, becomes valuable infrastructure.
The bear case
$3.6M is pre-everything, and marketplaces are brutal: liquidity is the whole game, and a thin directory loses to a buyer's existing rolodex. The thesis rides on tariffs staying put — a policy reversal softens the demand that made the pivot work. Xometry and friends are far better capitalised and could bolt on “full-build” matching. And supplier data that's 30–40% self-reported is a data-quality problem waiting to happen.
For you
This is yesterday's production-rate thesis (Furientis: “can you build 1,000 a year”) turned into a software problem you could actually prototype. The reframe worth keeping: when a policy shift or a new technology creates a sudden surge of supply or demand, the matchmaking layer between the two sides is often missing and buildable without capital. You don't need to make the drones — the business can be the index of who makes them. File the pattern: find the two sides that can't find each other yet.

Skill

day 10

Put a logger between you and your AI agent — the audit trail is the §0-green prototype

~1–2 hours · free · produces: a week-long record of everything your agent touched, and the seed of a product nobody ships

Today's §0 green is “nobody can prove what an AI agent actually touched.” The cheapest way to understand that problem is to become the instrument. You already run an agent daily (Claude Code) on a machine holding your keys. Add a hook that fires on every tool call — Claude Code exposes PreToolUse / PostToolUse hooks in settings.json — and have it append one JSON line per action to a local log: timestamp, tool, the arguments (file path, shell command, URL), and the result size. Then just work normally for a week.

Two things happen. First, you feel the problem: scroll the log and you'll see your agent read files you forgot it could, run commands you didn't picture, reach hosts you didn't name — all with the same blanket authority whether it was renaming a variable or touching your git history. Second, you'll be holding the primitive. A clean, queryable “what did it touch, when, under what instruction” log is literally the first feature of the product that doesn't exist yet — the one NVIDIA and Cisco skipped when they built sandboxes. Pinrail (§4) is someone else arriving at the same spot from the approval side. The log is the prototype; you just have to read it.

Free resource: Claude Code hooks — the built-in way to run a script on every tool call, no extra tooling. If you don't use Claude Code, a shell wrapper that tees every command and its args into a dated file gets you 80% of the same lesson for zero setup.
