Paperwork · the most expensive document you will ever skim

A man was owed a billion dollars in Nvidia stock, and lost it to one ambiguous sentence about vesting

Eric Gullichsen · Nvidia Technical Advisory Board, 1993 · 25,000 options granted · 15,625 vested and exercised · 9,375 disputed → 4,500,000 shares after 480× splits → ~$1B · HN 618 · primary account

What happened
In 1993, after a meeting on a houseboat in Sausalito with Jensen Huang, Curtis Priem and Chris Malachowsky, Gullichsen joined Nvidia's Technical Advisory Board and was granted 25,000 options. The agreement said the shares vest “in a series of quarterly installments so that all shares shall vest upon the expiration of one year from Grant Date.”
The dispute
Nvidia's CFO and its outside counsel at Cooley asserted the options vested over four years. Under that reading only 15,625 shares (62.5%) had vested by 1996, which he exercised — and then forgot about for nearly thirty years. The remaining 9,375 were treated as never vested. Read as written, the agreement says one year. Read as asserted, four.
What it cost
Those 9,375 shares, through 480× cumulative splits, would be 4.5 million shares today — roughly a billion dollars. He reviewed his original documents decades later, spotted the discrepancy, and spent a year in correspondence with Nvidia's counsel. They declined, citing the statute of limitations. His own attorneys concluded a thirty-year delay would not survive a motion to dismiss.
The bear case
One side of a dispute, told thirty years later. “Quarterly installments over one year” for an advisory grant was unusual then and is unusual now, so a drafting error is at least as likely as a deliberate reinterpretation. And the statute of limitations exists for exactly this reason — evidence and memory rot. The lesson survives either reading.
For you
This is the sequel to Issue 001's Anthropic dual-class story, and it is the same lesson from the other end of the telescope. Anthropic's founders are spending real effort to get the paperwork right before the money exists. Gullichsen didn't, when it would have cost him one uncomfortable phone call in 1996. Equity is decided by documents, early, and the window to argue closes while the stake is still worth nothing. When someone hands you an agreement — a cofounder split, an advisory grant, your first hire's options — the cheapest moment to read it carefully is the moment it seems least worth the trouble.
Inference economics · a whole category forming in public

Ember-1 does what Kimi K3 does using about 40% fewer tokens — efficiency is now the product

Fireworks AI · post-trained from Moonshot's open-weight Kimi K3 · ~40% fewer reasoning tokens at comparable quality · Research Preview on serverless, two-week access · HN 446 · announcement

What it is
Fireworks took Moonshot AI's open-weight Kimi K3 and post-trained it to produce shorter reasoning traces while holding task accuracy. Not a dial that reduces effort at inference time — the model learned which thinking was load-bearing and which was ceremony.
Why it matters
In agentic coding, reasoning tokens are most of the bill. Cutting them ~40% at equal quality roughly halves the cost of the same work. That is not a research result, it is a margin, and margins are businesses.
The pattern
Note who did this. Fireworks did not train a frontier model — they took someone else's open weights and made them cheaper to run. That is a viable company built entirely downstream of a model they did not pay to create. Open weights turn frontier labs' capital expenditure into everyone else's raw material.
Corroboration
Today's arXiv sweep carried the same theme independently, which is the signal worth trusting: Learning to Stop without Learning to Stop (self-supervised confidence training for reasoning efficiency), Compact Documentation for Coding Agents, Weight Pair Encoding, EAServe: Encode-Aware Disaggregated Serving. Four papers, one weekend, all about spending fewer tokens rather than achieving more. The research field and the product field have converged on cost at the same moment.
For you
The arXiv-to-product gap this brief watches for is unusually short here — weeks, not the usual eighteen months. When the papers and the launches arrive in the same week, the window is already open and closing. Anything that measurably reduces the cost of running agents has a buyer today, and the buyer is every company currently surprised by their inference bill.
Agentic commerce · and it is being tested where you live

Google is putting a Flipkart buy button inside Gemini — checkout is moving into the assistant

Pilot live in India · smartphones, electronics, mobile accessories · wider rollout reported for October, before the festive season · Google invested ~$350M in Flipkart in 2024 · TechCrunch

What happened
A limited set of users now see a Buy button on Flipkart listings surfaced inside Gemini and AI Mode in Search. Tapping it opens a Flipkart-branded checkout without leaving the AI interface. It sits inside Google's Universal Commerce Protocol work — an open standard for agents to move through a shopping journey including checkout — though Google has not confirmed UCP powers this specific pilot.
The real move
The search box has spent twenty-five years sending you somewhere else to transact. This keeps you. If the assistant becomes where purchases complete rather than where they begin, then the merchant's relationship with the customer is mediated by whoever owns the assistant — and the retailer becomes fulfilment.
Why India first
Not charity. India is Google's largest user base, Flipkart is a $350M related party, and the festive season is the year's demand peak. It is the right place to test a checkout flow at scale, cheaply, before it ships where margins are thinner and regulators are louder.
The bear case
Pilots like this die routinely. A handful of users and three product categories is not a rollout, trust in AI-initiated payments is unproven, and Indian payment rails plus UPI habits are not trivially wrapped by a foreign assistant layer. Watch whether October actually happens.
For you
You are building a booking funnel. This is the question it forces: where does the transaction complete, and who owns that surface? A product whose only advantage is a nicer checkout is renting a position that assistants are actively trying to take. A product whose advantage is something an assistant cannot do — hold real-world supply, match riders into a shared cab, know that the 6am Kolkata run is full — is not competing for that surface at all. Own the supply and the matching, not the form.

day 2

Sell it before you build it

~3 hours · free · builds: distribution, and proof the thing is wanted

Issue 001 was cold email — getting a stranger to reply. This is the next rep: getting a stranger to commit before the product exists. Today's r/startups thread is founders describing exactly how they did it, and the answers are more mundane than you'd expect.

The method: describe the thing precisely enough that someone can say yes or no, put a price on it, and ask for a commitment that costs them something — a deposit, a signed intent, a calendar slot. Not “would you use this?”, which everyone answers yes to and which teaches you nothing. Ten conversations is enough to know.

If you have something you're considering building, this is the week to find out whether anyone wants it — before you spend three months finding out the expensive way. Presales are also the only form of validation that arrives with money attached.
