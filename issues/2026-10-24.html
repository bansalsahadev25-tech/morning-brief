START THIS MONTHgreen · solo · no capital · no credentials

Deploying the software you already build is still hard — and agents made it urgent

The problem
Agents made it trivial to build software that will only ever have one or a handful of users — a tool for your team, a tracker for your own workflow, the thing you spun up in Claude Code last night. Deploying and sharing it is as hard as it was in 2015. AWS and Azure were built for software that scales to millions, and that complexity is now the bottleneck for software that will never need it.
Who says it's hard
Y Combinator's RFS names it outright (“A Cloud for Small Software”). And the wire priced the agent half of it this morning: Daytona hit a $1B valuation for “giving every agent its own computer” (§1). That is the machine an agent runs on — the layer that takes what the agent built and gives a human a URL, with auth, is still unclaimed.
Why it's still open
The hard parts are not the hosting. They are auth and permissions for arbitrary user-written code, per-person environment customisation, and letting a non-technical person share something executable without it becoming a security incident. Everyone underestimates the third one — and it just got worse, because the code is now written by an agent nobody audited.
Who's already trying
Vercel, Replit, Val Town, Cloudflare Workers, Deno Deploy — and now the agent-native layer (E2B, Modal, Daytona itself). All of them solve “run the code.” None has made “a normal person ships a private app to three colleagues” a one-click, trustworthy act.
Your angle
You already live inside this problem: you build with Claude Code and then have nowhere good to put the result. That is the founder's unfair advantage in its cheapest form — you are the user. §6 turns it into something you can ship this weekend.
Research path
  1. Build the ugliest possible version for yourself: one command that takes a Claude Code project and returns a live URL with login.
  2. Read Val Town's public writeups on sandboxing user-written code — they have hit every wall you will hit, and documented it.
  3. Then ask ten people in your hostel who build things where their projects currently live. The answer is “localhost,” and that is the market.

The U.S. Army asked founders, by name, to rebuild how it fights

The problem
YC's Fall 2026 RFS carries a category written by the sitting U.S. Secretary of the Army: “We need cutting-edge drones, resilient logistics, and advanced manufacturing.” The old defense model — a few primes, ten-year procurement, exquisite hardware — cannot produce the cheap, attritable, autonomous systems modern conflict actually consumes. The gap between what the Pentagon buys and what it needs is now a funded request.
Who says it's hard
Y Combinator, Requests for Startups — “The Future of American Defense,” authored by Daniel P. Driscoll, U.S. Secretary of the Army. And note where today's wire points: the public markets are now pricing small-nation defense hardware. Tonbo Imaging, a defense-optics firm, closed +66% on its India debut; Garuda Aerospace (§1), a profitable drone-maker partnered with three defense PSUs, is next. The buyers are forming a market in real time.
Why it's still open
The hard parts are not the drone. They are ITAR, clearance, capital intensity, and a procurement system built to reject newcomers. Anduril spent years and billions proving a startup could sell to the DoD at all. But the autonomy and coordination software is moving faster than the hardware and the paperwork — and that is where a small team lives.
Who's already trying
Anduril, Shield AI, Skydio, Neros, Saronic in the US; Garuda, ideaForge and Tonbo in India — most post-seed, most capital-heavy. The autonomy layer is the least crowded.
Your angle
Amber, not red, for one specific reason: you are US-eligible without sponsorship — the single barrier that blocks most of your peers from this entire market — and you already design and fly a two-rotor VTOL aircraft. The company is red (clearance, capital, ITAR). The on-ramp is not: autonomy software, swarm coordination in simulation, and a drone you can already demonstrate. Reframe the tiltrotor build log as a defense-autonomy demo and you are one domain from a problem a cabinet secretary signed.
Research path
  1. Read “The Future of American Defense” on the YC RFS page — a funded problem statement signed by the buyer.
  2. Study what Anduril's Lattice and Neros actually ship to see the real bar, then ask what your tiltrotor can sense, carry, or coordinate that clears a sliver of it.
  3. Apply to one US defense-tech internship or hackathon this cycle — the OpenCV and agentic-AI contests in §5 are the cheapest on-ramps. You qualify, and most applicants don't.

New on the feed today: nothing to add to the board. DARPA, IFP and Marginal Revolution all moved, but produced award topics and essays, not a new funded problem with a door attached. The IFP piece on a proposed $100k STEM-OPT fee is worth a read for one reason: it would price international graduates out of staying in the US — which makes your own no-sponsorship eligibility a larger, not smaller, edge. When YC's RFS changes or a grade here gets solved, that becomes a headline of its own.

Agent infrastructure · the pick-and-shovel layer, priced

Daytona is worth $1B for selling agents their own computers — two years after it sold something else

$60M Series B · $1B valuation · total raised $91M · led by FirstMark · angels: Collison, Lütke, Srinivas, Yuan, Pomel, D'Angelo, Foody · TechCrunch · Latent Space interview

What they do
Daytona rents computers to software agents. When an agent needs to run code it didn't write — compile something, test a change, browse a site — it spins up a sandboxed machine (Linux, Windows, macOS, or GPU), does the work in isolation, and throws it away. CEO Ivan Burazin's line: “giving every agent its own computer.” The twist agents need that normal cloud doesn't give: the ability to fork a running machine into parallel branches and snapshot mid-execution so state survives a crash.
The tech
The hard part is startup latency and density. A human developer waits ten seconds for a cloud box; an agent spawning thousands of short, stateful jobs cannot. Daytona runs on bare metal with a custom scheduler and claims 60ms single-sandbox startup and ~50,000 concurrent sandboxes in about 75 seconds. It is also the only major option that is open-source and self-hostable — the others (E2B on Firecracker microVMs, Modal for GPU) are managed-only.
The founders
Ivan Burazin (CEO), Goran Draganić and Vedran Jukić — a Croatian team that ran Shift, one of Europe's larger developer conferences, for a decade before this. That is the unfair advantage hiding in plain sight: they spent ten years as the people developers came to, so they saw the workflow break before anyone else did.
Where it came from
Daytona began as a tool that managed human developer environments. In early 2025 Burazin pivoted the whole company when he noticed the customer changing underneath him: the thing asking for a clean, disposable computer was no longer a person, it was an agent. The pivot is the lesson — he didn't invent a market, he watched his existing one mutate and moved before the incumbents noticed.
The bear case
“A computer for every agent” is infrastructure, and infrastructure commoditises. E2B, Modal, Fly, Cloudflare and the frontier labs themselves can all offer a sandbox; the moat is latency, density and developer love, all of which can be out-spent. $1B on $91M raised is a bet on the category exploding, not on today's revenue, which was not disclosed. If agents plateau, this is a dev-tool business wearing a frontier-infra valuation.
What it means
Two things. First, this is your §0-green problem with a billion-dollar price tag on half of it — Daytona runs the agent's machine; nobody owns the step that takes what the agent built and hands a human a live, authed URL. Second, read the founder shape: a decade of being close to developers, then a nerve to pivot the entire company the moment the user changed species. You are closer to this user than any VC on that cap table.
Drones × your lane · the story San Francisco isn't telling

India's biggest drone-maker is profitable and heading public — built by a swimmer, backed by Dhoni

₹25.9 Cr net profit (FY26, +41%) · ₹206 Cr revenue (+67%) · IPO ~₹1,000 Cr (SEBI-cleared) · Chennai · Inc42 · Wikipedia

What they do
Garuda Aerospace makes drones and sells drone services — the split is almost even (₹99 Cr of hardware, ₹107 Cr of service in FY26). Agriculture spraying (the “Kisan” drones), surveillance, mapping, and increasingly defense. It calls itself India's largest drone manufacturer, 3,000+ drones built, and says it is the first Indian drone company partnered with three defense public-sector units.
The tech
Not frontier — and that's the point. The hard problem Garuda solved is not autonomy, it is manufacturing a drone cheaply enough, in India, to clear a government procurement at a margin. The defense push is newer: it showed R&D to the Army Design Bureau in September, has DRDO support, and launched a “Made in India” stealth drone, Vajra. Service revenue is the tell — recurring, not one-off hardware sales.
The founders
Agnishwar Jayaprakash — a former competitive swimmer turned founder, not an aerospace engineer. The unfair advantage was distribution and narrative: he raised ~$44M including a 1.1% stake from cricketer MS Dhoni, whose name opens doors (and rural-market trust) no cap table can buy in India.
Where it came from
Garuda rode a policy wave: India banned most drone imports and stood up a production-linked incentive scheme, and Modi's government pushed drones for agriculture and last-mile delivery. Jayaprakash positioned a domestic manufacturer exactly as the state decided it wanted one. The access wasn't technical — it was reading where policy money was about to flow and being the Indian flag already planted there.
The bear case
Expenses grew faster than revenue (total costs +73% to ₹172 Cr; raw materials +62%), so margins are thin and import-dependent for components. A ₹4,000–5,000 Cr valuation on ₹26 Cr of profit is a ~150× earnings bet on the India-drone story holding. Government-dependent revenue moves at government speed and reverses with government mood.
What it means
This is the closest thing on today's wire to your own lane: a drone company, in India, reaching profitability and a public listing — proof the path exists where you are standing, not only in California. Two transferable reads: service revenue beats hardware sales (build the thing that recurs), and a non-engineer founder won on distribution and timing. Watch the DRHP when it goes public — it will tell you, in their own numbers, what an Indian drone buyer actually pays for.
Energy · the subsurface/power wedge · manufacturing as the moat

Blue Energy raised $380M to build nuclear reactors in shipyards and float them to the site

$380M led by VXI Capital (after a $45M Series A) · Constellation strategic · MIT spinout, 2023 · Victoria, TX · MIT News · World Nuclear News

What they do
Blue Energy builds nuclear power plants the way shipyards build ships: it fabricates 1,500-ton “Blue Block” modules — fully plumbed, wired and functional — inside existing shipyards, then barges them to the site for final assembly. The reactor itself is not theirs: it is GE Vernova Hitachi's small modular light-water reactor, already NRC-approved. Their invention is the factory, not the physics.
The tech — and why the shipyard matters
Conventional nuclear is built on-site, custom, in the open, by a workforce assembled once and disbanded — which is why it runs a decade late at double the budget. Blue Energy's insight: a nuclear plant is mostly steel welding, and shipyards already build oil-and-gas plants that way. They have the skilled crews, the heavy equipment, the controlled environment and the automation. Move the work there and you target ~$5,000 per kilowatt versus Vogtle's more-than-double, and ~36 months versus 10+ years.
The founders
Jake Jurewicz (MIT nuclear engineering '14, SM '15) and Matt Slotkin, 2023. Jurewicz's master's thesis was literally about borrowing construction lessons from offshore oil and gas; he then did corporate strategy at Exelon, America's largest nuclear operator, before founding the company. The unfair access: he studied the exact cost problem academically, then watched it from inside a utility.
Where it came from
The observation is the whole company: nuclear's problem has not been the reactor for twenty years — it has been how you build the building around it. Everyone chases better reactors; Jurewicz chased better construction, from a thesis to a utility desk to a shipyard. First project is a 2.5 GW hybrid plant in Victoria, Texas: gas online 2030, nuclear from 2032.
The bear case
Nuclear timelines slip and regulators are slow; a “36-month” plant has not yet been built this way, and the first one never hits its number. Final investment decision isn't until 2027 and nuclear doesn't start until 2032 — a long bet on a model unproven at scale, with $380M that buys optionality, not yet a running plant. If the NRC or the shipyard economics balk, it's a very expensive thesis.
What it means
This is the energy/subsurface wedge earning its place again — and it's the purest design-process story you'll read this week: the moat isn't the technology, it's the decision to move the work somewhere it can be manufactured. That is exactly the instinct you read for. And note the pattern with Commonwealth Fusion (§2) the same morning: the hard part of energy right now is building the thing cheaply, not inventing it — an under-glamorous, under-staffed field sitting next to your degree's subject matter.

The Remake

one evidenced candidate · never more
Same tool, unbundled — a reviews widget with taste, for brands that have taste

Unbundle the one thing merchants actually bought Yotpo for — and make it beautiful

The incumbent
Yotpo on the Shopify App Store — 4.8★ across 4,640 reviews, an all-in-one platform that bundles product reviews, loyalty, SMS and email. Its origin product was reviews; it now gates them behind an enterprise suite most small merchants never use.
The complaint
Real, dated 1-star reviews on Yotpo's own Shopify listing, all pointing at the same thing — you are paying a suite price for one feature. Consiglio's Kitchenware (Nov 2025): “BE VERY CAREFUL. THEY GET YOU INTO A CONTRACT … $329 packages … which will save you thousands per year.” Another merchant: “behaves really scammy … minimum $129/month” for limited function. A third: a support case promised in 4–6 hours, “now been two days, absolutely no response.”
Which remake
Same tool, unbundled (with a heavy dose of better taste). Do not rebuild the suite. Ship the one feature they resent paying suite prices for: a product-reviews app that collects, moderates and displays reviews — nothing else — with a widget whose default state looks hand-designed, not like a 2016 star-rating plugin. Judge.me and Loox already own “cheap reviews”; neither owns “reviews that look like part of a premium brand.”
The buyer
Design-led small Shopify brands — apparel, home, beauty, food — where the storefront is the brand and a generic review block breaks the spell. They pay Yotpo $129–329/mo today for far more than they use. A flat $19–39/mo for “reviews that match my store” is an easy switch, and the buyer is the user.
Why him
This is the one remake where your edge is the product. Everyone can ship a reviews widget; almost nobody ships one a designer would leave on without restyling. Your filmmaker's eye for how a thing should look, applied to the single most-seen surface on a storefront, is a moat a feature-list competitor cannot copy. It also lives inside Shopify, so distribution — the hard part — is half-solved by the marketplace.
First step
Today: install Yotpo, Judge.me and Loox on a dev store, screenshot each review widget on the same product, and redesign the block once in Figma until it looks like it belongs on a brand you admire. If your version is obviously better at a glance, that screenshot is your landing page. (Kill-filter check: the obvious idea — “another all-in-one like Yotpo” — fails: it needs every feature on day one and competes on breadth, Yotpo's turf. The unbundled taste-play passes all five: useful day one, buyer = user with budget, runs unattended, not ad-supported consumer, and three payers are named in the complaint thread above.)

This is the concrete event behind the “crypto-agility” red problem on the board — if machines start shortening proofs, the math that cryptography bets on being hard is the thing to watch. A mental model for the next six months, not a build.
  • Learn · the counterweight — IEEE Spectrum, “Why AI Can't Replace Engineering Teams.” The honest version of the Daytona/agent story: generation is cheap, but judgment, integration and accountability are not. Read it against every “agents will do it all” headline — the gaps it names are exactly where a human-shaped product still has to sit.
  • Design × buildable — MIT News: design software that reimagines everyday objects as self-aware devices — CAD that embeds sensing into the geometry of a part as you draw it. The decision-level design thinking you read for, pointed at exactly the kind of part your tiltrotor is made of.

Skill

day 24

Give one Claude Code project a live, authed URL this weekend — the whole of §0-green, in miniature

~2–3 hours · free · produces: one of your projects, deployed, behind a login — and a felt sense of exactly where the pain is

Today's §0-green is “deploying the software you already build is still hard,” and Daytona just got a $1B mark for the adjacent half of it. The way to understand a problem you might build in is to feel it end to end once. So this weekend, take one thing you made with Claude Code that currently lives on localhost and get it onto the public internet with a login screen — not a polished product, just the full path from “runs on my machine” to “a friend can use it with a password.” Pick the shortest route: Val Town for something small and server-side, Cloudflare Workers for something that needs to scale to zero and back, or Vercel if it's a web app. Add auth with whatever the platform gives you for free.

The point is not the deploy — it's the friction you hit on the way. Count the steps. Notice where you had to read docs, where you had to make a security decision you weren't sure about, where you'd have given up if you weren't technical. That list of friction is the product §0-green is pointing at, and you'll have written it from inside the problem instead of guessing at it. Do it with a real project, not a toy, so the auth and data decisions are real.

Free resources: Val Town's engineering blog on sandboxing arbitrary user code (the hardest part of the real version), Cloudflare's Workers free tier, and the YC RFS entry “A Cloud for Small Software” for the funder's own framing of what you just felt.
