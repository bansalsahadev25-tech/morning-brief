START THIS MONTHgreen · pure software · no capital · you already understand agentic code editing from the inside

API providers still announce breaking changes instead of applying them — and this week a single bad change took down inboxes, banks and 178 companies at once

The problem
When an API changes, every customer hand-edits their own codebase to keep up. The provider ships a changelog; the work of absorbing it falls on thousands of downstream teams, one repo at a time. Agentic coding tools just proved companies will grant an external tool write access to their code if the value is there — Claude Code, Devin and Greptile normalised exactly that. The infrastructure to let an API provider push a change into its customers' code now exists. The layer that does it does not.
Who says it's hard
Y Combinator's RFS, from a founder who worked with 50+ API vendors, names it directly. And the cost of getting change-propagation wrong was the whole wire this morning: Microsoft blamed its monster email outage on a defect introduced by a July fix, Optimizely served malicious JavaScript to 178 organisations including Zoom and HSBC through one compromised dependency, and an engineer argued, with receipts, that software is breaking down at a systemic level. Satya Nadella, same day, told an interviewer code verification matters more as generation gets cheap — which is this problem said from the other end.
Why it's still open
Two years ago nobody would grant an external tool write access to their repo, so nobody built the obvious thing. The window opened recently enough that it is still open. The hard part is not the codemod — it is trust: automated pull requests against a stranger's repository that a team will actually merge. Whoever earns that trust owns a position between every API provider and its customers.
Who's already trying
Greptile and Ellipsis (code review), Renovate (dependency bumps only, not API semantics). Nobody owns the specific thing: a provider-side button that turns “we're deprecating this endpoint” into a merged PR in every customer's codebase.
Your angle
Pure software, no capital, and you already understand agentic code editing from the inside — you are the person who grants Claude Code access to a repo and watches what it does. Narrow enough to prototype in a weekend: pick one API, one breaking change, one automated migration. The trust problem is the moat, and feeling it on your own repos first is the cheapest research there is.
Research path
  1. Pick one API with frequent breaking changes (Stripe, Twilio) and read two years of its changelog — every entry is a migration someone did by hand.
  2. Write the migration codemod for a single breaking change by hand, on one of your own projects. Feel the work before you automate it.
  3. Read Renovate's architecture docs on how automated PRs against a stranger's repo earn enough trust to get merged — that mechanism, not the diff, is the company.
NEW · FILE, DON'T STARTred — the company is defense, clearance, hardware, a decade; the on-ramp is contactless sensing you can prototype on your drone

Deciding who to save first — from a distance, with no hands on the patient — just got a DARPA finals and a $1.5m winner four days ago

The problem
In a mass-casualty event — a tornado-struck market, a battlefield, a collapsed building — the first decision is triage: who is hurt worst, who to reach first, who can wait. Today that needs a trained human to get close and touch each casualty. The hard problem DARPA just funded is doing it remotely and automatically: an autonomous platform that reads the physiological signatures of injury — breathing, bleeding, consciousness — from stand-off sensors, in smoke and darkness, and ranks casualties before a human can arrive.
Who says it's hard
DARPA, via the DARPA Triage Challenge, whose Finals concluded 13 Nov at Disaster City, Texas: RoboScout won the Systems competition and $1.5M, Disruptors won the Data competition and $1M. DARPA's director noted human-machine teams beat every other approach — but no single team was strong at identification, triage and evacuation together. The integration is unsolved, which is the tell that it's genuinely open.
Why it's still open
Contactless physiological sensing is an underdetermined inference problem: you're estimating vital signs and injury from faint, ambiguous, remote cues — thermal, RF, optical, acoustic — through smoke and motion, then making a life-or-death ranking no do-over forgives. It's the same family of hard as the board's passive-ranging: sense-making from what the world radiates, not from what you emit.
Who's already trying
DARPA performers — RoboScout, DART, Disruptors, OCA-SENTINEL, UAS-DTU, Guardian Angel, plus Carnegie Mellon's Team Chiron. Adjacent: battlefield-medicine and casualty-evac robotics, and the autonomous-drone sensing stack generally.
Your angle
Red as a company — defense, clearance, hardware, capital, a decade; do not try to start it. But the on-ramp is the exact capability your tiltrotor needs anyway: passive, contactless perception and on-board scene understanding from a flying platform, provable in simulation on a laptop. It pairs with american-defense (you're US-eligible without sponsorship) and passive-ranging. Own the sensing math and vocabulary; let someone with a cleared lab own the company.
Research path
  1. Read the Triage Challenge Finals writeup — note which stand-off signatures the Systems competition scored, and why no team was strong across all three stages.
  2. Study remote vital-sign estimation as a signals problem: rPPG (pulse from ordinary video), thermal and RF respiration sensing — the laptop-sized version of the same question.
  3. Ask it of your own aircraft: what could a camera plus cheap sensors on your tiltrotor infer about a person below without landing? Prototype the perception, never the aircraft.

On the rotation: the green (api-change-propagation) has never led §0 inside the 21-day window — it is the single freshest green on the board, and a wire full of changes propagating badly (Microsoft's July-fix defect, the Optimizely dependency compromise) is the right morning to run it. The red is new: DARPA's Triage Challenge finished its finals on 13 Nov, so autonomous-triage was appended to config/problems.yaml this run and surfaces fresh — a genuinely new, funder-attached hard problem one domain from your drone work. Also appearing but not promoted: DARPA's NOM4D in-space-assembly milestone (already filed, in-space-assembly, red) and the Institute for Progress piece on the brain's cell types (a science bottleneck, not yet an attackable problem). YC's RFS is unchanged since the Winter-2027 reset.

The security analyst, rebuilt as a model · stealth to unicorn in 90 days

Corma reached a $1B valuation three months after leaving stealth — betting that defense, not offense, is the part of cybersecurity AI nobody has trained for

Corma (Tel Aviv + San Francisco) · $125M Series A · $1B valuation · led by NEA, existing backers Sequoia, Khosla, with Coatue · total raised $185M · ~90 days from a $60M Sequoia-led seed · founded 2025 · compute expanded 24× since Aug 2026 · claimed early results at Fortune 100/500: threat-response time down 94%+, coverage up 15× · TechCrunch · The Register · Margin of Safety

What they do
Corma is building a foundation model purpose-trained for defensive cybersecurity — AI agents that embed inside a company and work alongside its security team, triaging threats and responding at machine speed. The thesis is specific: general models are getting exponentially better at attacking, because attack data and attack objectives are abundant, and barely better at defending, because defense was never the thing they were trained on. Corma wants to close that asymmetry with a model built for the defender from the ground up.
The tech — what's hard
Two hard things. One, there is almost no standardised benchmark for “defensive cybersecurity intelligence,” so even proving the model is good is unsolved science — you're building the ruler and the thing it measures at once. Two, a from-scratch frontier foundation model is enormously capital-hungry; the skeptics' sharpest point (see Margin of Safety) is that $185M may not buy frontier-grade reasoning built from zero, which would make this a very expensive fine-tune wearing a foundation-model's clothes.
The founders — the unfair access
CEO Alon Pluda is an alumnus of Israel's Unit 8200 and the Psagot program; Sequoia, which led the seed, called him “one of the most elite hackers in the world.” The team pairs cybersecurity veterans from 8200 with AI researchers out of Google and DeepMind. That is the access: people who spent years on the offensive side, who know exactly how modern attacks are built, turning around to train the defender. You cannot model what you've never had to break.
The bear case
The two hard things above are also the kill shots: if the benchmarks never materialise, buyers can't tell Corma from a wrapper, and if the capital can't fund a real frontier model, the “foundation model” framing is marketing. Add that incumbents (CrowdStrike, Palo Alto, Microsoft) own distribution and the Fortune 500 relationships, and that “94% faster” is the company's own number with no third-party audit. A $1B price in 90 days is paying for the team and the thesis, not proven product.
What it means
The transferable lesson is the shape of the bet, not the raise you can't replicate. Corma found a place where the general model is structurally weak — trained for one objective and therefore bad at its mirror image (defense) — and built the specialist. That's a repeatable pattern you can hunt for with no capital: where is the frontier model systematically weak because of what it was trained to do, and what narrow tool wins there? It also validates today's security wire as a market, not just noise — and §0's api-change-propagation is one such narrow, trust-shaped slice.
The remake thesis, funded · rebuild Salesforce AI-native, by the people who built Freshworks

Dreamteam raised $53m to rebuild the CRM as four AI coworkers — because the richest customer context always lived everywhere except the CRM

Dreamteam (San Francisco) · $53M · from Accel, Together Fund, Premji Invest · founded 2025 by ex-Freshworks execs Anand Venkatraman (25+ yrs enterprise SW, incl. Salesforce), Ramesh Parthasarathy, Senthil Kanthaswamy · 100+ customers in its first 3 months of selling · targets Salesforce and HubSpot · TechCrunch · Inc42 · TNW

What they do
Dreamteam is a CRM rebuilt around a shared memory it calls the Company Brain — a system that continuously ingests context from email, calendars and meetings across Gmail, Outlook, Slack, Zoom and Google Meet, capturing buyer intent, objections, competitor mentions and who-knows-whom. On top sit four named AI coworkers (Rachel, Sally, Raj, Alex) that do research, sales operations and CRM admin themselves — the data entry a human rep is supposed to do and never does. The pitch isn't “AI inside your CRM,” it's “the CRM maintains itself.”
The tech — what's hard
The moat is the ingestion, not the chat. Pulling reliable structure out of the messy exhaust of real sales work — threads, calls, calendar invites — and keeping it current enough that a manager trusts the pipeline view is the hard, unglamorous part. A chatbot bolted onto Salesforce is a weekend; a shared memory that stays correct across five tools and a whole sales team is years, and it's exactly what the incumbents' architecture, built for manual entry, makes hard to retrofit.
The founders — the unfair access
Venkatraman spent 25+ years in enterprise software, including Salesforce and Freshworks, and kept hitting the same wall: reps updated records, yet managers still lacked a dependable view of the business. “The CRM was supposed to give companies a complete picture of their customers, yet the richest parts of that picture have always lived outside the CRM.” That is founder-market fit in one sentence — three people who built a CRM company (Freshworks) and therefore know precisely which promise CRMs never kept.
The bear case
Salesforce and HubSpot are not asleep — both are shipping their own AI agents onto distribution and data Dreamteam can't match, and “rip out your CRM” is the hardest switch in enterprise software. 100 customers in three months is real but tiny, and the category is crowded with AI-native-CRM pitches that sound identical. The “Company Brain” also needs deep access to every communication tool a company runs — a security and trust ask right as the whole industry gets nervous about exactly that.
What it means
This is your §A playbook at venture scale: take a proven, resented incumbent category and rebuild it for how work actually happens now. Note what they did not do — they didn't add a feature to a CRM, they moved the system of record to where the data already lived (the inbox, the call) and let agents keep it. The portable lesson: the opportunity is usually the gap between what a tool promises and what it has always silently required a human to do. Find that unkept promise in a category you can watch, and the remake writes itself.
Persuasion as a product · the Wolf's closer, shipped as software

Jordan Belfort raised ~$10m for an AI voice that sells with his tonality — the exact thing you've been told to study, now packaged and sold by the man himself

Tone (Jordan Belfort) · ~$10M friends-and-family + seed · revenue ~$700K–$1M · a voice-AI platform that imbues sales calls with Belfort's persuasion tonality · customers already live: law firms, health insurers, auto dealers — businesses with more leads than human reps can work · new: a self-serve tier turning authors' books into “expressive AI narration” · TechCrunch · Dealroom

What they do
Tone is an AI voice agent engineered not around what it says but how — the tonality Belfort has taught since the late 1980s. It phrases statements as questions to “build engagement,” drops to a whisper to imply “scarcity of information,” and paces a call the way a trained closer does. Belfort claims it already closes almost as well as a top human rep and will catch up within a year. The buyers are businesses drowning in leads their human teams can't reach.
The tech — what's hard
The technically interesting bet is that in voice sales, prosody is the product — timing, pitch, the implied smile or whisper — and that it can be encoded as deliberately as a script. Modern voice models made the raw synthesis trivial; the hard, defensible part is the judgment layer that decides when to ask versus tell, when to slow down, when to imply scarcity — the stuff Belfort's seminars sell. Whether that's a durable moat or a prompt someone copies in a quarter is the open question.
The founders — the unfair access
The access is a person, not a dataset. Belfort is the “Wolf of Wall Street” — a convicted stock fraudster turned sales trainer whose name alone opens the sales-leadership market and whose decades of tonality coaching are the training signal no competitor has. That is the whole thesis: the most transferable thing in this company is a founder with unusual, hard-won access to how persuasion sounds, and he's turning the seminar into software.
The bear case
Belfort's name is both the distribution and the liability — a convicted fraudster selling AI built to be maximally persuasive is a headline and a regulator's first example. AI voice agents are a crowded, fast-commoditising field (a dozen funded startups, plus the model labs), and “persuasion tonality” may not survive contact with TCPA, robocall law and consent rules once it scales. Revenue is ~$700K–$1M on ~$10M raised: an interesting start, not a proven business.
What it means
You were told, by your own profile, to study persuasion as craft — and here is persuasion being productised, which is the sharper lesson. Two takeaways. First, watch it as a live case of a durable question: when a skill (tonality, taste, judgment) gets encoded into a tool, does the expert keep the margin or does the model commoditise it? That question governs §0's fifteen-dollar-expert too. Second, §7 tonight is Influence for exactly this reason: the levers Belfort is selling as software are the ones you should be able to name and recognise — from both sides of the call.

The Remake

one evidenced candidate · no idea without a linked complaint

Ship the Shopify page builder whose pages you actually own — so an app update can't make “years of work… PUFF, gone forever”

The incumbent
Shogun Landing Page Builder on the Shopify App Store — a drag-and-drop page builder with paid tiers running from about $39/mo up into the hundreds. Well-liked on average (4.8★, 1,980 reviews), which is the point: the opening isn't a bad product, it's a structural grievance inside a good one. Page builders like it also carry a documented tax — independent speed tests put the JavaScript layer they add at roughly 300–600ms per page.
The complaint
A real merchant, in their own words, on the reviews page — Bened Life (US), a four-year customer: “They just split the Personalization feature into a New App… someone just pushed the new app and REMOVED THE YEARS OF PERSONALIZATION work. Just PUFF, GONE forever.” The nerve underneath it: your store's pages live inside the app, so when the vendor restructures, re-prices or splits features into a second paid app, your work is hostage. Uninstall and the pages can break. You rent your own storefront.
Which remake
#2, same tool, better taste — with ownership as the feature. Build a section/page builder whose output is clean native theme code the merchant owns: the sections it writes stay in the theme and keep working if the app is ever removed. Calm, fast, legible, no second-app upsell, no JavaScript bloat layer. The incumbent can't copy this without dismantling the lock-in that is its business model — which is precisely why taste-plus-ownership is a defensible wedge and not just a nicer UI.
The buyer
Shopify merchants already paying Shogun, PageFly or GemPages $39–$499/mo for landing pages — buyers with a card out, inside a marketplace that solves discovery for you. The felt pain is concrete and dated: lock-in, surprise re-pricing, page-speed penalties that cost them conversions.
Why him
This is a taste-and-trust product in a marketplace that hands you distribution — your two strongest edges in one place. The design judgment (a builder that's quiet and fast instead of a bloated suite) is exactly what you have and the incumbent won't ship. And “you own the output” is a story a filmmaker can tell cleanly, which is half of why a merchant switches.
First step
Doable in a day: build a Shopify theme app extension (an app block) that renders one clean, fast hero section — and prove the output survives uninstall by deleting the app and watching the section stay. If a merchant can drop one on-brand section into their theme in five minutes and see that it's theirs, not rented, you have the wedge and the demo. (See §6: today's skill is exactly this extension.) (Kill-filter check: useful on first install, one store, zero network effects; buyer = user, a merchant with budget and a dated grievance; runs unattended once a section is placed; lives inside the Shopify App Store where buyers already shop. The honest risk: a full page builder is a real engineering lift and the category is crowded — so don't chase feature parity. Win one thing, clean fast sections you own, and let the incumbents keep the bloat.)

Skill

day 39

Build a Shopify theme app extension that renders one clean section — the exact wedge under today's §A, and the thing that proves “you own the output”

~2–3 hrs · free (Shopify dev account + CLI) · produces: an app block rendering a real, on-brand section inside a live store's theme — and proof it survives the app being uninstalled

Today's §A turns on one technical fact: a theme app extension (an “app block”) writes its section into the merchant's theme, so the content is theirs and keeps working even if your app is removed — the opposite of the lock-in that made Bened Life watch years of work go “PUFF, gone forever.” So go build the smallest possible version. Install the Shopify CLI, scaffold an app with a theme app extension, and ship a single block — a hero section with an editable headline and image — that a merchant can drop into their theme from the editor. Don't build the whole §A builder; build one block, well.

Then do the thing that is the entire pitch: uninstall your app and reload the storefront. The section should still be there, because it was baked into the theme, not rented from your server. That five-second demo — “delete the app, the page stays” — is the whole §A wedge made real, and it's the kind of concrete, legible promise a merchant switches for. You cannot judge the Shogun opportunity from the outside; you have to feel how little stands between you and a section a merchant actually owns.

Free resource: Shopify's theme app extension docs — the single path from zero to an app block live in a real theme. Read it once and “you own your pages” stops being a slogan and becomes a thing you've shipped, which is more than most people pitching marketplace tools can say.

  • The Areni-1 Cave Shoe (Atlas Obscura) — a single leather shoe, roughly 5,500 years old, preserved almost perfectly by sheep dung and a dry Armenian cave — older than Stonehenge, older than the pyramids, and recognisably a shoe, cut from one piece of hide and laced.   • “It runs Doom” is more than a meme (Scientific American) — why a 1993 game ends up running on pregnancy tests, printers and gut bacteria isn't just a joke: it's a story about an engineering decision — portable, modular, radically lean code — that made the thing nearly universal.   • Neanderthal DNA linked to lower schizophrenia risk (Scientific American) — the genetic legacy humans carry from interbreeding with Neanderthals tens of thousands of years ago turns out to shift the risk of a modern psychiatric condition.   • Crime Pays: How ‘Grand Theft Auto’ Became a Cultural Colossus (Longreads) — the long story of how one game franchise grew into the most valuable entertainment property on earth, bigger than any film.   • Astrocyte Biocomputing (arXiv, neuroscience) — the claim that astrocytes, the brain's star-shaped “support” cells long treated as mere glue, may actually compute — contributing to how neural networks process information. If right, half the brain's cells have been quietly doing a job nobody credited them with, and our model of what thinking even is gets redrawn.
  • The Lukashian Calendar (arXiv, social physics) — a proposed universal calendar built on years and days of variable duration, tracking real astronomical motion instead of forcing nature into fixed boxes. Gloriously impractical, maybe, but a clean example of questioning a convention so deep you forgot it was a choice — which is where most original ideas start.

“How to Make Almost Anything” — MIT's legendary digital-fabrication course, free online, and aimed exactly at the way you build

free · full course site, notes & lectures · MIT MAS.863 (Gershenfeld)

Neil Gershenfeld's “How to Make Almost Anything” is the course that launched the global fab-lab movement, and the whole site — assignments, tool documentation, student build logs — is public. It walks through designing and physically making things across every process you'd touch building a tiltrotor: CAD, laser cutting, 3D printing, molding and casting, machining, electronics design, embedded programming. This is the design-process-at-the-part-level thing you said pulls you, taught as a making discipline rather than a lecture: every week is “here is a process, now produce an object with it.” For a build staged into seven publishable rungs, it's a map of the capabilities each rung needs and a model for documenting them in public. Spend an evening in the molding-and-casting or the embedded-programming week and your own build log gets sharper — you'll see which fabrication decision to make, and why, before you cut anything.
