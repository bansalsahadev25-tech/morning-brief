START THIS MONTHgreen · solo · no capital · no credentials

Millions of apps are now written by people who cannot audit them — and Google just added millions more

The problem
AI-generated code ships daily from people without the background to review it — no sense of what an exposed key, an open endpoint or a missing auth check looks like. The population that writes software now dwarfs the population that can tell whether that software is safe.
Who says it's hard
r/SaaS practitioners asking, in the same week, how to even test a vibe-coded app (r/SaaS) — and the premise got louder today, not quieter: Google shipped a feature that lets anyone build private, vibe-coded apps that run on Android, and wants to host them in the Play Store. The number of shipped, unaudited apps is about to step-change, from the company with the widest distribution on earth.
Why it's still open
Brand new. The affected population did not exist three years ago, and the tools that do exist — Snyk, Semgrep — were built for professional engineers who already know what a vulnerability is. Nobody has built the version that assumes the user cannot read the finding.
Who's already trying
Snyk, Semgrep — powerful, and aimed at exactly the wrong audience. The gap is a tool that speaks to someone who shipped an app with Claude Code and has no idea it's leaking. That gap is the product.
Your angle
You ARE the user, and so is everyone in your hostel who ships with AI. Zero capital, immediate feedback, and a market you can interview in person this week — which is exactly what today's §6 skill turns into a working artifact.
Research path
  1. Take three things you built with Claude Code and genuinely try to break them — exposed keys, open endpoints, missing auth. Write down what you find.
  2. Read Semgrep's rule syntax — the state of the art you'd be simplifying for people who can't read it.
  3. Ask in r/SaaS what they'd pay to never ship a leak again. They answer — and today's Google launch just widened that crowd.

The software running physical work hasn't changed in 20 years

The problem
80% of the global workforce doesn't sit at a desk. Construction, maintenance, fleet operations still run on software that dispatches people, tracks them, manages assets and bills the customer — and those industries spend 10–100× more on labour than on software. The coordination layer is ancient.
Who says it's hard
YC's RFS — “New Operating Systems for the Physical World” — and today the wire sharpened it twice: TechCrunch on Industrial Next wanting factory robots to switch tasks in minutes, not weeks, and Rivian's robotics spin-out hiring ex-Ford/Apple EV chief Doug Field. The capital is betting the physical layer is where the next decade's software gets built.
Why it's still open
Incumbents charge for human coordination. Nobody has built the layer that schedules agents, robots and humans together — and whoever does captures end-to-end records of how the work actually happens, which no frontier lab or robotics startup will have.
Who's already trying
ServiceTitan, Samsara, Procore — all built for the old model, all pricing human dispatch. Industrial Next and Mind Robotics (both today) are attacking the robot end; the scheduling-and-records layer between them is still open.
Your angle
This is the category your cab-booking project sits in — dispatch, matching, fleet, real-world supply, with users walking past you, which is the part nobody can buy. You are not building a booking form; you are building dispatch. Amber not green because it needs real users and real vehicles, not a hostel room.
Research path
  1. Re-read your own PLAN.md with this framing: the product is the record of how the work happened, not the form.
  2. Talk to the middleman you already know about what he cannot see — idle vehicles, no-shows, driver reliability. That blindness is the feature.
  3. Read Samsara's investor materials — what fleet operators actually pay for, in their own words.

The defence door · a company built on the exact sensing your drone needs — and its IPO opens tomorrow

A CMU-trained roboticist built India's thermal-imaging monopoly for weapons — 93% of the country's export units — and takes it public tomorrow

Tonbo Imaging · founded 2012, Bengaluru · co-founders Arvind Lakshmikumar (CEO; CMU robotics PhD, ex-Sarnoff), Ankit Kumar (CTO, ex-Sarnoff), Cecilia D'Souza, Sudeep George · Rs 413 Cr secondary raise (Amansa, SBI MF, HDFC Life) at ~Rs 4,000 Cr · IPO opens Oct 15, price band Rs 665–700, ~Rs 852 Cr (all offer-for-sale) · Inc42 · tonboimaging.com

What they do
Tonbo designs and builds the eyes of weapons systems: thermal-imaging sights, electro-optic sensors, missile seekers, fire-control and targeting systems for land, naval, air and tactical use. It is, by its own filing, India's largest maker of thermal imaging for government and defence — and accounted for roughly 93% of India's thermal-imaging export volume by units in FY24 and FY25. FY25: revenue Rs 469 Cr, net profit Rs 73 Cr, with 65% of sales from exports.
The tech — and what's hard
The hard part isn't the camera; it's seeing passively, in the dark, while moving, without emitting anything — the exact problem DARPA just put money behind in its CIDAR passive-ranging challenge (§0's standing red). Thermal and low-light electro-optics, sensor fusion, and target recognition on a moving platform are an estimation problem most teams can't do well, which is why so few firms worldwide ship it and why export-controlled optics are a genuine moat. Tonbo's edge is a decade of vertically integrated hardware-plus-vision that newcomers can't assemble over a weekend.
The founders & where the idea came from
Lakshmikumar did his robotics doctorate at Carnegie Mellon and ran programs for DARPA, NASA, the US Army, AFRL and DRDO before heading technology at Sarnoff (the RCA research lab that invented colour TV); he and Ankit Kumar left Sarnoff together to found Tonbo. The access was unusual: two people who had built military vision systems inside the US defence-research complex, then pointed that know-how at a country with a large procurement budget and almost no domestic supplier. That is the transferable lesson — not the product, the position.
The bear case
The IPO is entirely offer-for-sale — no fresh money goes into the company; early backers are cashing out, which is a tell, not a crime. Defence revenue is lumpy and order-driven: Tonbo posted a loss in a recent quarter, and a single delayed government contract swings a year. Valued near Rs 4,000 Cr on Rs 73 Cr of profit, it is priced for growth it hasn't booked yet, and its customers are governments whose budgets move with politics.
For you
This is the §0 “American defence” and passive-ranging threads made concrete: the sensing Tonbo sells is the same math your tiltrotor needs the day GPS drops — passive, emitter-less, on a moving platform. Two takeaways. One: a defence-optics company can be built by two engineers with the right prior access, not a prime with a billion dollars — and you are US-eligible without sponsorship, the barrier that blocks your peers from that whole market. Two: watch this IPO as a live read on what public markets will pay for Indian deep-tech hardware. If it prices well tomorrow, every Indian hardware founder's fundraising just got easier — including, eventually, yours.
The material supply chain · the energy/subsurface wedge, as a company already at $200M run-rate

Three ex-marketplace operators are using AI to make the chemicals the West buys from China — and tripled revenue to a $200M run-rate doing it

Scimplify · founded 2023, Bengaluru · co-founders Salil Srivastava (ex-Zetwerk chemicals, ex-ITC), Sachin Santhosh (IIT Madras; ex-Bizongo, ex-OfBusiness), Dheeraj Dhingra (ex-Zetwerk) · $25M led by Hitachi Ventures (Henkel, Teknor Apex, Accel, 3one4, Omnivore) · $80M total raised · valuation $250M · revenue run-rate $200M (3× YoY) · TechCrunch · scimplify.com

What they do
Scimplify is a full-stack platform for specialty chemicals and critical materials — the tungsten derivatives, rare earths and fine chemicals that go into semiconductors, defence and energy. It develops the chemistry, then manufactures it across a network of 200+ specialised plants, so a Western manufacturer can source a material without a Chinese supplier. It serves 1,000+ customers in 40+ countries (TDK, BASF, Hitachi), is EBITDA-positive, and now does >50% of revenue internationally — up from under 5% eighteen months ago. US ARR went from $10M to $60M.
The tech — and what's hard
The platform, Scinode, uses specialised AI agents for research, manufacturing and QC. The genuinely hard part is the thing the agents attack: turning promising chemistry into a product you can actually buy at scale — a step that normally takes 12–18 months of lab-to-line work. Scimplify claims to compress that to as little as two months in some cases. The moat isn't the model; it's the model wired into 200 real factories. Software alone can't make a molecule; factories alone can't find a new route fast. The pair is the product.
Where the idea came from
All three founders spent the previous decade at Zetwerk, Bizongo and OfBusiness — the Indian manufacturing-marketplace generation — bringing Indian factories to global buyers. They had already seen, from the inside, that India's chemical plants were underused and that the world wanted off China. The access was distribution and supply-chain reps, not chemistry. They pointed a muscle they already had at a geopolitics that had just shifted.
The bear case
Specialty chemicals is capital-heavy, regulated, and physically dangerous — a long way from software margins, and a $200M run-rate at a $250M valuation means the market is pricing it like a trading/manufacturing business, not a multiple-rich SaaS. “Challenge China’s grip” is a decade-long industrial fight with state actors on the other side; if Beijing floods a material to kill a Western alternative, Scimplify's economics on that line vanish. Thin EBITDA leaves little cushion for a bad batch or a plant incident.
For you
This is the energy/subsurface wedge the brief keeps testing — and today it earned its place, because the story is critical materials as the bottleneck under the whole AI/energy buildout, which is your degree's neighbourhood. The transferable move is the same one Fervo's founder made: a team used the unglamorous skill it already owned (manufacturing distribution) against a problem the geopolitics had just made urgent. You don't start a chemicals company. You learn the vocabulary of where the materials actually come from — because every “AI needs power” story bottoms out in a mineral someone has to refine, and almost nobody on the software side can talk about it.
The founder study · a filmmaking-adjacent tool, and a lesson in abandoning your first idea

Three IIT students built a note-taking app, threw it away, and the pivot — AI that makes a company's videos — just raised $10M from 1,000+ paying customers

Clueso · founded 2023 (YC W23), San Francisco + Bengaluru · co-founders Akash Anand (CEO), Prajwal Prakash, Neel Balar — all IIT Madras · $10M Series A led by Dharana Capital (Founders Future, Neotribe, YC, F7) · ~$12M total · 1,000+ paying customers (Atlassian, GitLab, Zendesk, PhonePe) · 39 staff · Inc42 · clueso.io

What they do
Clueso's AI agents learn a company's product, process and brand, then make and maintain its video — you record a rough screen walk-through and it returns a polished video with script, AI voiceover, animations and captions, plus the written doc. When the product changes, you refresh the video instead of reshooting it. Over 1,000 companies pay for it; 65% of usage is US, and more than 70% of the videos are customer-facing. Anand is pointed about the framing: “a dedicated end-to-end platform for enterprises, not a single prompt-to-video tool.”
The tech — and what's hard
The easy part is prompt-to-clip — every lab ships that now (see §4's open video models). The hard part is consistency at enterprise scale: keeping voice, brand, terminology and accuracy identical across hundreds of videos, and keeping them current as the product ships weekly. That's an agent-plus-memory problem, not a generation problem, which is why incumbents who only do generation don't threaten them and why 1,000 companies pay rather than use a free text-to-video toy.
Where the idea came from
Here's the part worth copying. The three built Desklamp, an academic PDF reader / note-taking app, while still at IIT — a product in the exact category today's HN top thread mocks (“note-taker clone after note-taker clone,” §3). They went through YC with it, saw it wasn't working, and pivoted: started by helping support teams make product tutorials faster, then noticed the same pain in marketing, sales and training. The unfair advantage wasn't a brilliant first idea. It was the willingness to kill one and the reps to spot a bigger problem hiding next to the small one.
The bear case
Generative video is the most crowded lane in AI, and the base models underneath Clueso keep getting better and cheaper — which can erode the “we do it end-to-end” edge the moment a foundation lab ships a good-enough enterprise video agent. $12M raised against OpenAI, Google and a dozen funded startups in the same space is thin. And “video for every function” risks being a feature inside a bigger suite (Notion, Atlassian) rather than a standalone company.
For you
Two things, both yours. First, this is the filmmaker's instinct turned into a business — the craft of making video, productised. Second, and bigger: the lesson is the pivot. They shipped a note-taking app (the most derivative category there is), had the honesty to abandon it, and found the real problem one domain over. That is permission to start with the obvious thing and stay alert for the better one standing next to it — which is exactly how §A's remake candidates are supposed to work.

The Remake

one evidenced candidate

You don't need a new idea — you need a proven one, rebuilt for someone specific, with better taste, sitting inside a marketplace that already has buyers. One candidate per issue, and never one without a real user complaining in their own words. Today the complaint is almost too loud: Shopify is killing a free app a huge install base depends on.

The incumbent
Stocky — Shopify's own free inventory-forecasting and purchase-order app, bundled with POS Pro for years. Shopify pulled it from the App Store on Feb 2, 2026 and shuts it off entirely on Aug 31, 2026 (Shopify community thread). A free tool with a large, captive, paying-platform user base is being switched off — and Shopify Admin's built-in inventory doesn't replace it.
The complaint
Real merchants, in their own words on r/shopify and the Shopify community: “I have 2,000 POs in Stocky and no way to bulk export them,” and “the demand forecasting was terrible but at least it existed.” Shopify Admin “will tell you what you have and warn you when stock is low, but it will not tell you what to buy, how much, or when.” Supplier data can't be exported at all. These people are not browsing — they have a shutdown date and a wallet open.
Which remake
#3 — same tool, unbundled. Don't rebuild all of Stocky. Ship the one thing merchants are screaming for and nobody handles well: a clean Stocky → export-and-reorder wedge — bulk export of POs and supplier data, plus the simple reorder maths (“what to buy, how much, when”) Shopify Admin refuses to do — wrapped in taste the generic replacements lack.
The buyer
Shopify merchants with real SKU counts — the ones who ran Stocky because they had enough inventory to need it. Willingness-to-pay is already proven: the replacements merchants are being pushed toward (Inventory Planner, Prediko, Canopy) charge roughly $30–$150+/mo. Stocky was free; the switching cost is now forced, and the App Store puts you in front of them with a card already on file.
Why him
Distribution is solved for you — the Shopify App Store is the single best marketplace in the playbook, full of merchants who lose money on bad inventory calls daily. The replacements shipping now are functional and ugly; taste is your edge, and inventory tools are exactly the kind of unloved B2B software where a clean, fast, legible UI wins. And the migration pain (bulk export) is unglamorous enough that the incumbents are ignoring it — which is the wedge.
First step
Today, build the free Stocky → CSV bulk exporter — the exact thing merchants say doesn't exist — and list it as a free App Store utility. It's doable in a day with Shopify's API, it solves a real complaint on its own, and it becomes the lead magnet that funnels those same merchants into the paid reorder tool. Read three live Shopify community threads first and write down the five features everyone names.
Rejected today
A “Pulley alternative” (cap-table software) — a real migration complaint, but it fails the playbook's network-effects kill-filter: cap tables only work if your investors adopt them too, and Carta's lock-in is the moat. Hard to win solo, so filed and skipped.

day 16

Add a secret-scanner that blocks the commit before it leaks — and you've automated today's §0-green

~1 hour to set up · free · produces: a pre-commit hook that physically stops you shipping a key, on one of your own repos

Today's §0-green is “millions of apps written by people who can't audit them,” and last week's skill was manually breaking your own app. This is the next move: automate the catch so it never gets shipped in the first place. Install gitleaks (one binary, free, open source) on one of your Claude Code projects and wire it in as a pre-commit hook. Now every time you try to commit, it scans the diff for API keys, tokens, private keys and connection strings — and refuses the commit if it finds one. Then run gitleaks detect across the full history of three old repos and watch what you already pushed to GitHub months ago.

The point isn't the tool — it's the felt gap. gitleaks catches secrets, which is maybe 20% of what a vibe-coded app leaks; it says nothing about an open endpoint, a missing auth check, or a Supabase table anyone can read. You'll finish the hour with a working guard and a sharp sense of everything it doesn't cover — and that uncovered 80%, explained to someone who can't read a Semgrep rule, is the entire §0-green product. Build the guard first; feel the hole second.

Free resources: gitleaks (the scanner), pre-commit (the hook framework, gentle setup), and Semgrep's rules once you're ready to catch the logic flaws, not just the secrets. Start with one repo and one hook; expand only after it's blocked you at least once.
