The day the money skipped the software and bought the bottleneck. Benchmark made its first-ever pure-defense bet — $25M into a startup whose real product is the ability to manufacture missile interceptors ~100× faster than America does today; $200M went into a stellarator fusion plant; and a self-taught Thiel Fellow shipped a free AI that runs entirely on the computer already in your hand. One pattern underneath all three: capital is fleeing the undifferentiated software middle for the two ends it can't fake — atoms (missiles, electrons) and the edge (your own device) — and in two of the three, what actually got funded was proof-of-work, not a credential. Own a bottleneck, or own the edge. Not the middle.

START THIS MONTHgreen · solo · no capital · no credentials

Deploying personal software is still hard — and agents just made it urgent

The problem
Agents made it trivial to build software that will only ever have one or a handful of users — a tool for your team, a tracker for your own workflow. Deploying and sharing it is still as hard as it was in 2015. AWS and Azure were designed for software that scales to millions, and that complexity is now the bottleneck. The pressure only went up this week: Anthropic started handing startups a free year of Claude Team plus $1,000 in credits — cheaper than ever to generate a working app, with still nowhere good to put it — and Bluesky is pitching “your own domain on the open web” as a feature people will pay for. The build step collapsed; the ship step didn't.
Who says it's hard
Y Combinator, Requests for Startups — “A Cloud for Small Software” — source. Still on the current list, unchanged since Fall 2026.
Why it's still open
The hard parts aren't the hosting. They're auth and permissions for arbitrary user-written code, per-person environment customisation, and letting non-technical people share executable code without it becoming a security incident. Everyone underestimates the third one — and it's the exact flaw that keeps showing up in the vibe-code security news.
Who's already trying
Vercel, Replit, Val Town, Cloudflare Workers, Deno Deploy — all strong on hosting, none yet owning the “one command, now it has auth and a URL a stranger can safely use” moment for someone who can't read the code they shipped.
Your angle
You already live inside this problem: you build with Claude Code and then have nowhere good to put the result. That is the founder's unfair advantage in its cheapest form — you are the user. Green because it needs no capital and no credential, only taste about the one workflow that hurts.
Research path
  1. Build the ugliest possible version for yourself first: one command that takes a Claude Code project and hands you a URL with auth already on it.
  2. Read Val Town's public writeups on sandboxing user-written code — they have hit every wall you will hit.
  3. Ask ten people in your hostel who build things where their projects currently live. The answer is “localhost”, and that is the market.
NEEDS CAPITAL, A LAB OR A DECADEred · filed so you recognise it, not so you start it

AI is running out of electricity and land, not chips

The problem
New data centres take years to win approval and enough megawatts, and can still be killed by local opposition. The constraint on AI is quietly shifting from “can we get the GPUs” to “can we power them.” Today's board is thick with the evidence: a $200M fusion round (see §1), Google's data centres openly craving nuclear, Lambda raising $4B and a Singapore datacentre operator filing to go public. The money that isn't going into models is going into the electrons to run them.
Who says it's hard
Y Combinator RFS (“Bringing Compute to the Oceans”) — source — plus the capital itself: Type One's $200M, Google's multi-hundred-MW nuclear deals, SpaceX reportedly seeking $40B partly for infrastructure.
Why it's still open
Permitting, capital intensity, and physics. None of it is software, and none of it yields to a clever weekend. A stellarator is a decade and hundreds of millions; a geothermal well is a drilling rig and a balance sheet. This is a problem you buy your way into, not hack your way into.
Who's already trying
Crusoe, Fervo, Mazama, Oklo, and now Type One Energy with a utility (TVA) attached. Google, Microsoft and Amazon are all signing power deals directly — the buyers have arrived before the supply.
Your angle
Red as a company — be honest with yourself about that. But this is the one red problem where your degree's subject matter is the actual bottleneck: subsurface instrumentation, drilling data and geothermal siting are underbuilt and adjacent to what you already study. The move here is not to found — it's to learn the vocabulary now, so that when a cheap software edge opens inside this (data, monitoring, a permitting workflow), you recognise it before the room does. This is the energy/subsurface thread earning its keep with real numbers, exactly as it was asked to.
Research path
  1. Chip War (on your §7 canon shelf) for why compute and energy are now geopolitics, not just engineering.
  2. Read Fervo's and Mazama's engineering blogs — the actual hard parts of enhanced geothermal, in their own words.
  3. Follow Type One's Infinity Two build at TVA's Bull Run as a live case study in what “power for compute” costs and how long it takes.

New problems on the feed today: the two highest-signal sources — DARPA and the Institute for Progress — both failed to return (DARPA timed out, IFP's host was unreachable), so there is no fresh solicitation to read. YC's RFS is unchanged since the Fall 2026 publish, and Marginal Revolution did not come through this run. One refinement worth filing by hand, not as a new problem: Furientis (§1) sharpens the board's existing american-defense entry — the bottleneck it names is not the drone or the missile, it's the production rate. “Can you build 1,000 a year” is the fundable question, not “can you build one.” No new entry added today.

The Big Three

researched
Founder war story · your exact lane, with Benchmark's first defense cheque

Benchmark's first-ever pure-defense bet is a startup whose product is a factory, not a missile

Furientis · Los Angeles · founded May 2025 by Brody Franzen (ex-deputy chief engineer, Virgin Galactic; ex-Castelion) & Aris Simsarian (ex-rocket-engine test lead, Virgin Orbit) · $25M seed led by Benchmark (GP Chetan Puttagunta joins the board), after a $5M pre-seed · valued ~$125M · funded OTA prototype contract with the Department of War · TechCrunch · company

What they do
Furientis builds low-cost defensive interceptors — the missiles that shoot down incoming missiles and drones — and designs them around two manufacturing numbers, not two performance numbers: 1,000 units a year per facility, and 30-minute final assembly. The product they are really selling is production rate. In their own words, “America made the best defense technology in the world, [but] the rate at which it is currently produced is critically deficient.” They've already run 21 vehicle tests in ten months (13 flights, 8 static fires) and operate propellant lines turning out dozens of solid rocket motors a month.
Where the idea came from
One number. The U.S. Navy takes delivery of roughly 300–500 interceptors a year; China claims it can build 3,000 anti-ship cruise missiles a month. “We're being outproduced by a factor of 100 plus. That is why we started the company,” Franzen said. The founders didn't come from Washington — they came from the engine test stands of Virgin Orbit and the hypersonics startup Castelion, where the whole culture is “make it cheaper and make more of it.” They pointed that instinct at the single most supply-constrained object in American defense.
Why it works (if it works)
It inverts the defense industry's deepest habit. Primes (Raytheon, Lockheed) optimise for the most capable interceptor; Furientis optimises for the most buildable one that still works. Puttagunta's tell is the capital efficiency: “they manufactured prototypes with just $5 million” and had already flown about a dozen launches before the seed. Benchmark — famously a small, un-defense fund — crossing into defense for the first time is itself the signal: the production-rate thesis is now investable by generalists, not just defense specialists.
The bear case
It's a “crowded space” by Puttagunta's own admission — Anduril, Castelion and Shield AI are all better capitalised, and the primes own the procurement relationships that take years to pry open. Hardware at volume is brutal: a 30-minute assembly target on paper is a supply chain, a workforce and a QA regime in reality. And a startup's fortunes here ride on defense budgets and a political appetite that can turn. One funded OTA is a door, not a business.
For you
This is the most on-thesis story in the brief for you, on three axes at once. Domain: you design and fly a two-rotor VTOL — the §0 american-defense on-ramp is autonomy and cheap airframes, exactly your build log reframed. Eligibility: you are US-eligible without sponsorship, the one barrier that locks most of your peers out of this entire market. Lesson: the founders had no political credential — they had 21 flight tests on $5M. Proof-of-work beat the résumé, and a top generalist fund paid for it. Study how two ex-engineers turned “we can build it faster” into a $125M company, because the move — not the missile — is the transferable part.
Deep tech · proof-of-work over a credential, at your age

A self-taught Thiel Fellow shipped a free AI that never leaves your computer

Underdog (Conway Research) · founder Sigil Wen (self-taught; moved to SF at 17, lived in an AI hacker house with Andrej Karpathy; Thiel Fellow, no college) · on-device assistant for Mac/Windows (Linux, iPhone, Android coming) · runs a Qwen3.8-27B fine-tune on his own Husky inference engine · backers a16z (Chris Dixon), Khosla, Hummingbird, SV Angel, the Anthropic/Menlo Anthology Fund; angels Patrick Collison, Guillermo Rauch, Noam Brown · TechCrunch

What they do
Underdog is a private AI assistant that runs entirely on hardware you already own — nothing goes to a server. It encrypts the authorisation keys for your email and connected accounts locally. It launches free, with no ads and no subscription: Wen says on-device costs are low enough that he doesn't need to charge, and instead takes a tiny cut of transactions the assistant makes for you through Stripe's payment rails — closer to an interchange fee than a SaaS plan. It's yesterday's Ghost thesis (privacy as the product) shipped as software instead of a $3,499 box.
The founder & where it came from
This is the data point to internalise. Wen taught himself to code, moved to Silicon Valley at 17, and lived in a hacker house alongside Andrej Karpathy; he tested early versions of Claude, GPT-3, Midjourney and Stable Diffusion, and Naval Ravikant hired him for Airchat. No degree — he's a Thiel Fellow building this instead. The idea comes from having watched, from the inside, exactly how much of your life these assistants have to see to be useful — and betting that a lot of people want that power without the surveillance.
Why it works (if it works)
The enabling tech is Husky, his inference engine, which he claims moves less data between a machine's CPU and GPU than rival on-device engines — the bottleneck that makes local models feel slow. On top of it, a 27B Qwen fine-tune he says matches Claude Opus 4.6 on everyday tasks (shopping research, homework). The wager: for most of what normal people ask an assistant, you don't need a frontier datacentre model, so the private version is simply good enough and free — and “free, private, no ads” is a direct counter to OpenAI putting ads in ChatGPT.
The bear case
Underdog runs much smaller models than the hosted frontier, so anything genuinely hard will expose the ceiling, and “as good as Opus on some benchmarks” is doing a lot of work. The privacy-local category is suddenly crowded — Ghost, Instinct, Muse, Hark, Apple and every OEM — and “data stays local” is a value most people say they want and few change habits for. A transaction-fee model needs real purchasing volume to pay the bills, which is a chicken-and-egg away.
For you
Read the founder, not the app. Wen is proof — alongside Ghost's Javaid yesterday and Furientis above — that in 2026 the thing a16z, Khosla and Patrick Collison write cheques for is a track record of having built and shipped, not a transcript. That is the exact bet you're making with your tiltrotor build log. Note the specific on-ramp: he got access (a hacker house, early model betas, Naval) by being useful early, and the access compounded into the company. Access is the most transferable asset in this whole brief — go manufacture some.
Energy · the real bottleneck under every AI story, funded

$200M went into a stellarator — the hard way to do fusion — to power a plant by 2034

Type One Energy · Knoxville, TN · CEO Christofer Mowry; CTO Thomas Sunn Pedersen (ran the W7-X stellarator at Max Planck); roots in Univ. Wisconsin–Madison plasma physics · $200M Series B led by Breakthrough Energy Ventures & Clutterbuck, with Siemens Energy, Lowercarbon, SiteGround · ~$400M+ raised total · first plant Infinity Two, 400MW, at TVA's Bull Run site · TechCrunch · company

What they do
Type One is building a stellarator — a fusion machine that confines plasma with fiendishly complex, twisted external magnets, rather than the simpler doughnut of the more common tokamak. Their first commercial device, Infinity Two, is planned as a 400-megawatt plant at the Tennessee Valley Authority's Bull Run site, with AECOM engineering it and a target of first power by 2034.
The tech & why it's hard
A stellarator trades a brutal engineering problem for a physics advantage: the twisted field holds plasma stably and continuously, where a tokamak needs a driven current and tends to pulse. The catch is that those magnet shapes are so complex they were essentially un-buildable until modern supercomputer optimisation and precision manufacturing caught up — which is the bet. Pedersen's W7-X in Germany proved the physics works; Type One is betting the manufacturing now works too. As TechCrunch puts it, this sits “at the cutting edge of plasma physics, materials science and advanced computation” all at once.
Why the money came
Not fusion romance — the AI power crunch (today's §0 red). A utility partner (TVA), a turbine-maker (Siemens Energy) and a climate fund (Breakthrough) co-signing means the capital is treating this as an energy infrastructure deal with a buyer attached, not a science project. Fusion got investable the moment compute demand made firm, clean, dense power worth pre-ordering a decade out.
The bear case
Fusion's entire history is “30 years away, always.” 2034 is eight years out for a first-of-its-kind machine whose hardest step — manufacturing those magnets at plant scale and keeping them running — is exactly the part no one has done commercially. $400M sounds huge and is a rounding error against what a working plant costs. Any slip and the AI buildout simply routes around it to nuclear and gas, which are here now.
For you
You are not going to start a fusion company, and you shouldn't — this is the §0 red, filed to recognise, not to attack. The use for you is as a compass: the smartest energy-and-climate capital just voted, with $200M, that power is the binding constraint on AI. That tells you where a decade of demand, jobs and adjacent software problems will sit — grid, siting, subsurface, monitoring — the exact neighbourhood of your degree. Watch Infinity Two the way you'd watch a slow deal close: for what it teaches about who actually pays, and when.

Skill

day 9

Run a real AI model entirely on your own machine — feel the §1 privacy wedge from the inside

~2 hours · free · produces: a private local assistant, and a felt sense of what Ghost and Underdog are actually selling

Two of this week's funded companies — Ghost's $3,499 box and today's Underdog — are selling one idea: a capable AI that runs entirely on hardware you already own, with nothing sent to a server. The fastest way to know whether that's a real wedge or a nerd's fantasy is to be the product for an afternoon. Install Ollama (one command; Mac/Windows/Linux), then pull the same class of model Underdog runs — ollama run qwen3 for a Qwen model, or a smaller Gemma if your machine is light. Ask it the everyday things you'd normally give ChatGPT: summarise a page, draft an email, work through a problem set. Notice two things precisely — how fast it is on your hardware, and exactly where it hits a ceiling a frontier model wouldn't.

That gap you just felt — “good enough and private” versus “smarter but watched” — is the entire bet under §1. Underdog's whole claim is that a 27B model running locally is enough for most of what normal people ask, and that private-plus-free beats a smarter model with ads. After two hours on your own laptop you'll have an informed opinion on whether that's true, which is worth more than any amount of reading the launch coverage. You'll also, usefully, have a private model you can keep — and a concrete sense of the hardware the whole local-AI category is betting gets cheaper.

Free resource: Ollama — the simplest way to run open models locally; its model library lists what fits your RAM. If your machine struggles, drop to a smaller quantised model — that compromise is the product lesson.
