START THIS MONTHgreen · pure software · no capital · you are the user, and so is everyone building small tools with AI

Building personal software got trivial; deploying and sharing it is still stuck in 2015 — and today a whole front page was people hacking their own gadgets to own them

The problem
AI made it trivial to build software that will only ever have one or a handful of users — a tool for your team, a tracker for your own workflow, an app to drive a device you own. Deploying it, sharing it, and putting a login in front of it is as hard as it was a decade ago. AWS and Azure were built for software that scales to millions; that complexity is now the bottleneck for the person who just wants a URL, with auth, for the thing they vibe-coded on Tuesday.
Who says it's hard
Y Combinator's RFS named it (“A Cloud for Small Software”), and this morning the wire made the adjacent ache loud: HN's front page carried “Reverse engineering my Coway air purifier to ditch the cloud,” a maker who built her own app for a subscription-locked embroidery machine (yesterday's §A), and Edge0-145B plus llama.cpp v1.0.0 — “a stronger foundation for the local AI ecosystem.” The common thread is people who can now make the software wanting to own and run it without renting a platform they can't operate.
Why it's still open
The hard parts aren't the hosting. They're auth and permissions for arbitrary user-written code, per-person environment setup, and letting a non-technical maker share something executable without it becoming a security incident. Everyone underrates the last one — and it is exactly adjacent to the vibecoded-security problem this board carried yesterday. Val Town, Replit and Cloudflare Workers have each hit one wall of it; nobody owns the “one command, from Claude Code project to shareable URL with a login” path for the solo maker.
Who's already trying
Vercel, Replit, Val Town, Cloudflare Workers, Deno Deploy — all closer to developers than to the new population of people who build with AI but don't think of themselves as deployers. That gap, between “it runs on my laptop” and “my three teammates can use it,” is the opening.
Your angle
You live in this problem: you build with Claude Code and then have nowhere good to put the result. That's the founder's cheapest unfair advantage — you are the user. Build the ugliest possible version for yourself first (one command → URL + auth), then show it to the ten people around you who also ship with AI and currently answer “where does it live?” with “localhost.” That answer is the market. §6 is today's first step.
Research path
  1. Take one thing you built with Claude Code and get it online with a login in an afternoon — use Cloudflare Workers or Val Town, and write down every step that was harder than it should be. That list is the product spec.
  2. Read Val Town's engineering writeups on sandboxing user-written code — they have hit every wall you will, and documented it.
  3. Ask ten people who build things where their projects currently live. “localhost” is the market; the person who makes sharing a one-liner wins it.
FILED, NOT STARTEDred — permitting, capital, physics; but it is where your degree's subject matter is the bottleneck

AI is running out of electricity and land, not chips — and today the money went to boiling ammonia with the ocean and spending $40bn on clouds

The problem
New data centres take years to approve and enough megawatts to run, and can still be killed by local opposition. The binding constraint on the AI build-out is no longer silicon — it is power and land. Answers now range from the sober (geothermal, nuclear) to the frankly science-fictional (compute barges at sea), and all of them are capital-and-physics problems, not software ones.
Who says it's hard
The capital said it twice today. Drogue raised $20m (XYZ, Toyota Ventures, Boost VC and others) to power floating data centres with ocean-thermal energy — drawing warm surface water and cold water from 1,000m down to boil ammonia and drive a turbine, with a first prototype planned for 2028. The same morning, Nebius committed to ~$40bn in planned spending on the “neocloud” build-out. YC's RFS has been here too (“Bringing Compute to the Oceans”). When someone is boiling ammonia at sea to find a spare megawatt, the shortage is real.
Why it's still open
Permitting, capital intensity, and physics — none of it is a weekend. OTEC has been theoretically sound since the 1880s and has never been commercial because the temperature differences are small and the engineering marginal. A floating compute barge is a bet that AI power demand is desperate enough to make a century-old marginal technology finally pencil out. That it's being tried at all is the data point.
Who's already trying
Crusoe, Fervo, Mazama and Oklo on land (geothermal, nuclear); Drogue and the “compute at sea” crowd offshore; Nebius and the neoclouds building the conventional version at $40bn scale. All capital-heavy, all slow.
Your angle
Red as a company — honestly so. But this is the one red on the board where your field of study is the actual bottleneck: subsurface instrumentation, drilling data and reservoir characterisation are underbuilt and sit next to enhanced geothermal. This is the recurring energy/subsurface thread's audition, and today it finally has real funded evidence rather than vibes. Watch it, learn the vocabulary, apply it to a geothermal data problem if one appears — do not try to start the company.
Research path
  1. Read the Drogue piece, then look up why OTEC never worked before — the gap between “physically possible” and “commercial” is the whole lesson in deep-tech energy.
  2. Read the Fervo and Mazama engineering blogs for the land version — the actual hard parts (drilling, reservoir, heat exchange) in their own words, and the parts that are secretly data problems.
  3. Hold this thread to its own deadline: if the energy/subsurface wedge hasn't convinced you with evidence like today's by the end of the month, retire it and stop forcing it. Today it earned one more look.

The buying surface just changed · agents can now spend your money

OpenAI gave its agents a wallet: “dots” can now shop and check out for you — and the whole web is being reshaped for buyers who aren't human

ChatGPT Wallet · launched 13 Nov 2026 · built with Visa, Mastercard, American Express · US ChatGPT Pro users, 18+, to start · cards tokenised — neither merchant nor agent sees the real number · every purchase needs a Face ID / fingerprint approval · works with existing checkout pages, no Wallet-specific integration required · OpenAI · TechCrunch · The Verge

What they do
You add a card once; you ask your “dot” (OpenAI's name for a ChatGPT agent) to find something; when it's time to pay, you see the merchant, the items, the total and the card, and approve with a biometric check. The agent completes the checkout on your behalf. A payment token stands in for your real card, so the number never reaches the dot or the store. Alongside it, OpenAI shipped a Commerce Partner Playbook telling merchants how to make their sites work for agent shoppers (§8).
The tech — and what's hard
The payment plumbing is the easy, solved part — tokenisation is how Apple Pay already works. The hard part is authority and trust: how much can an agent buy before it must ask again, who is liable when it buys the wrong thing, how a merchant tells an agent from a bot it wants to block, and how any of it is audited after the fact. That last question — proving what an agent did and under whose authority — is the open problem this board has filed as agent-permissions-audit, now with a credit card attached to it.
Why it works — the real move
The quiet, important detail is “works with existing checkout pages, no integration required.” OpenAI isn't asking the web's permission; it's inserting an agent into the checkout everyone already has. That's why Amazon reacted the same week by building a “world product graph” to index nearly every product sold online — it does not want agents routing purchases through someone else's map of what's for sale. The buying interface is being contested right now, and the contest is over who owns the layer between the shopper's intent and the merchant's checkout.
The bear case
Gated to US Pro users on three card networks on day one — a demo, not yet a market. Trust is unproven: the first well-publicised “my agent bought the wrong $400 thing and nobody will refund it” story could freeze adoption. Merchants may resist being disintermediated by a layer that keeps the customer relationship inside ChatGPT. And Google's competing Universal Commerce Protocol plus Amazon's graph mean this standardises slowly, in public, with three giants pulling against each other.
What it means
This is a platform shift with build-openings on the merchant side — exactly the §A logic of selling where buyers already shop. “Make your Shopify store agent-ready” tooling, agent-purchase reconciliation and dispute-logging for small sellers, and the audit layer for “what did my agent actually buy” are all now real, dated needs rather than speculation. Read the Commerce Partner Playbook (§8) as a map of a market that opened yesterday — it literally lists the four things every merchant must fix, which is a to-do list you could build a tool for.
Your lane, funded · the money is in governing agents, not building models

LangChain raised $300m at a $3bn valuation — built by a solo dev whose 800-line weekend Python library became the layer companies use to watch their AI agents

$300M Series C · valuation $3B (up from $1.25B ~a year ago) · led by CapitalG · new investors GIC, DFJ Growth · existing IVP, Sapphire Ventures · flagship product LangSmith: 7,000+ organisations incl. Nvidia, Bridgewater, LinkedIn, Workday, Harvey, Rippling · CEO Harrison Chase · revenue not disclosed · TechCrunch · announcement · Crunchbase

What they do
LangChain started as an open-source Python library for gluing language models to tools and data. The company now sells the layer above that: LangSmith, which runs, debugs, evaluates and governs AI agents in production, and LangGraph for orchestrating multi-step agent workflows. The pitch, and the tagline, is “own your intelligence” — the same phrase as a YC RFS category. The money is explicitly in observability and governance, not in a model.
The founders
Harrison Chase built LangChain almost solo, publishing it in October 2022 as a small Python project (reportedly ~800 lines) just as ChatGPT broke. Before that he led the ML team at Robust Intelligence and the entity-linking team at Kensho, with a Harvard stats/CS background. The unfair advantage wasn't credentials — it was timing and distribution: he had the default open-source toolkit in every AI developer's import list at the exact moment the field exploded, and converted that mindshare into a paid control plane.
Where the idea came from
Chase was building LLM apps himself and kept rewriting the same plumbing — prompt templates, tool-calling, memory, retrieval. He packaged it, open-sourced it, and the community's adoption was the market research. The commercial insight came later and is the transferable one: once everyone's agents run in production, the valuable product is not the framework that built them but the dashboard that tells you what they did and whether they're behaving.
The bear case
The open-source framework is widely disliked by senior engineers — “LangChain is pointless” was a top HN thread, and the complaints (bloat, breaking changes, leaky abstractions, a past CVSS 9.3 key-exfiltration bug) are real. Revenue is undisclosed, so $3B rests on 7,000 logos of unknown spend. And the existential risk is that the foundation labs — OpenAI, Anthropic, Google — ship their own agent SDKs and observability for free, commoditising exactly the layer LangChain charges for.
What it means
Read this as a signpost, not a company to envy: the market just priced agent governance — the plumbing that watches agents — at $3bn, while the model layer below it is a public-company commodity (yesterday's Anthropic IPO). The buildable openings are all in that observed middle: logging what an agent touched, catching when it misbehaves, proving what it bought (see the Wallet story above). You already run agents daily on a machine holding your keys; instrument your own for a week and you'll have felt the exact problem a $3bn company sells into.
A mental model · the week's most useful reframing · read the shortage skeptically

The report that reframes the whole AI boom: Nvidia allegedly funded the startups that bought its chips — and the man who built that machine just walked out the door

Allegation, reported — label it as such · a Wall Street Journal report says Nvidia invested in and guaranteed financing for AI startups that then bought its GPUs · interim CEO Colette Kress reportedly pushed to reduce these circular deals · a Nvidia director sold ~$1B in stock weeks before the Huang scandal broke · Democrats have opened an investigation · TechPowerUp · Stratechery · Yahoo Finance

What happened
With Jensen Huang out as CEO (covered here yesterday), a second story surfaced: a report alleging that instead of letting surplus GPUs flood the market and push prices down, Nvidia funded and financially backstopped AI startups that couldn't otherwise afford its chips — so they would keep buying. The framing is “circular financing”: Nvidia invests in a customer, the customer spends it on Nvidia hardware, the demand looks organic. Kress, now interim CEO, reportedly argued internally to unwind these deals.
The model — why this is the real item
This is not gossip; it's a lens. If a meaningful slice of GPU “demand” was vendor-financed, then some of the shortage, some of the valuations built on that shortage, and some of the “AI infrastructure is insatiable” narrative were partly manufactured. You don't have to believe the strongest version to use the weaker one: when a dominant supplier also funds its own customers, read its demand figures as a claim, not a fact. It's the same pattern that inflated telecom in 1999 and vendor-financed networking gear before the crash — the canon's Barbarians instinct, applied to the AI build-out.
The bear case (on the story)
It's an allegation in a report, not a filing or an admission — treat the dollar scale as unquantified until someone shows the numbers. Vendor financing is also legal and common; “circular” is an interpretation, and a real business can genuinely need and use the chips it was helped to buy. The honest position is skepticism in both directions: the demand is not all fake, and it is not all real.
What it means
This is the most valuable thing on today's page precisely because it changes how you read everything else on it. The week's giant rounds (§2 — Mirendil $1bn, Mercor $750m at $20bn) look different if some of the demand underneath the whole sector is supplier-engineered. Hold the question for every AI-infra number you see for the next year: who, exactly, is the customer, and who paid for them to be one? And watch §7's canon — the Acquired Nvidia series — to see how the same man built the machine now being taken apart.

The Remake

one evidenced candidate · no idea without a linked complaint

A “make your Shopify store agent-ready” app — because OpenAI just published the exact four-item checklist merchants must fix, and most can't

The incumbent
Nothing, yet — which is the point. Agentic commerce (ChatGPT Wallet, §1) went live this week, and the thing being “remade” is the manual, expensive agency work of getting a store ready for it. The playbook's model #1 (same capability, one narrow audience): take the generic “prepare for AI agents” consulting that big brands buy and package it as a self-serve Shopify app for the small merchant who will never hire a consultant.
The complaint
From the merchant-facing agentic-commerce guides already springing up: stores keep failing agent checkouts for boring, fixable reasons. One practitioner playbook spells out the pain — “audit the catalog for completeness and sellable variants; the checkout for price, tax, shipping and order-state accuracy… reduce unnecessary declines” — and OpenAI's own Commerce Partner Playbook lists the same four audit areas as the gate to working with agents at all. The complaint is latent but dated: “my products don't show up / my checkout gets declined when an agent tries to buy.”
Which remake
#1, same tool, one audience (agency work → self-serve app for small Shopify merchants), carried by #2, better taste — a clean “here's what an agent sees when it tries to buy from you, and here's the one thing to fix” report, where the incumbent alternative is a PDF and a consultant invoice.
The buyer
Shopify merchants — the archetypal “losing money daily, paying to fix revenue leaks” buyer the playbook rates strongest. They already pay $29–$299/mo for Shopify plus a stack of apps; an “agent-readiness” app at $19–$49/mo that measurably recovers agent-driven sales sits inside a store where the card is already out and distribution is solved by the Shopify App Store.
Why him
Distribution is the hard part and the marketplace solves it — this lives inside Shopify, not as a standalone app nobody finds. The timing edge is real and narrow: the standard (OpenAI's Agentic Commerce Protocol, Google's UCP) is days old, so there is no entrenched incumbent and the whole merchant base needs the same fix at once. Taste and a fast, legible audit UI are exactly your edge.
First step
Doable in a day: take one real Shopify store (yours, or a friend's), run its product feed and checkout against OpenAI's published playbook requirements by hand, and write the one-page “here's what an agent can't buy from you and why” report. If fixing it visibly changes the outcome, you have the product's core screen — and the first merchant to show it to. (Kill-filter check: useful on first install (the audit has value with zero other users); buyer = user with proven spend and an urgent, dated reason to care; runs mostly unattended; sits in a marketplace, so distribution isn't the daydream. The honest risk is that the standard is so new it could change under you, or that Shopify ships a first-party “agent-ready” check and eats the category — which is the real one to watch. If Shopify announces it natively, kill this and move on; until then the window is open and empty.)

Skill

day 36

Deploy one thing you've built: take a project off localhost and put it on the internet with a login, in a single afternoon

~3–4 hrs · free tier · produces: a live URL you can send someone, and a felt map of exactly where today's §0-green problem lives

Today's §0-green is “building is trivial, deploying is still stuck in 2015.” The fastest way to understand a problem is to hit it yourself, so: pick one thing you've already built with Claude Code — anything, however small — and get it onto the public internet, behind a login, this afternoon. Use Cloudflare Workers or Val Town (both have real free tiers and get you a URL in minutes), add the simplest auth you can (a shared password, then a real login if you have appetite), and send the link to one person. Keep a running note of every step that was more annoying than it should have been — the DNS, the env vars, the “wait, how do I stop strangers from seeing this,” the moment you realised sharing executable code safely is genuinely hard.

That note is the point. Two things fall out of it. One, you now have a live artifact instead of a localhost demo — which is the difference between “I'm learning to code” and “I ship,” and the thing you can actually put in front of a person. Two, your annoyance list is the product spec for the §0-green problem: every friction you felt is one a few million other AI-assisted builders are feeling this week, and the person who smooths it into one command wins the market YC named. You can't feel that reading about it; you have to deploy the thing and get mad at the right steps.

Free resource: Val Town's engineering blog — they write honestly about the hardest part of this whole problem, sandboxing and running other people's code safely. Read one post after you've deployed your own thing; it'll land far harder once you've felt the wall they're describing.
