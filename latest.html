START THIS MONTHgreen · solo · no capital · no credentials

Nobody can prove what an AI agent actually touched

The problem
Agents run with far more authority than their task needs — the same shell, filesystem and credential access whether they're summarising a file or changing infrastructure. When one goes somewhere it shouldn't, there is no audit trail to answer the only question that matters afterward: what did it touch, and under whose authority? Sandboxing (stopping the agent) shipped. Provenance (recording what it did) did not.
Who says it's hard
It stopped being abstract this week: IEEE Spectrum profiled the Anthropic engineer whose entire job is keeping its coding agent trustworthy — a frontier lab assigning a named human to the gap because tooling doesn't close it yet. And Simon Willison, quoting Addy Osmani, reframed the review problem precisely: “did a human read the code?” is an imprecise proxy for “what could go wrong, and which check is the right one to catch it.” That is the whole unsolved question, said out loud by the people living it.
Why it's still open
Sandboxing shipped (NVIDIA, Cisco); provenance didn't. The research is almost all 2024–26 preprints — roughly three peer-reviewed systems out of twenty-one surveyed. A field that new has no incumbent and no standard, which is exactly when a small, legible tool can define the category.
Who's already trying
NVIDIA (NemoGuard-style sandboxing) and Cisco both wall the agent off; neither keeps the signed, queryable record of what it did and why. The audit layer — “show me every tool call, with the authority it used” — is the open space.
Your angle
Zero capital, zero credentials, and you feel the problem daily: you run Claude Code on a machine holding your keys. You are the user today, for free — and §6 turns this into a one-hour artifact you can start tonight.
Research path
  1. Read arXiv “How Agents Ask for Permission” — the whole landscape in one paper.
  2. Read “Lingering Authority” on the credentials an agent still holds after its task ends — the subtle half of the problem.
  3. Instrument your own setup: log every tool call your agent makes for a week (the §6 skill). The log is the prototype — once you have it, you'll see what a real audit layer has to answer.
REAL WITHIN A YEARamber — the company is red (clearance, capital, ITAR); the on-ramp isn't

The U.S. Army asked founders, by name, to rebuild how it fights — and public markets just priced the thesis

The problem
YC's Fall 2026 RFS carries a category written by the sitting U.S. Secretary of the Army: “we need cutting-edge drones, resilient logistics, and advanced manufacturing.” The old model — a handful of primes, ten-year procurement, exquisite hardware — cannot produce the cheap, attritable, autonomous systems modern conflict actually burns through. The gap between what the Pentagon buys and what it needs is now a funded request with a buyer's name on it.
Who says it's hard
Y Combinator, Requests for Startups — “The Future of American Defense,” authored by Daniel P. Driscoll. And today it got a price: Bengaluru's Tonbo Imaging — thermal sights, missile seekers, counter-drone optics, 28,000+ systems fielded — went 8× oversubscribed at a ~79× P/E. Public money will pay richly for defence hardware that works.
Why it's still open
The hard parts aren't the drone. They're ITAR, clearance, capital intensity, and a procurement system built to reject newcomers — Anduril spent years and billions just proving a startup could sell to the DoD at all. But the autonomy and coordination software moves faster than the hardware and the paperwork, and that's where a small team lives.
Who's already trying
Anduril, Shield AI, Skydio, Neros, Saronic — all post-seed, most capital-heavy. On the hardware side, Tonbo just showed an Indian OEM can go public ITAR-free; DARPA's Vertical Lift Challenger prize is still live, and unclaimed.
Your angle
Amber, not red, for one specific reason: you're US-eligible without sponsorship — the single barrier that blocks most of your peers from this entire market — and you already design and fly a two-rotor VTOL. The company is red. The on-ramp is not: autonomy software, swarm coordination in simulation, and a drone you can already demonstrate. You are one domain from a problem a cabinet secretary put his name on.
Research path
  1. Read “The Future of American Defense” on the YC RFS page — a funded problem statement signed by the buyer.
  2. Reframe your tiltrotor build log as a defence-autonomy demo, not a hobby: what can it sense, carry, or coordinate? Study what Anduril's Lattice and Neros actually ship to see the real bar.
  3. Listen to DARPA's Vertical Lift Challenger recap for why the VTOL prize stayed unclaimed — the unsolved parts are your opening — then apply to one US defence-tech internship or hackathon this cycle. You qualify; most applicants don't.

The distribution study · the clearest “building is free, selling is the moat” story on the wire

A 19-year-old sold a calorie-photo app to MyFitnessPal by renting broke college kids as influencers — and he's already raised for the next one

Cal AI · founded 2024 · co-founders Zach Yadegari (b. 2007, left college) & Henry Langmack · >$30M revenue in two years, ~$40–50M run-rate at exit · acquired by MyFitnessPal (announced Mar 2026, terms undisclosed) · Yadegari's new startup Persona raised $10M (led by Vine Ventures) in Oct 2026 · TechCrunch · TechCrunch (Persona) · Forbes

What they do
Cal AI is a calorie tracker: point your phone at a plate, it estimates the macros. The app is not the story — dozens exist. The story is that two teenagers took it from zero to tens of millions in revenue in under two years and sold it, while better-funded competitors with the same idea went nowhere.
The tech — and why it barely matters
Under the hood it's a vision model doing food recognition on top of a nutrition database — genuinely useful, entirely unremarkable, and buildable by anyone this year. That is the point worth sitting with: the defensible asset wasn't the model. It was the go-to-market machine they built around it. Yadegari describes a “clear formula”: recruit influencers — deliberately the broke college kids who want cash, not equity — pay them tied to user-acquisition targets, favour the ones with “very tight communities,” and scale the ones that convert. One early post brought ~1,000 users in two days; they then “started recruiting influencers as fast as we could.”
Where the idea came from — the unfair access
This is the transferable part, and it's not capital or a lab. Yadegari's first hit was Totally Science, a site that served unblocked browser games to kids stuck behind school web filters. He didn't research that market — he was that market. The same edge carried into Cal AI: a teenager understands teenage attention and the economics of micro-influencers natively, the way a 40-year-old VC-backed founder never will. His unfair advantage was being the distribution channel's native speaker.
The bear case
Influencer-led growth is rented, not owned — the moment you stop paying, acquisition stops, and MyFitnessPal is now discovering what it actually bought. The exit terms are undisclosed, so “sold for millions” could mean a modest acqui-hire dressed as a triumph. And a formula that works in consumer fitness, where one post can convert, does not transfer cleanly to anything with a long or considered sales cycle.
For you
The lesson sits exactly on top of §A and the whole brief: when building is free, the company is the distribution, not the code. Three transferable facts. (1) Pick a market where you are the native speaker — your real edge is the channel you already understand, not the tech. (2) Micro-influencers with tight communities, paid per-acquisition, are a measurable growth engine, not a vanity spend — treat them like a sales channel with a CAC. (3) Serial shipping beats one perfect idea: Totally Science → Cal AI → Persona is the same person pulling the same lever three times. That is a repeatable skill, and it's free to start.
The business-model study · a $29M bet that AI sells as a service, not a seat

An enterprise-AI startup raised $29M not for its model, but for the humans who stand behind the model's output

RapidCanvas · co-founders Rahul Pangam (CEO) & Uttam Phalnikar (CTO) — previously built Simility (AI fraud/risk, acquired by PayPal) · $29M Series B led by Vertex Ventures SEA & India, with Hitachi Ventures; existing Accel, Peak XV, Valley Capital, Titanium · citybiz · YourStory · Tech Startups

What they do
RapidCanvas sells enterprises custom AI applications — but the product is deliberately not just software. It pairs an AI platform with “field AI engineers” who learn each customer's business, build the thing, and — the key word — stay accountable for the outcome after delivery. You're not buying a tool and hoping; you're buying a result someone owns.
The model — and what's actually hard
The hard problem in enterprise AI right now isn't capability, it's the “last mile”: a model that demos beautifully and dies in production because nobody owns the gap between generic AI and a messy, specific business. RapidCanvas's bet is that the market will pay more for certainty than for software — services-as-software, where humans absorb the risk the model can't yet carry, and the platform “compounds with every deployment” so the human cost falls over time. If that compounding is real, it earns a software multiple; if it isn't, it's a consultancy wearing a SaaS costume.
Where the idea came from — the unfair access
Pangam and Phalnikar already did this once: Simility, their AI fraud-detection company, sold to PayPal. That exit is the access — it gave them enterprise credibility, a template for productising hard AI inside Fortune-scale buyers, and the investor relationships (Accel followed them from the last company) that make a $29M Series B routine. Second-time founders aren't raising on an idea; they're raising on a proven ability to finish.
The bear case
“AI platform plus field engineers” is how every consultancy from Accenture down is now repositioning. The whole thesis rests on the platform genuinely compounding — if each deployment still needs near-full human effort, margins stay services-grade (~2× revenue), not software-grade. And “we own the outcome” is a promise that scales badly: the better it sells, the more humans you must hire to keep it true.
For you
The useful idea for a solo builder: the sellable gap right now is accountability, not capability. Everyone can generate a model's worth of output; almost nobody will stand behind it in a specific business. If you build with AI, the premium isn't “I made an app” — it's “I own the result.” That reframes the §0-green audit-trail idea too: being able to prove what an agent did is the infrastructure that makes “I own the outcome” a claim you can actually back.
The deep-tech study · a defence IPO that prices the §0-amber problem in public

India's public markets paid ~79× earnings for a defence-optics maker — and the founders sold, they didn't raise

Tonbo Imaging · founded 2003, Bengaluru · founder/CEO Arvind Lakshmikumar, co-founders Ankit Kumar & Cecilia D'Souza · ₹852 Cr IPO, 7.78× oversubscribed by day 2 · ~79× P/E, 100% offer-for-sale (no fresh capital to the company) · 28,000+ systems fielded as of Jun 30 · Inc42 · Business Standard

What they do
Tonbo designs and manufactures electro-optic and infrared (EO/IR) hardware for defence: thermal imaging cores, weapon sights, thermal binoculars, missile seekers, fire-control and counter-drone targeting. It is a hardware OEM — real systems, in real combat and border use, 28,000+ of them deployed. Not a slide deck; a factory.
The tech — and the ITAR trick
The quiet moat is regulatory, not optical. Because Tonbo designs and builds in India, its EO/IR portfolio sits outside US ITAR export controls — which means it can sell to buyers that American primes legally can't touch. In defence, “who are you allowed to sell to” is as much a moat as “how good is the sensor,” and a sovereign, ITAR-free supply chain is exactly what a fragmenting world is suddenly willing to pay a premium for.
Where the idea came from — and the tell in the share structure
Lakshmikumar built this over 23 years — the opposite of the Cal AI weekend-sprint story, and worth holding in the same issue as its counterweight. But read the structure: the IPO is 100% offer-for-sale. Not one rupee goes into the company; every share sold is an existing holder cashing out. The 8× demand is a real signal that public money wants Indian defence hardware — but the founders and early backers are selling into that demand, not funding new growth with it. Validation and an exit, at the same time.
The bear case
~79× earnings on a hardware company is priced for a decade of flawless execution; defence revenue is lumpy, government-dependent and slow. A 100%-OFS listing means insiders chose this moment to sell — which is information. And a listing this euphoric in a hot sector is as much a read on the market's mood as on the company.
For you
This is the §0-amber problem with a live price tag. Two takeaways. One: the defence-hardware thesis is no longer speculative — public markets just paid up for it, which means every Indian deep-tech and defence founder's raise and exit math just improved. Two: the moat was regulatory positioning (ITAR-free), not raw technology — a reminder that in regulated markets, “who can legally buy from me” can be worth more than being the best sensor. File both against your tiltrotor: the autonomy is your build, but the eligibility is your edge.

The Remake

one evidenced candidate

You don't need a new idea — you need a proven one, rebuilt for someone specific, with better taste, sitting inside a marketplace that already has buyers. One candidate per issue, never one without real users complaining in their own words. Today the complaint isn't one review — it's the single most common reason merchants rage-quit a Shopify app at all.

The incumbent
The whole class of usage-priced Shopify apps — reviews, upsells, email, subscriptions — that bill on order volume rather than a flat fee. A small store's app stack runs $80–$220/mo, and the apps that matter “don't charge a flat fee — they charge on something that grows: your orders, your list, or a slice of the money they move.” The bill quietly doubles as the store succeeds.
The complaint
Not anecdote — data. A Shopify Community analysis of 2,797 one- and two-star app reviews found billing complaints in 18.7% of them, versus 3.6% for setup. The tell: “the median one-star reviewer had already been paying for 90 days… reviews showing up early being happy, and ones months later — after real money has changed hands — being angry ones about billing.” Merchants don't hate the feature. They hate watching the price climb with their own success.
Which remake
#4 — same tool, different price shape (with a dash of #1, one narrow audience). Pick a single high-install category where the leaders meter on orders — product reviews is the cleanest — and ship one beautiful, flat-priced app for small brands: the same core feature, one honest monthly number that never moves no matter how well they sell.
The buyer
Small and growing Shopify merchants already paying $80–$220/mo in app fees and feeling the per-order creep most. The willingness-to-pay is proven by the incumbents' own revenue; you're not creating demand, you're catching the people actively rage-reviewing their way toward a switch. The Shopify App Store puts you in front of them with a card already on file — distribution, solved.
Why him
Two of your edges at once. Taste (#2): review and upsell widgets are visual, on-storefront, and mostly ugly — your filmmaker's eye is a commercial advantage here, not a hobby. And a number (#4): “flat $X, forever, no matter your orders” is a one-line pitch that writes its own ad and is the exact thing the angry reviews are begging for. You compete on the two things you can actually win on.
First step
Today: open the Shopify App Store listing for the top order-metered review app, read its most recent 50 one- and two-star reviews, and paste the billing complaints into one doc — that's your feature list and your ad copy, written by customers. Then build the smallest possible flat-priced review widget (a storefront star-rating block + a simple admin) as a Shopify embedded app. A day gets you the demo and the landing page; the reviews tell you what to charge.
Rejected today
A real, loud r/startups post — “how can I get clients for a freelancing marketplace… freelancers are easy to get but [clients are hard]” — fails two kill-filters at once: it needs network effects to be useful on day one (classic two-sided chicken-and-egg), and the poster is describing the demand problem as their problem. Filed and skipped.

Skill

day 18

Log every single tool call your AI agent makes for one day — and you'll be holding the prototype of today's §0-green from the inside

~1 hour · free · produces: a timestamped record of everything your agent touched, under whose authority — the raw material of an audit layer

Yesterday's skill was shipping a tool at all; today's is watching the agent that builds them. Pick the agent you already run — Claude Code is perfect — and wrap it so that every tool call it makes (file read, file write, shell command, network request) gets appended to a plain log file: timestamp, the tool, the arguments, and which credential or path it used. You don't need anything fancy: a hook, a wrapper script, or even piping its tool-use output to a file. The point is to make the invisible visible for a day of your normal work.

Then read the log. You will be unsettled by how much authority the thing runs with — how often it touched files or keys the task never needed. That discomfort is the §0-green problem, felt instead of described: nobody can currently answer “what did my agent touch, and why” without doing exactly this by hand. You'll finish the hour with a real artifact (the log) and a real product question (what would an audit layer have to answer that this log can't?). Build the log first; feel the gap second. The gap is the product.

Free resources: “How Agents Ask for Permission” (the landscape), “Lingering Authority” (the credentials an agent keeps after the task), and your own agent's hook/tool-use documentation. One agent, one day of logging — then look at what you can't yet answer.
