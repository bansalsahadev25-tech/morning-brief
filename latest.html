START THIS MONTHgreen · solo · no capital · no credentials · you are the user

Deploying personal software is still hard, and agents just made it urgent

The problem
Agents made it trivial to build software that will only ever have one or a handful of users — a tool for your team, a tracker for your own workflow. Today TechCrunch described OpenAI turning its coding tools into “dots,” an always-on personal assistant that builds and runs little programs for you on command. The building is solved. Deploying and sharing the result — with a URL, a login, and someone else's data kept safe — is as hard as it was in 2015.
Who says it's hard
Y Combinator, Requests for Startups — “A Cloud for Small Software.” AWS and Azure were built for software that scales to millions, and that complexity is now the bottleneck for software that will never need to.
Why it's still open
The hard parts are not the hosting. They are auth and permissions for arbitrary user-written code, per-person environment customisation, and letting non-technical people share executable code without it becoming a security incident. Everyone underestimates the third — and with “dots”-style agents writing the code, the volume of ship-it-somewhere software is about to spike.
Who's already trying
Vercel, Replit, Val Town, Cloudflare Workers, Deno Deploy. All good; none has made “one command, auth included, share with a human” feel trivial for the person who just vibe-coded a thing at midnight.
Your angle
You already live in this problem: you build with Claude Code and then have nowhere good to put the result. That is the founder's unfair advantage in its cheapest form — you are the user. §6 turns it into a two-hour exercise that ships one of your own projects to a real URL.
Research path
  1. Build the ugliest possible version for yourself first: one command that takes a Claude Code project and hands you a URL with a login.
  2. Read Val Town's engineering writeups on sandboxing user-written code — they have hit every wall you will hit.
  3. Ask ten people in your hostel who build things where their projects currently live. The answer is “localhost,” and that is the market.

The software running physical work has not changed in 20 years

The problem
80% of the global workforce does not sit at a desk. Construction, maintenance and fleet operations still run on software that dispatches people, tracks them, manages assets and bills the customer — and those industries spend 10–100x more on labour than on software. The moment labour gets scarce or automated, whoever owns the coordination layer owns the record of how the work actually happens.
Who says it's hard
Y Combinator RFS — “New Operating Systems for the Physical World.” And today the money agreed, loudly: Bedrock Robotics raised $600M at $4.1B to automate excavators precisely because skilled operators are retiring (§1), and a Databricks co-founder told TechCrunch the way you win these buyers is to hitch a ride on a giant's sales team, not out-tech them.
Why it's still open
Incumbents charge for human coordination. Nobody has built the layer that schedules agents, robots and humans together — and whoever does captures end-to-end records of how real-world work happens, which no frontier lab or robotics startup will have.
Who's already trying
ServiceTitan, Samsara, Procore — all built for the old, all-human model. Bedrock is attacking one slice (the machine) from the hardware side; the scheduling-and-billing OS above it is still open.
Your angle
This is the category your cab-booking project sits in. Dispatch, matching, fleet, real-world supply — you are already inside the problem, with users walking past you, which is the part nobody can buy. Amber, not green: it needs real operators using it, not a weekend demo.
Research path
  1. Re-read your own PLAN.md with this framing: you are not building a booking form, you are building dispatch — the physical-world OS in miniature.
  2. Talk to the middleman you already know about what he cannot see today: idle vehicles, no-shows, driver reliability. That blindness is the product.
  3. Read Samsara's investor filings for what fleet operators actually pay for, in their own words — then watch how Bedrock prices autonomy into the same budget.

AI economics · the first real look inside a frontier lab's books

Anthropic filed to go public — and the S-1 is a masterclass in enterprise lock-in

$16.3B H1 2026 revenue (from $1.2B a year earlier) · ~900% net revenue retention · 11,900+ customers at $100k+ · SEC S-1 · TechCrunch

What they do
Anthropic makes Claude — the model behind your Claude Code — and sells it three ways: raw API (the Platform), a developer tool (Claude Code), and apps (Cowork). The S-1 is the first time the numbers are public, and they are steep: $16.3 billion of revenue in the first half of 2026, up from $1.2 billion in the same period of 2025.
The mechanism
The filing all but names the strategy: land on one layer, expand across all three. More than half of $100k+ customers now use both the Platform and a first-party app, up from ~30% at the end of 2025. Anthropic's own words: an advance at any layer “compounds across all three.” The tell is the retention number — net revenue retention ~900%, meaning the average cohort spends roughly nine times more a year later. The highest-spending enterprises that adopt Claude Code spend ~20x what comparable customers without it do.
The founders
Siblings Dario and Daniela Amodei, who left OpenAI in 2021 — Dario had been VP of Research — over a disagreement about safety and direction. Their unfair advantage was never a secret technique; it was leaving with a team that had already built GPT-3 and knew exactly what the next three years required.
The bear case
The coverage I pulled reports “mounting losses” without a figure, and that is the whole risk: 900% retention is spectacular while revenue compounds, but the losses that buy it are enormous, and the S-1 flags customer-concentration (~250 customers spend $10M+) and dependence on cloud partners for compute. A retention number that good can also be a bubble number — it holds only while enterprises keep expanding AI budgets.
What it means
Read the S-1 itself (§8) — it is the best free business-school case study you will get this year, and it teaches one transferable move you can use at any scale: land small, then expand across layers so leaving costs more than staying. That is the same logic under your §0-green (ship the tool, then own the deploy + auth + data the user can't easily move). And note what durable revenue looks like up close, because §2's SpaceX line and §7's canon make the same argument from the opposite direction.
Physical-world autonomy · your §0-amber, funded in the open

Bedrock raised $600M to teach excavators to drive themselves — because the operators are retiring

$600M Series C · $4.1B valuation · ~$1B raised since 2024 · led by CapitalG + Emergence, NVIDIA in · San Francisco · TechCrunch

What they do
Bedrock Robotics builds a retrofit self-driving kit that bolts onto existing construction machines — cameras, lidar and compute — starting with excavators rather than a purpose-built robot. The pitch is not “robot dig”; it is “keep the $500k machine you already own and make it run a night shift without an operator.”
The mechanism
The thesis is demographic, not technological: it takes five years or more to master operating an excavator, and industry estimates say over 40% of construction workers retire within five years. The work is dangerous, repetitive and structurally short of skilled hands — which is the exact profile autonomy attacks first. They are live on three commercial sites today: a Nevada water-treatment build, a Texas earthwork job with Champion Site Prep, and a 1.2-million-cubic-yard Zachry Construction civil project.
The founders
CEO Boris Sofman led Waymo's self-driving-truck program and co-founded Anki Robotics; CTO Kevin Peterson and much of the team are Waymo veterans. The unfair advantage is transferred, not invented: they spent years learning why autonomous trucking is brutally hard (open roads, infinite edge cases) and picked a domain that is the opposite — a fenced worksite, low speeds, a bounded task.
The bear case
Three commercial sites is a pilot, not a business, and construction is famously unforgiving of downtime — one autonomous machine hitting a buried line undoes a hundred clean digs. Waymo pedigree raised the money; it has not yet proven that excavation generalises across soil, weather and site chaos the way a demo suggests. And at $4.1B the valuation prices in a scale they are years from.
What it means
This is your §0-amber made concrete, and the transferable lesson is the founders' domain choice: they took hard-won autonomy expertise and aimed it at the easiest-to-automate corner of a labour-starved industry, not the most glamorous. Your tiltrotor is the same kind of asset — don't ask “what's the coolest thing it can do,” ask “what bounded, dangerous, repetitive task is someone already paying a human to do badly?” That is where autonomy money is flowing, and NVIDIA writing the check tells you the compute layer believes it.
Frontier AI · capability, and a fight about what counts as knowledge

OpenAI says its model proved three theorems — and the mathematicians won't take the credit it skipped

3 claimed results in math, CS, physics · ETH disproof found 16 Oct, formalised in Lean next day · none peer-reviewed · OpenAI · TNW · scope check

What happened
OpenAI announced three results its models produced: a harmonic-analysis proof (the local-smoothing / Kakeya conjecture in all dimensions), an existence proof touching quantum chromodynamics and Yang–Mills, and the headline — that the Exponential Time Hypothesis is false, a 27-year-old assumption about how hard certain problems are. OpenAI says it found the ETH disproof in natural language on 16 October and formalised it in Lean the next day.
The honest scope
None of the three is independently verified. As a careful scope check lays out: the ETH result is being examined by Ryan Williams and the hypothesis's original authors and was reportedly Lean-checked; the Kakeya proof is posted to GitHub for review; the Yang–Mills proof hasn't even been released publicly. Crucially, this is not P vs NP — “P still probably doesn't equal NP, and standard encryption is not affected.” Results like these are fragile, with a fuzzy line between proved and assumed.
The fight
The more useful story is the backlash. Terence Tao — the most respected living mathematician — now says problem-solving that arrives without explanation, citation, or people who will answer questions “will not be part of our literature.” Scott Aaronson disputes the remedy but not the harm: once a problem is public, anyone — or anything — may solve it. The discipline is openly arguing over whether an answer with no reasoning you can interrogate is knowledge at all.
The bear case
“AI proved a theorem” and “AI produced a document a human later verified” are different claims, and the gap is where the hype lives. A Lean check proves the logic is sound; it says nothing about whether the result matters or was stated correctly. Until Williams and the Yang–Mills referees report, treat all three as claims, not facts — which is exactly OpenAI's own wording, buried under the headline.
What it means
The transferable model isn't the math — it's Tao's objection. As AI gets better at producing answers, the scarce and trusted thing becomes legible reasoning: the explanation, the citation, the person who stands behind it. That is a product insight for your §0-green (an audit nobody can read is worthless) and a career one: in a world of cheap answers, being the person who can show their work is the moat. File the ETH result under “watch the crypto angle” — it is not a break today, but it is the direction the board's crypto-agility problem warned about.

The Remake

one evidenced candidate · never more
Same tool, different price shape — charge for what you did, not what you didn't

The Shopify upsell app that only bills for the sales it actually caused

The incumbent
ReConvert and its lookalikes (Selleasy, AfterSell) on the Shopify App Store — the dominant post-purchase upsell apps, thousands of installs each. They raise average order value, and they price on a usage tier that counts your store's total orders.
The complaint
Real, dated 1-star reviews on ReConvert's own listing all circle the same wound — you pay for orders the app never touched. TEKVIYON (Oct 21, 2026): “they charge you extra if you make a lot of orders even though they have not done anything for these orders. I have made 1200 sales this month — this app only generated 40. Yet they charged me $150.” A second theme repeats in the same filter — Stepure (Oct 25, 2026): “they started charging me a monthly fee… even after I deleted the app.” The pricing feels like a tax on growth the merchant didn't agree to.
Which remake
Same tool, different price shape. Don't out-feature ReConvert. Ship a post-purchase upsell widget with one promise it structurally cannot match: you are billed only on the incremental revenue the app actually generated — attributed, shown transparently, flat or pure rev-share, nothing charged on orders it never saw. The entire pitch is the exact axis of the complaint above.
The buyer
Mid-volume Shopify merchants — apparel, food, beauty — doing hundreds to low-thousands of orders a month, who today pay ReConvert roughly $5–50+/mo on a tier that scales with all their orders. For a store like TEKVIYON's (1,200 orders, 40 from the app), a plan that charges a slice of that incremental AOV instead of a growth-tax is an obvious switch — and the buyer is the user.
Why him
This is a taste-and-trust play, which is your edge twice: a cleaner, more beautiful upsell flow (your filmmaker's eye on a UI merchants stare at all day), and a pricing page a merchant doesn't resent — which is a design decision competitors treat as a billing afterthought. It lives inside Shopify, so distribution (the hard part) is half-solved, and the complaint thread is a pre-written list of the people who would switch.
First step
Today: install ReConvert on a Shopify dev store, build one post-purchase offer, and screenshot its pricing tier beside TEKVIYON's review. Then write the one sentence that is your whole landing page — “you only pay when we actually make you money” — and mock the billing screen that proves it (orders influenced vs. orders charged). If that screen is clear enough to make a merchant angry at their current bill, you have the product. (Kill-filter check: the tempting version — “a full ReConvert competitor” — fails: it needs feature parity on day one on the incumbent's turf. The price-shape play passes all five: useful day one, buyer = user with budget, runs unattended, not ad-supported consumer, and two payers are named in the complaint thread above.)

Skill

day 26

Deploy one of your Claude Code projects to a real URL with a login — today, in two hours

~2 hours · free · produces: one of your own tools live on the internet, behind auth — and first-hand knowledge of exactly where §0-green's pain is

Today's §0-green is “agents make building trivial, deploying is still hard.” The only way to understand that problem is to feel it. Take one thing you built with Claude Code that currently runs on localhost — a tracker, a tool, anything with a bit of state — and get it onto a real URL that a friend can open, behind a login, before lunch. Pick one platform and commit: Val Town if it's a small script, Cloudflare Workers or Vercel if it's a web app. Don't research the perfect choice — the point is to hit the walls, not avoid them.

Keep a running note of every place you get stuck: where auth got confusing, where you nearly leaked a secret, where the docs assumed you already knew the thing. That note is the product. Every friction you hit is one a thousand other people who vibe-coded something last night will hit too — and the §0-green opportunity is to make those frictions disappear. You now know, in your hands rather than in theory, which part of “ship my small software” is actually hard. Do it on a real project, not a toy, so the pain is honest.

Free resources: Cloudflare Workers docs (the generous free tier and the clearest mental model of deploy-a-function), and Val Town's blog on sandboxing user code — the hard parts of §0-green, written down by people living in them.

Deals

1 free resource worth your time

Read Anthropic's S-1 on SEC EDGAR — the best free business-school case study of the year

free · primary-source filing · sec.gov

Today's §1 turns on numbers from one document, and the document is free, public, and yours to read in full. An S-1 is the most honest thing a company ever writes: the law forces it to state its revenue, its losses, its risks, and exactly how it makes money, in plain language, because lying is a crime. Anthropic's is a rare chance to see a frontier AI business dissected — how the Platform, Claude Code and apps feed each other, why net revenue retention hits ~900%, and which risks the company is most afraid of (read the “Risk Factors” section first; it's where the real story hides). Spend an hour with the summary and the MD&A and you'll read every future funding headline differently — and you'll know what a good S-1 looks like long before you ever have to write one. EDGAR has every company's filings the same way, for nothing.
